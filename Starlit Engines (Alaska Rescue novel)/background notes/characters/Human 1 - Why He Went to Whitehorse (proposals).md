# Human #1 — Why He Went to Whitehorse

2026-09-22. **Proposals, not decisions.** Written because the question "why did he go?" was open, and because
the worldbuilding done this month appears to have answered it almost by accident.

---

## What Is Already Established

From `huMan-1.md`, written well before any of the Alaska Republic worldbuilding existed:

> **Inciting Event:** Travels from Vancouver, BC, Cascadia to Whitehorse, Alaska **to work out some sort of
> deal or agreement (specifics TBD)** — meets Lucy Grayson at the railyard there.
>
> **Problem:** After leaving Whitehorse to return home, ends up **way off-course and becomes lost** — with Lucy
> by his side. Eventually **goes radio-silent**, worrying Human #2.
>
> **Unique quirk:** Charming type.

Four facts, and every one of them now points the same direction.

---

## The Convergence

**He is a Cascadian who traveled to the Alaska Republic's capital to negotiate an agreement.** That sentence
was written before Whitehorse was settled as the capital, before the map reference established the highway
network, and before the transit problem was identified. It now reads as though it were written afterward.

What the worldbuilding established (see
`worldbuilding/nations/Alaska Republic and Cascadia - The Transit Problem (three resolutions).md`):

- The Alaska Republic **cannot reach its own eastern territory or any deep-water port without crossing
  Cascadia.** Whitehorse → Yellowknife is ≈1,919 km, of which ≈650 km is Cascadian.
- **45% of the Alaska Highway is in Cascadia.** Anchorage and Fairbanks reach the continent only through it.
- The Republic's only tidewater access — Skagway, Haines, Prince Rupert, Vancouver — is entirely Cascadian.

**So the single most consequential ongoing negotiation between these two countries is about transit.** And a
charming Cascadian sent to the Alaskan capital to work out an agreement is, on the face of it, working on
exactly that.

**And then there is the railyard.** He meets Lucy at the railyard in Whitehorse — and the railway out of
Whitehorse is the **White Pass and Yukon Route to Skagway**, which the partition has turned into an
**international line.** He is not at the railyard by coincidence. The railyard is the Cascadian border
question, made physical, sitting in the Alaskan capital.

## Candidate Deals

Ranked by how tightly they fit what is already written.

### 1. Rail — the White Pass and Yukon Route *(strongest fit)*

He is in Whitehorse about the railway: haulage rights, rolling stock, restored service, tariffs, or the terms
under which Alaskan freight moves down a line that now crosses a frontier to reach a Cascadian port.

**Why it fits best:** it is the only candidate that explains *the railyard* without any additional invention.
It puts him precisely where the notes already put him, talking to precisely the people he would be talking to,
about the one piece of infrastructure that is simultaneously the Republic's lifeline and Cascadia's leverage.

**What it gives Lucy:** a railyard negotiation means locomotives, rolling stock, and maintenance capacity are
on the table — which means an expert engine mechanic is not a bystander but an asset. See the Doorway #1
interlock below.

### 2. Road transit and the corridor

He is negotiating the road agreement itself — transit rights on the Watson Lake–Fort Nelson–Fort Liard
corridor, maintenance cost-sharing, customs arrangements. The real-world model is the **Shakwak Project**, in
which one country funds road maintenance inside another's borders because it depends on the route.

**Why it fits:** it is the most consequential version, and it makes his return journey meaningful (below).
**Why it fits slightly less well than #1:** it does not explain the railyard by itself.

### 3. Rolling stock, parts, or repair capacity *(the commercial version)*

Not a treaty at all — a purchase. He came to buy locomotives or parts, or to contract repair work, on behalf
of a Cascadian firm. The "deal or agreement" is commercial rather than diplomatic.

**Why it fits:** it is the most plausible thing for a private individual to be doing, and it explains the
railyard perfectly. **Why it may be too small:** it makes him a salesman rather than someone whose
disappearance matters beyond his friend.

### 4. Resource offtake

Cascadia needs something the Republic has — coal from Healy, zinc, refined product — and he is arranging
supply. **Why it fits less well:** no railyard connection unless the cargo moves by rail, and no reason for the
capital specifically.

### 5. Something about dolls

A transit agreement necessarily includes rules about **property in transit** — and in this era, before the
Jeju-do ruling, that category includes Lucy and Jennifer. He could be there about the legal status of robots
crossing the border.

**Why it is interesting:** it makes the book's central injustice the literal subject of its inciting event.
**Why to be careful:** it risks being too neat. It may work better as a *clause* in one of the deals above —
something he barely notices negotiating, which the reader notices instead.

## The Strongest Single Answer

**He is a Cascadian commercial or governmental agent, sent to Whitehorse on the rail-and-transit question —
and he is traveling the overland route home on purpose, to assess it.**

That last clause is the part that earns its place, because it solves a problem the notes leave open.

### It explains why he gets lost

"Ends up way off-course" is difficult to justify for a man simply driving home on a highway. **It is easy to
justify for a man who is not simply driving home.** If part of his job is to evaluate the corridor — its
condition, its passability, what it would cost to depend on it — then he is deliberately traveling roads he
does not know, at the edge of what is maintained, in country where the maintained road and the unmaintained
one look identical for the first hundred kilometers.

A man following a highway does not get lost. **A man surveying a route does.**

### It explains why Lucy is with him

A route assessment needs a mechanic. Not as a favor, not as a passenger — as the person who keeps the vehicle
running in country where a breakdown is the whole danger. **He has a professional reason to want her
specifically**, which is the exact shape her arc requires.

### It explains the radio silence

He is off the maintained corridor, in terrain that blocks line-of-sight, with a schedule nobody can check him
against because the whole point of the trip is that the route is unsurveyed. Silence is not immediately alarm —
until it lasts.

### It explains how Human #2 can follow

Human #2 knows roughly where he was going, because it was a *job* with a route. Not "somewhere in the north" —
a corridor, with a start and an end. That is followable. A man who vanished at random is not.

## How This Interlocks With Lucy's Doorway #1

Her constraints file (`../../structure/character arcs/Lucy Grayson - Doorway and Midpoint Constraints.md`)
sets a hard requirement:

> **The offer must be structured so that accepting it does not feel like accepting help.** The only door a
> Social Five with an SP Three undercurrent walks through voluntarily is one framed as *her providing value* —
> a job, a commission, a problem that needs her specifically.

A route survey is that offer, exactly. He is not rescuing her from the railyard. **He needs a mechanic who can
fix anything, for a journey through country where that skill is the difference between arriving and not** —
and she is demonstrably the best one in Whitehorse. She is not being helped. She is being *hired*, by someone
who came a thousand miles and needs what she has.

It also satisfies the constraint that **the pull must be recognition, not escape.** Someone wanting her work,
by name, for a stated reason, is the Social Five's threshold. And it satisfies the requirement that the
situation be *tolerable rather than abusive* — she is not fleeing the railyard, she is taking a better job.

And the framework's own note applies cleanly: the Maiden Arc's **Predator and Protector need not be different
characters.** A man who arrives with an apparent way out, who is charming, and whose proposal is genuinely
good — and who then leads her into country he does not know — is both at once, without anyone having to be a
villain.

## What This Does to the Book's Geography

The map reference establishes that the Cassiar Highway junction sits on the Alaska Highway **west of Watson
Lake**, and that the Cassiar runs south through Dease Lake and Iskut toward Kitwanga.

If Human #1 and Lucy leave Whitehorse southbound and go off-course somewhere in that corridor, then Human #2
and Jennifer — coming north from Vancouver — are traveling **the same road from the other end.** The four
converge in northern Cascadia, which is exactly what the premise already requires, and then continue to Prince
Rupert for the vessel.

**That means the route is not a backdrop. It is the plot.** The reason the group forms at all is that two
pairs entered the same corridor from opposite ends, and only one pair knew where they were going.

## Open Questions

1. **Is he government or commercial?** A treaty negotiator and a company's agent are different men with
   different stakes. Charm suits both; only one of them can be disowned by his employer.
2. **Does he know the route is dangerous?** If yes, taking Lucy is a calculated risk and he is partly
   culpable. If no, he is out of his depth from the first page. Both are playable; they are different books.
3. **Does the deal succeed?** A man going home from a successful negotiation and a man going home from a failed
   one behave differently, and only one of them has a reason to take the long way and look at the road.
4. **What, specifically, was agreed?** Left open here deliberately — it constrains the wider Alaska/Cascadia
   relationship, which is itself unsettled (see the three resolutions file).
5. **Names.** Both humans remain TBN.
