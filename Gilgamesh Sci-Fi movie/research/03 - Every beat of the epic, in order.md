# Every beat of the Epic of Gilgamesh, in order

Research compilation, 2026-10-25. **What the original epic contains, beat by beat; nothing here is adaptation or decision.** Source: Andrew George, trans., *The Epic of Gilgamesh*, 2nd ed. (Penguin Classics, 2020), the standard Babylonian version, Tablets I-XI, with Tablet XII as an appendix.

**Totals:** 151 scenes and 867 beats in Tablets I-XI; 39 beats in the Tablet XII appendix.

## How to read this
- **Beat numbers** (B1, B2, ...) run through the whole epic. **Scene numbers** (I.1, I.2, ...) and line references match the per-tablet files in `01 - Original epic play-by-play (George 2020)/`. Each of those files holds the scene's full record: places, people, specifics, gaps, quotations and comparisons with the older versions.
- **Markers carried over from the tablet files:**
  - **"about"** before a line number: the line falls between George's printed margin numbers and was counted.
  - **"restored" or "conjectural"**: the words are George's reconstruction of broken text.
  - **⚠️**: uncertain.
  - **P, Y, Si, Hittite, etc.** after a beat: the standard version's own lines are lost there, and George fills the gap from an older tablet.
- **The beats are paraphrase.** Quotations are short and cited.
- **Not covered here: episodes found only in older versions.** They are listed at the end, with pointers.

## Tablet I. The Coming of Enkidu

### I.1 Prologue: "He who saw the Deep" (I 1–about 28; pp. 1–2)
- **B1.** Narrator: praises the man who saw the Deep, knew the right ways and was wise in everything. The couplet is then said again with his name, Gilgamesh (I 1–4).
- **B2.** Narrator: he explored all the seats of power and knew all wisdom; he saw secret and hidden things; he brought back a story from before the Flood (I 5–about 8).
- **B3.** Narrator: he traveled far, grew weary and found peace; all his labors were written on a stone tablet (about I 9–10).
- **B4.** Narrator: he built the rampart of Uruk and of the temple Eanna (about I 11–12).
- **B5.** Narrator → listener (commands): look at the wall and the unmatched parapet; climb the ancient stairway; approach Eanna, Ishtar's seat, which no later king could equal (about I 13–17).
- **B6.** Narrator → listener: go up on the wall and walk along it; inspect the foundations and the brickwork. He asks whether the bricks were not kiln-fired and whether the Seven Sages did not lay the foundations (about I 18–21).
- **B7.** Narrator: gives Uruk's area in four parts (about I 22–23).
- **B8.** Narrator → listener: find the cedar tablet-box, open its bronze clasps and its lid, take out the lapis lazuli tablet, and read aloud everything Gilgamesh suffered (about I 24–28).

### I.2 Paean: "Surpassing all other kings" (about I 29–62; pp. 2–3)
- **B9.** Narrator: Gilgamesh is greater than all other kings, heroic in build, a raging wild bull (about I 29–30).
- **B10.** Narrator: he leads from the front and can be trusted at the rear; he shields his men like a riverbank and smashes walls like a flood-wave (about I 31–34).
- **B11.** Narrator: his parentage: the wild bull of Lugalbanda, nursed by the Wild Cow Ninsun (I 35–about 36).
- **B12.** Narrator: his deeds: he opened mountain passes, dug wells in the uplands, crossed the ocean to the sunrise, searched the world for life, forced his way to Uta-napishti, restored the cult centers the Flood destroyed, and set up rites for the people (about I 37–44).
- **B13.** Narrator: no one can rival his kingship or claim it as he does (I 45–about 46).
- **B14.** Narrator: he bore the name Gilgamesh from birth and is two-thirds god, one-third human (about I 47–48).
- **B15.** Narrator: the Lady of the Gods designed his figure, and Nudimmud perfected his build (about I 49–50).
- **B16.** Narrator: gives his size and looks (about I 51–62). He is eleven cubits tall, his chest four cubits wide, his foot a triple cubit, his leg half a rod and his stride six cubits; the curls at his cheeks are three cubits long (uncertain). His beard is dark as lapis lazuli and his hair as thick as barley. Several of these words are restored.

### I.3 Gilgamesh's tyranny in Uruk; the people's complaint (about I 63–78; pp. 3–4)
- **B17.** Narrator: Gilgamesh struts about Uruk like a dominant wild bull, head held high (about I 63–64).
- **B18.** Narrator: no one equals him with weapons, and his *contests* keep his companions constantly on their feet (I 65–about 66).
- **B19.** Narrator: he harasses Uruk's young men without cause and lets no son go to his father (about I 67–68).
- **B20.** Narrator: his oppression grows worse day and night, though he is supposed to guide and shepherd the people (about I 69–71).
- **B21.** Narrator: he lets no daughter go to her mother (about I 72).
- **B22.** Narrator: he is their herdsman and they are his cows; their complaint rises to the far heaven (about I 73–74).
- **B23.** The people (a two-line quoted complaint): the mighty Gilgamesh lets no girl go to her bridegroom (I 75–about 76).
- **B24.** Narrator: Ishtar hears the complaint of the warriors' daughters and the young men's brides (about I 77–78).

### I.4 The gods of heaven appeal to Anu (about I 79–93; p. 4)
- **B25.** Narrator: the gods of heaven call out to Anu (about I 79–80).
- **B26.** Gods → Anu: you have raised a brutal wild bull in Uruk. They list his deeds: no one equals him with weapons, his contests wear out his companions, he harasses the young men, he keeps sons from fathers, and his tyranny grows worse day and night (about I 81–86).
- **B27.** Gods → Anu: yet he is the people's shepherd, guide and herdsman, and he keeps girls from their bridegrooms (about I 87–91).
- **B28.** Narrator: Anu listens to the complaint of the daughters and brides (about I 92–93).

### I.5 Aruru creates Enkidu in the wild (about I 94–112; pp. 4–5)
- **B29.** Narrator: they summon Aruru (about I 94).
- **B30.** They → Aruru: you created mankind, so now create what Anu has in mind (I 95–about 96).
- **B31.** They → Aruru: make him a match for the storm in Gilgamesh's heart, so the two can compete and Uruk can rest (about I 97–98).
- **B32.** Narrator: Aruru hears and forms Anu's idea within herself (about I 99–100).
- **B33.** Narrator: she washes her hands, pinches off clay and throws it down in the wild (about I 101–102).
- **B34.** Narrator: in the wild she creates Enkidu, whom Ninurta makes strong (about I 103–104).
- **B35.** Narrator: his whole body is covered in hair, which is long like a woman's and thick as barley; he knows no people and no land (I 105–about 108).
- **B36.** Narrator: hairy like the god of the animals, he grazes with the gazelles, joins the herd at the water-hole and enjoys the water with the animals (about I 109–112).

### I.6 The hunter comes upon Enkidu at the water-hole (about I 113–121; p. 5)
- **B37.** Narrator: a hunter meets Enkidu at the water-hole (about I 113–114).
- **B38.** Narrator: this happens on a first, a second and a third day (I 115–about 116).
- **B39.** Narrator: the hunter's face freezes at the sight; Enkidu goes back to his lair with his herds (about I 117–118).
- **B40.** Narrator: the hunter is troubled, silent and gloomy, with sorrow in his heart and the look of a man who has traveled far (about I 119–121).

### I.7 The hunter tells his father; the father's advice (about I 122–145; pp. 5–6)
- **B41.** Hunter → father: a man has come to the water-hole, the strongest in the land, with strength like a rock fallen from the sky (about I 122–125).
- **B42.** Hunter → father: the man roams the hills all day and grazes with the herd, and his tracks are always at the water-hole; the hunter is too afraid to go near him (about I 126–129).
- **B43.** Hunter → father: the man fills in the hunter's pits, tears up his snares and frees the animals, so the hunter cannot work (I 130–about 133).
- **B44.** Father → hunter: go to Gilgamesh in Uruk and stand before him, whose strength is also like a rock from the sky (about I 134–137).
- **B45.** Father → hunter: set out for Uruk; human strength alone will not do this (about I 138–139).
- **B46.** Father → hunter: bring Shamhat the harlot, whose allure can overcome even the mighty (I 140–about 141).
- **B47.** Father → hunter (the plan): when the herd comes to drink, she should undress; the man will see her and come to her, and then his herd will reject him (about I 142–145).

### I.8 The hunter goes to Uruk and reports to Gilgamesh (about I 146–166; pp. 6–7)
- **B48.** Narrator: the hunter follows his father's advice and sets out for Uruk (about I 146–148).
- **B49.** Narrator: he speaks before the king (about I 149).
- **B50.** Hunter → Gilgamesh: he repeats his whole report word for word (I 150–160).
- **B51.** Gilgamesh → hunter: go, and take Shamhat the harlot with you (about I 161–162).
- **B52.** Gilgamesh → hunter: he repeats the father's plan word for word (about I 163–166).

### I.9 Hunter and Shamhat travel to the water-hole and wait (about I 167–179; p. 7)
- **B53.** Narrator: the hunter sets out with Shamhat (about I 167–168).
- **B54.** Narrator: they reach the place on the third day and sit down to wait (about I 169–170).
- **B55.** Narrator: after two days of waiting, the herd comes to drink (about I 171–172).
- **B56.** Narrator: the animals arrive, and so does Enkidu (about I 173–174).
- **B57.** Narrator: Enkidu grazes with the gazelles and enjoys the water with the herd (I 175–about 177).
- **B58.** Narrator: Shamhat sees him (about I 178–179).

### I.10 Shamhat seduces Enkidu: six days and seven nights (I 180–about 194; pp. 7–8)
- **B59.** Speaker (the hunter, by context) → Shamhat: this is the man; uncover yourself so he can see you (I 180–about 181).
- **B60.** Speaker → Shamhat: do not pull back; breathe in his scent; he will see you and come to you (about I 182–183).
- **B61.** Speaker → Shamhat: spread your clothes for him to lie on and give him a woman's lovemaking; once he has had his fill, his herd will reject him (about I 184–187).
- **B62.** Narrator: Shamhat undresses and he looks at her (about I 188–189).
- **B63.** Narrator: she does not pull back; she spreads her clothes and he lies with her (I 190–about 191).
- **B64.** Narrator: she makes love to him, and he embraces her (about I 192–193).
- **B65.** Narrator: Enkidu couples with Shamhat for six days and seven nights (about I 194).

### I.11 The herd flees from Enkidu; he gains reason (I 195–about 204; p. 8)
- **B66.** Narrator: sated, Enkidu looks toward his herd (I 195–about 196).
- **B67.** Narrator: the gazelles run off and the other animals shy away from him (about I 197–198).
- **B68.** Narrator: he has made his pure body unclean; his legs stand still while his herd moves away (about I 199–200).
- **B69.** Narrator: he is weaker and cannot run as before, but now he has reason and understanding (about I 201–202).
- **B70.** Narrator: he goes back and sits at Shamhat's feet, studying her face (about I 203–204).

### I.12 Shamhat invites Enkidu to Uruk; Enkidu vows to challenge Gilgamesh (I 205–about 223; p. 8)
- **B71.** Narrator: Enkidu now listens to what she says (I 205–about 206).
- **B72.** Shamhat → Enkidu: you are handsome, like a god; why roam the wild with animals? (about I 207–208).
- **B73.** Shamhat → Enkidu: let me lead you to Uruk and the sacred temple of Anu and Ishtar, where Gilgamesh lords it over the men like a wild bull (about I 209–212).
- **B74.** Narrator: her words please him; by instinct he knows he should look for a friend (about I 213–214).
- **B75.** Enkidu → Shamhat: take me there, to the temple and to Gilgamesh, the wild bull among men (I 215–about 219).
- **B76.** Enkidu → Shamhat: I will challenge him, since I am strong; I will boast in Uruk that I am the mightiest and change the way things are run, because the one born in the wild is strong (I 220–about 223).

### I.13 Shamhat describes Uruk and Gilgamesh (about I 224–244; p. 9)
- **B77.** Shamhat → Enkidu: let the people see you; a broken line follows (about I 224–225).
- **B78.** Shamhat → Enkidu: go to Uruk, where the young men wear waistbands; there is a festival every day, with drums (about I 226–229).
- **B79.** Shamhat → Enkidu: there are beautiful, charming harlots there, who can rouse even old men from bed (I 230–about 232).
- **B80.** Shamhat → Enkidu: you know nothing of life yet; I will show you Gilgamesh, a joyful man; look at his face (about I 233–235).
- **B81.** Shamhat → Enkidu: he is handsome and dignified, stronger than you, and never sleeps (about I 236–239).
- **B82.** Shamhat → Enkidu: give up your sinful thoughts; Shamash loves Gilgamesh, and Anu, Enlil and Ea have made him wise (I 240–about 242).
- **B83.** Shamhat → Enkidu: Gilgamesh was already dreaming of you in Uruk before you came from the uplands (about I 243–244). The retold dreams follow (I.14–I.15).

### I.14 Retold: Gilgamesh's first dream (the rock from the sky) and Ninsun's interpretation (I 245–about 272; pp. 9–10)
- **B84.** Gilgamesh gets up and tells his mother his dream from the night (I 245–about 246).
- **B85.** **Dream (Gilgamesh → Ninsun):** the stars appeared above him, and one fell in front of him like a rock from the sky (about I 247–248).
- **B86.** **Dream:** he tried to lift it and to roll it, but it was too heavy to move (about I 249–250).
- **B87.** **Dream:** all Uruk gathered and crowded around it (about I 251–254).
- **B88.** **Dream:** the people kissed its feet as if it were an infant; he loved and embraced it as he would a wife (I 255–about 256).
- **B89.** **Dream:** he carried it to his mother and laid it at her feet, and she made it his equal (about I 257–258).
- **B90.** (Within Shamhat's speech) The narration introduces Ninsun as clever, wise and learned (about I 259–260).
- **B91.** **Interpretation (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** she repeats the dream back to him, adding that it was she, Ninsun, who made the thing his equal (about I 261–267).
- **B92.** **Prophecy (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** a mighty comrade will come and save his friend. He will be the strongest in the land, Gilgamesh will love him like a wife, and he will often save Gilgamesh (about I 268–272).

### I.15 Retold: Gilgamesh's second dream (the axe), Ninsun's interpretation, and Gilgamesh's wish (about I 273–298; pp. 10–11)
- **B93.** After a second dream, Gilgamesh goes in to his mother and says he has dreamed again (about I 273–276).
- **B94.** **Dream (Gilgamesh → Ninsun):** an axe lay in a street of Uruk, and all the people crowded around it (about I 277–282).
- **B95.** **Dream:** he laid it at his mother's feet and loved it like a wife, and she made it his equal (about I 283–285).
- **B96.** (Within Shamhat's speech) The narration introduces Ninsun as before (about I 286–287).
- **B97.** **Interpretation (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** the axe is a friend; you will love him like a wife, and I will make him your equal (about I 288–290).
- **B98.** **Prophecy (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** she repeats that a mighty comrade will come and save his friend, the strongest in the land (about I 291–293).
- **B99.** Gilgamesh → Ninsun: may this happen, by Enlil's command (about I 294–295).
- **B100.** Gilgamesh → Ninsun: may I find a friend to advise me; I will have one (about I 296–297).
- **B101.** Shamhat (closing her speech): that is how Gilgamesh saw his dreams (about I 298).

### I.16 Shamhat and Enkidu make love again (about I 299–300; p. 11)
- **B102.** Narrator: once Shamhat has told Enkidu the dreams, the two begin making love (about I 299–300). Tablet I ends here, at the marker I 300.

## Tablet II. The Taming of Enkidu

### II.1 The second week; Shamhat invites Enkidu into human society and clothes him (II 1, then about P 44–71; pp. 11–12)
*Source note:* Only the first line is from the standard version (II 1). After it comes a lacuna, which George fills with P, warning that "the overlapping lines are slightly different" (p. 11). The standard-version lines about II 2–35 are lost.
- **B103.** Narrator (SB): Enkidu is sitting before her; the line breaks off (II 1).
- **B104.** Narrator (P): while Gilgamesh was telling his dream, Enkidu sat before the harlot (about P 44–45).
- **B105.** Narrator (P): they make love, and Enkidu forgets the wild where he was born (P 46–about 47).
- **B106.** Narrator (P): they couple for seven days and seven nights (about P 48–50).
- **B107.** Shamhat → Enkidu (P): you look like a god to me; why live with the animals? (about P 51–55).
- **B108.** Shamhat → Enkidu (P): come, I will lead you to Uruk, to Eanna, Anu's temple (about P 56–60).
- **B109.** Shamhat → Enkidu (P): there, men practice skilled crafts, and you too will find your place among them; you are cut off from the lands that have no shepherd (about P 61–65).
- **B110.** Narrator (P): her advice wins him over (about P 66–67).
- **B111.** Narrator (P): she takes off her garment, dresses him in part of it and wears the other part herself (P 70–about 71).

### II.2 Shamhat leads Enkidu to the shepherds' camp (II 36–about 43; p. 12)
*Source note:* Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II resumes," p. 12).
- **B112.** Narrator: she leads him by the hand, like a god, to the shepherds' camp by the sheep-pen (II 36–about 37).
- **B113.** Narrator: the shepherds crowd around him and discuss him (about II 38–39).
- **B114.** Shepherds (to one another): he is built just like Gilgamesh, tall and towering (II 40–about 41).
- **B115.** Shepherds: this must be Enkidu, born in the uplands, with strength like a rock from the sky (about II 42–43).

### II.3 Bread and ale; Enkidu is groomed and "turned into a man" (about II 44–46, then P 90–about 111; pp. 13–14)
*Source note:* Standard version for about II 44–46. Then George: "Here Tablet II becomes fragmentary again, and the episode is best taken from" P (p. 13). The standard-version lines about II 47–58 are not given.
- **B116.** Narrator (SB): they put bread and ale in front of him (about II 44–45).
- **B117.** Narrator (SB): Enkidu does not eat the bread; he eyes it *askance* (about II 46).
- **B118.** Narrator (P): he does not know how to eat bread or drink ale, never having been taught (P 90–about 91).
- **B119.** Shamhat → Enkidu (P): eat the bread and drink the ale (about P 92–97).
- **B120.** Narrator (P): he eats until he is full and drinks seven jugs of ale (P 100–about 101).
- **B121.** Narrator (P): he relaxes, sings, and grows cheerful and bright-faced (about P 102–105).
- **B122.** Narrator (P): the barber grooms his hairy body; he is anointed with oil and becomes a man (about P 106–107).
- **B123.** Narrator (P): he puts on a garment, looks like a warrior, and takes up a weapon to fight lions (P 110–about 111).

### II.4 Enkidu guards the shepherds at night (about II 59–62; p. 14)
*Source note:* Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II resumes again," p. 14).
- **B124.** Narrator: while the shepherds sleep at night, Enkidu kills wolves and drives off lions (about II 59–60; the night line is wholly restored).
- **B125.** Narrator: the senior shepherds sleep while Enkidu, their shepherd boy, stays awake (about II 61–62).

### II.5 The wedding guest: Enkidu learns of the king's right to the bride (about II 63–64, then P 135–165; pp. 14–15)
*Source note:* Standard version for about II 63–64. Then "another lacuna intervenes in Tablet II," filled again from P (George, p. 14). The standard-version lines about II 65–102 are not given.
- **B126.** Narrator (SB): a man invited to a wedding is on his way to Uruk for the feast (about II 63–64).
- **B127.** Narrator (P): Enkidu, who is taking his pleasure with Shamhat, looks up and sees the man (P 135–about 136).
- **B128.** Enkidu → Shamhat (P): bring him here; I want to know why he has come (about P 137–141).
- **B129.** Narrator (P): Shamhat calls to the man and goes up to him (about P 142–143).
- **B130.** Shamhat → fellow (P): where are you hurrying, and why is your journey so hard? (P 145–about 146).
- **B131.** Fellow → Enkidu (P): he is invited to a wedding, since marriage is the people's lot, and he will heap the ceremonial table with fine food (about P 147–152).
- **B132.** Fellow → Enkidu (P): for Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, the bride's *veil* will be parted for him as the one who chooses first. He says this twice (about P 153–157).
- **B133.** Fellow → Enkidu (P): Gilgamesh sleeps with the bride before the bridegroom does; the gods decreed it, and she was destined for him from his birth (about P 158–162).
- **B134.** Narrator (P): hearing this, Enkidu goes pale with anger (P 165).

### II.6 Enkidu enters Uruk and blocks Gilgamesh's path (P 175–about 201; p. 15)
*Source note:* P only, continuing after the lacuna (about P 166–174).
- **B135.** Narrator: Enkidu leads the way and Shamhat follows (P 175).
- **B136.** Narrator: he walks into Uruk, and a crowd forms around him (about P 176–177).
- **B137.** Narrator: he stops in the street while the people gather and talk about him (P 180–about 181).
- **B138.** People (among themselves): he has Gilgamesh's build but is shorter and heavier-boned; he must be the one born in the uplands, raised on animals' milk (about P 182–187).
- **B139.** Narrator: Uruk held regular festivals of sacrifice; the young men celebrated and set up a *champion* as a rival for the godlike Gilgamesh (P 190–195).
- **B140.** Narrator: the bed was prepared for the goddess of weddings, and Gilgamesh met the maiden at night (about P 196–197).
- **B141.** Narrator: Enkidu steps out into the street and blocks Gilgamesh's way (P 200–about 201).

### II.7 The crowd around Enkidu; he bars the wedding-house door; Gilgamesh and Enkidu fight (II 103–about 117; p. 16)
*Source note:* Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II becomes legible once more," p. 15).
- **B142.** Narrator: all Uruk stands and crowds around Enkidu (II 103–about 106).
- **B143.** Narrator: they kiss his feet as if he were an infant; a broken half-line follows (about II 107–108).
- **B144.** Narrator: the bed is ready for the goddess of weddings, and a substitute has been set up for the godlike Gilgamesh (about II 109–110).
- **B145.** Narrator: Enkidu plants his foot in the doorway of the wedding house and will not let Gilgamesh in (about II 111–112).
- **B146.** Narrator: they grapple at the door and fight on into the street and the Square of the Land (about II 113–114).
- **B147.** Narrator: the doorposts shake and the wall trembles (II 115).
- **B148.** Narrator (restored repeat): the fight in the street and square, and the shaking doorposts and wall, are told again (about II 116–117).

### II.8 The fight ends; Enkidu acclaims Gilgamesh's kingship (about P 228–240; p. 16)
*Source note:* George: "Another lacuna intervenes, again partly to be filled by" P (p. 16). These are P's last lines: "At this point the Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet ends" (p. 16).
- **B149.** Narrator: Gilgamesh goes down on one knee, with one foot on the ground (about P 228).
- **B150.** Narrator: his anger cools, and he stops fighting (P 230).
- **B151.** Narrator: after he stops, Enkidu speaks to him (about P 231–232).
- **B152.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: your mother, the goddess Ninsun, bore you as one of a kind (P 235–about 236).
- **B153.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: you are raised above all warriors, and Enlil destined you to be king of the people (about P 237–240).

### II.9 Gilgamesh presents Enkidu to Ninsun; Ninsun speaks of Enkidu's lack of kin (II 162–about 177; pp. 16–17)
*Source note:* Standard version. George: "After another lacuna Tablet II resumes with an episode in which Gilgamesh introduces Enkidu to his mother" (p. 16).
- **B154.** Speaker (Gilgamesh, per George's note) → Ninsun: the end of a speech about Enkidu: the strongest in the land, strong as a rock from the sky, tall and towering (II 162–about 164).
- **B155.** Narrator: Ninsun begins to speak to her son (II 165–about 168).
- **B156.** (Lacuna: about five lines lost.)
- **B157.** Ninsun → Gilgamesh (fragment): something done "Bitterly"; Enkidu has no *kith or kin*; with long shaggy hair, born in the wild, he has no *brother* (about II 174–177).

### II.10 Enkidu weeps; the two take hands; Gilgamesh asks why (about II 178–191; p. 17)
*Source note:* Standard version.
- **B158.** Narrator: Enkidu, standing there, hears her words, thinks them over and sits down *weeping* (about II 178–179).
- **B159.** Narrator: his eyes fill with tears; his arms go limp; his strength *drains away* (II 180–about 181).
- **B160.** Narrator: they take hold of each other and *join* hands; both lines are broken (about II 182–183).
- **B161.** Narrator: Gilgamesh (broken) speaks to Enkidu (about II 184–185).
- **B162.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: why are you in tears, limp and drained? (about II 186–187).
- **B163.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: my friend, my heart is grieved (about II 188–189; broken at the end).
- **B164.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: sobbing makes his *legs* shake, and terror has entered his heart (II 190–about 191).

### II.11 Gilgamesh proposes killing Humbaba in the Forest of Cedar; Enkidu warns him (about Y 89–119, then II 216–about 229; pp. 17–18)
*Source note:* Y, which "fills another gap in the standard version" (George, p. 17), for about Y 89–119. Then "The text of Tablet II resumes" (p. 18) at II 216.
- **B165.** Narrator (Y): Gilgamesh speaks to Enkidu (about Y 89–90).
- **B166.** (Lacuna: about Y 91–96 lost.)
- **B167.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu (Y): let us kill ferocious Humbaba and end his power (Y 97–about 98).
- **B168.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu (Y): let us take him by surprise in his lair in the Forest of Cedar (Y 100–about 101).

## Tablet III. Preparations for the Expedition

### III.1 The elders advise Gilgamesh and put him in Enkidu's care
- **B206.** Narration: the elders address Gilgamesh. This introductory couplet is wholly restored, and George numbers it as the last line of Tablet II.
- **B207.** Elders → Gilgamesh: they wish him a safe return to the city's quay, warn him not to trust only in his own strength, and tell him to watch carefully and make every blow count.
- **B208.** Elders → Gilgamesh: they cite a proverb: the man who walks ahead protects his comrade, and the man who knows the road keeps his friend safe.
- **B209.** Elders → Gilgamesh: so Enkidu should lead. He knows the route to the Forest of Cedar, he is battle-hardened, he will look after his friend, and he will bring Gilgamesh home safely to "the wives" (George marks this rendering as uncertain).
- **B210.** Elders → Enkidu (a stage direction added by George marks the change of addressee): the assembly is entrusting the king to him, and he must bring the king back and hand him over to them again.

### III.2 Gilgamesh proposes a visit to Ninsun
- **B211.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he suggests they go together to the Palace Sublime and appear before his mother, the great queen Ninsun.
- **B212.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he explains why: Ninsun is clever, wise and knowledgeable about everything, and she will guide their steps with good advice.

### III.3 At the Palace Sublime: Gilgamesh asks his mother's blessing
- **B213.** The two friends walk hand in hand to the Palace Sublime.
- **B214.** Gilgamesh stands up and goes in before Ninsun, the goddess who is his mother (the words naming her are restored).
- **B215.** Gilgamesh → Ninsun: he announces that, bold as he is, he will travel the long road to Humbaba's home, into a fight and along a route that are both unknown to him.
- **B216.** Gilgamesh → Ninsun: he asks for her blessing on the journey, so that he may see her again unharmed and come back happy through the city gate.
- **B217.** Gilgamesh → Ninsun (a vow): if he returns, he will hold the New Year festival two times in one year, with celebrations and drumming in her presence.

### III.4 Ninsun bathes, dresses and goes up to the roof
- **B218.** Ninsun, called Wild-Cow (the epithet is restored here), listens to the two at length and with sorrow.
- **B219.** She enters the bath-house seven times and washes in water infused with tamarisk and soapwort.
- **B220.** She puts on a fine garment and chooses a jewel for her breast (both verbs are uncertain restorations).
- **B221.** She puts on a cap (uncertain restoration) and then her tiara.
- **B222.** A damaged line mentions harlots and the ground; its sense cannot be recovered.
- **B223.** She climbs the stairs to the roof and sets up a censer there for Shamash.
- **B224.** She scatters incense and raises her arms toward the Sun God in appeal (her prayer follows in III.5).

### III.5 Ninsun's prayer to Shamash for the journey
- **B225.** Ninsun → Shamash (complaint): she asks why he burdened her son with a spirit that cannot rest. Because Shamash has stirred him, Gilgamesh will now take the long road to Humbaba's home and face a fight and a route he has never known.
- **B226.** Ninsun → Shamash: she sets out the span of time she means: all the days of the journey out and back, until Gilgamesh reaches the Forest of Cedar, kills the fierce Humbaba and rids the land of the evil that Shamash hates.
- **B227.** Ninsun → Shamash: during that time, each day as Shamash makes his round of the world (an uncertain restoration), she wants Aya, Shamash's fearless bride, to remind him to place Gilgamesh in the keeping of the night watches. (A message Aya is asked to pass on.)
- **B228.** [About five lines are lost: III about 58-62.]
- **B229.** Ninsun → Shamash (praise of the sunrise): when Shamash rises he lets the herds out [through the gates] (uncertain), brings light to people, gives shape to the hills (uncertain), brightens the sky and rouses the wild animals with his red glow (uncertain).
- **B230.** [About five more lines are lost: III about 67-71.]
- **B231.** Ninsun → Shamash: the crowd assembles as his light appears, and the Anunnaki gods wait for his radiance. She repeats the request that Aya remind him to give Gilgamesh into the care of the night watches (largely restored).
- **B232.** Ninsun → Shamash (requests for the road): give Gilgamesh a safe road; touch Enkidu so that he walks in front, since he knows the way to the forest; let him open a path and make the mountains crossable (much of this is restored).
- **B233.** Ninsun → Shamash: let "him" carry a torch (a word after "torch" is lost) all the way to the forest; let the days be long and the nights short; let him travel girded and with long strides.
- **B234.** Ninsun → Shamash: let "him" set up camp at nightfall so they can lie down and rest in the evening.
- **B235.** Ninsun → Shamash: she wants Aya to remind him of one more thing: on the day the two meet Humbaba, Shamash should stir up the thirteen great winds against Humbaba (listed under Specifics), darken Humbaba's face, and so let Gilgamesh's weapons overcome him. (A request; Shamash does exactly this in Tablet V, V about 159-165.)
- **B236.** Ninsun → Shamash: once his own "fires" (uncertain) are lit, he should turn and look on the "supplicant" (uncertain).
- **B237.** Ninsun → Shamash (comforts promised to him): his swift mules will carry him on; a seat to rest on and a bed for the night will be waiting; his brother gods will bring him food he likes; and Aya will dry his face with the hem of her robe (most of the verbs here are uncertain restorations).

### III.6 Ninsun's second request: Gilgamesh's place among the gods
- **B238.** Ninsun puts a second request to Shamash, framed as a series of questions.
- **B239.** Ninsun → Shamash: she asks whether Gilgamesh will not be counted among the gods (the verb is uncertain), share heaven with Shamash, and hold a scepter and crown together with the Moon.
- **B240.** Ninsun → Shamash: she asks whether he will not gain wisdom alongside Ea of the Ocean Below, govern the black-headed people alongside Irnina, and live alongside Ningishzida in the Land-of-No-Return.

### III.7 Ninsun adopts Enkidu and blesses the two travelers
- **B241.** When her charge to Shamash is done, Ninsun, praised again as clever and wise, kisses the floor (uncertain).
- **B242.** She puts out the censer and descends from the roof (uncertain).
- **B243.** She calls Enkidu to her and makes a formal declaration.
- **B244.** Ninsun → Enkidu: although she did not give birth to him, from now on his offspring are to be counted with Gilgamesh's votaries: the priestesses, the hierodules and the temple women.
- **B245.** She hangs "the symbols" around Enkidu's neck.
- **B246.** Ninsun (declaration): she recalls that priestesses once took in the foundling and that the Divine Daughters raised the fostered child. She now takes Enkidu, whom she loves (uncertain), as her son, and says Gilgamesh will treat him with favor as a brother (uncertain).
- **B247.** [About one line is lost: III about 129.]
- **B248.** The speaker after the gap is presumably Ninsun (not named) → both: as they travel together to the forest, may their days be long and their nights short, their loins girded and their stride long; they should camp when night falls; and may Shamash guard them by day (this last line is a conjectural restoration).

## Tablet V. The Combat with Humbaba

### V.1 The Forest of Cedar seen from its edge
- **B329.** The two stand and wonder at the forest, looking up at the tall cedars and at the way in.
- **B330.** Humbaba's comings and goings have worn a track; the paths run straight and are well used.
- **B331.** They see the Mountain of Cedar, where gods and goddesses have their seat and throne.

- **B522.** [Hittite, dream] Anu → Enlil: because these two killed the Bull of Heaven and killed Humbaba, the guardian of the cedar-forested mountains, one of the two must die.
- **B523.** [Hittite, dream] Enlil: Enkidu is to die, not Gilgamesh.
- **B524.** [Hittite, dream] Shamash → Enlil ("the hero Enlil"): wasn't it at Enlil's word that they killed the Bull and Humbaba? Must innocent Enkidu die now?
- **B525.** [Hittite, dream] Enlil, angry, → Shamash: Shamash marched along with them every day as if he were their comrade.

### VII.2 Enkidu weeps before his "brother" (Hittite passage, within the lost VII 2-36; p. 54)
- **B526.** [Hittite] Enkidu lies down in front of Gilgamesh, his tears running like streams.
- **B527.** [Hittite] Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his brother is dear to him. He will never again be raised up to see his brother; he will sit among the dead and cross their threshold, and never set eyes on his brother again.

### VII.3 Enkidu curses the cedar door (VII 37-63, pp. 54-55)
- **B528.** Enkidu raises his eyes, as though toward the door, and talks to it as if to a man (both phrases are conjectural restorations).
- **B529.** Enkidu → door: it is a door of the woodland and has no sense; he has the understanding it lacks.
- **B530.** Enkidu → door, retelling how it was made: he searched twenty leagues for the finest timber until he found a tall cedar, a tree without rival in the Forest of Cedar.
- **B531.** Its measurements: six rods high, two rods wide, one cubit thick. Its pole and its pivots, top and bottom, are a single piece.
- **B532.** He made it, lifted it and hung it upright in Nippur.
- **B533.** Enkidu → door: had he known it would repay him like this, he would have taken his axe to it and floated it on a raft down to Ebabbara.
- **B534.** He would have taken it to Ebabbara, Shamash's temple, set the cedar up in its gate and placed the Lion-Bird and a bull colossus in the doorway as guardians at its entrance.
- **B535.** A broken passage: he would have done something to the city for Shamash, and something in Uruk, because Shamash heard what he said and, in a time of danger, gave him a weapon.
- **B536.** Enkidu → door, the curse: as the one who made it and raised it, he can also curse it and tear it down. May a king who comes after him hate it, or hang it where no one can see it, and erase Enkidu's name from it and write his own.

### VII.4 Gilgamesh weeps and promises prayers and a gold statue (VII 64-83, pp. 55-56)
- **B537.** "He" tears off his garments and throws away his finery, and as he listens his tears flow quickly. ⚠️ The line does not name its subject; the next couplet says it is Gilgamesh who is listening to Enkidu and weeping.
- **B538.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: (broken) Enkidu, who had understanding and reason, now speaks madness?
- **B539.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: why does his heart speak madness? The dream was extraordinary and the anxiety great. His feverish lips buzzed like flies; the misgivings were great and the dream rare.
- **B540.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a saying: grief is what the dead leave to the survivor; the dead leave sorrow to those still living.
- **B541.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a promise of prayer: he will beg the great gods, seek out Shamash, appeal to Enkidu's god, pray to Anu, father of the gods, in Enkidu's presence, have the great counselor Enlil hear him, and win favor with Ea.
- **B542.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a promise of a statue: he will make Enkidu a statue in gold without limit. The next line is lost.

### VII.5 Enkidu refuses the offering: Enlil's word is fixed (VII 84-89, p. 56)
- **B543.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he should give no silver, no gold, no gemstones.
- **B544.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: what Enlil has said, the gods cannot take back. What he orders, he does not erase; what he lays down, he does not erase.
- **B545.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his own fate is fixed; people go to their doom before their time.

### VII.6 At dawn, Enkidu curses the hunter (VII 90-99, p. 56)
- **B546.** At first light Enkidu lifts his head and laments to Shamash, his tears running in the sunlight.
- **B547.** Enkidu → Shamash: he pleads for his precious life.
- **B548.** Enkidu → Shamash, a curse on the hunter (the trapper) who "let me not get as much as my friend": may the hunter not get as much as his friend; may Shamash destroy his profit and cut his income; may his share be reduced in Shamash's presence; may the god of any house he enters leave it by the window.

### VII.7 Enkidu curses Shamhat (VII 100-131, with VII 124-129 lost; pp. 56-57)
*(the curse is condensed here into groups)*
- **B549.** After cursing the hunter to his heart's content, Enkidu decides to curse Shamhat the harlot too.
- **B550.** Enkidu → Shamhat: he will fix her destiny with a great curse that lasts forever and takes effect at once.
- **B551.** No home or family: she will never get a home she can enjoy, never live among a family, never sit in the young women's quarters.
- **B552.** Ruined clothes: the ground will soil her best garment and drunkards will dirty her festive gown; she will never have a beautiful household.
- **B553.** A bare dwelling: her home will be a potter's pit, with no bedroom, family shrine or hearth and no fine furniture; her couch will be a hard bench.
- **B554.** Living rough: she will sit in the dust of the crossroads, sleep among ruins and stand in the shade of the city wall, and thorns and briars will tear her feet.
- **B555.** Abuse and neglect: drunk and sober men alike will hit her face, wives will take her to court, no builder will plaster her roof, an owl will roost in her room, and no banquet will be held in her house.
- **B556.** Six lines are lost.
- **B557.** The reason: she made him weak when he was undefiled; she weakened him in the wild.

### VII.8 Shamash rebukes Enkidu and foretells his funeral (VII 132-147, pp. 57-58)
- **B558.** Shamash hears Enkidu's words, and a voice calls out from the sky at once.
- **B559.** Shamash → Enkidu: why curse Shamhat? She fed him bread fit for a god and ale fit for a king, dressed him in a splendid garment and gave him handsome Gilgamesh as his companion.
- **B560.** Shamash → Enkidu, a prophecy: Gilgamesh, his friend and brother, will lay him out on a magnificent bed of honor, place him at his left on a seat of repose, and the rulers of the underworld will all kiss his feet.
- **B561.** Prophecy: Gilgamesh will have the people of Uruk mourn and lament him, and fill the fair people with grief.
- **B562.** Prophecy: after Enkidu is gone, Gilgamesh will let his hair grow matted in mourning and wander the wild dressed in a lion's skin.

### VII.9 Enkidu calms down and blesses Shamhat (VII 148-161, p. 58)
- **B563.** Enkidu hears "Shamash the hero," and his angry, furious heart grows calm.
- **B564.** Enkidu → Shamhat: he will fix her destiny again; the mouth that cursed her will now bless her.
- **B565.** Blessing: governors and noblemen will love her. Men a league away will slap their thighs, and men two leagues away will shake out their hair.
- **B566.** Blessing: soldiers will be quick to unbuckle their belts for her and will give her obsidian, lapis lazuli, gold and gold earrings.
- **B567.** Blessing: Ishtar, the ablest of the gods, will get her into the house of a man whose home is secure and whose wealth is piled high, and his wife, mother of seven though she is, will be abandoned for her.

### VII.10 Enkidu's second dream, part 1: seized by the dark-faced man (VII 162-182, with VII 178-181 lost; pp. 58-59)
- **B568.** Enkidu, troubled, lies by himself and broods.
- **B569.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he had a dream during the night.
- **B570.** [Dream] The heavens thundered, the earth echoed back, and he was standing between them.
- **B571.** [Dream] There was a man with a darkened face and features as fierce as a Lion-Bird's. His hands were a lion's paws and his claws an eagle's talons. He grabbed Enkidu by the hair and overpowered him.
- **B572.** [Dream] Enkidu hit him, but he sprang back like a skipping rope; he hit Enkidu and overturned him like a raft.
- **B573.** [Dream] He trampled Enkidu like a mighty wild bull and drenched his body with poisonous slaver.
- **B574.** [Dream] Enkidu called to his friend to save him (the rest of the line is lost).
- **B575.** [Dream] Gilgamesh was afraid of the man, and did something (lost).
- **B576.** Four lines are lost.
- **B577.** [Dream] The man struck him and turned him into a dove.

### VII.11 Enkidu's second dream, part 2: the House of Dust (VII 183-208, then VII 209-250 lost; pp. 59-60)
*(all dream, retold)*
- **B578.** The man tied Enkidu's arms like a bird's wings and led him captive to the house of darkness, the seat of Irkalla.

- **B580.** Dust lay thick on the door and bolt, and a deathly silence hung over the House of Dust.

- **B582.** Priests of several kinds were there: *en*-priests, *lagar*-priests, lustration-priests, *lumahhu*-priests and the *gudapsû*-priests of the great gods.

- **B594.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his god has turned against him; he is not dying like a man who falls in battle.

## Tablet VIII. The Funeral of Enkidu

### VIII.1 The lament, part 1: Gilgamesh calls on all things to mourn (VIII 1-41, pp. 61-62)
*(the long list of mourners is condensed into groups, in the order the text gives them)*
- **B598.** At first light Gilgamesh begins to mourn his friend.
- **B599.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu, his origins: his mother was a gazelle and his father a wild donkey; wild asses reared him on their milk and the beasts of the wild taught him the pastures.
- **B600.** Wish: may the paths of the Forest of Cedar mourn him day and night without pause.
- **B601.** Wish: may the elders of Uruk and the crowd who blessed the two of them mourn him.
- **B602.** Wish: may the peaks of the hills and mountains mourn (the next line is lost apart from "pure"); may the pastures lament like his mother.
- **B603.** Wish: may the myrtle, cypress and cedar mourn him, the trees the two crept through in their fury.
- **B604.** Wish: may the wild animals mourn him: bear, hyena, panther, cheetah, stag, jackal, lion, wild bull, deer, ibex, all the beasts.
- **B605.** Wish: may the sacred river Ulay, whose banks they walked, and the holy Euphrates, whose water they poured out as libation from skins, mourn him.
- **B606.** Wish: may the young men of Uruk, who saw the two of them fight and kill the Bull of Heaven, mourn him.
- **B607.** Wish: may the working people who fed and praised him mourn him: the ploughman in his furrow, who sings his name; someone in Uruk (lost) who spreads his name; the herdsman, who gave him milk and butter; the shepherd boy, who gave him ghee; the brewer, who gave him ale; the harlot, who anointed his head with sweet oil.
- **B608.** Broken wishes: someone in the wedding house (lost); others (lost) who are to mourn him like brothers and let down their hair like sisters, and weep for him, his mother and father (broken).
- **B609.** Gilgamesh: this very day he himself will mourn him.

### VIII.2 The lament, part 2: Gilgamesh before the young men and elders (VIII 42-56, pp. 62-63)
- **B610.** Gilgamesh → the young men and the elders of Uruk: listen to him.
- **B611.** Gilgamesh → them: he will weep for his friend Enkidu and wail bitterly, like a hired mourning woman.

- **B613.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he calls him by a triple epithet, twice over: a wild ass on the run, a donkey of the uplands, a panther of the wild (VIII 50-51).
- **B614.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: together they climbed the mountains, seized and killed the Bull of Heaven, and destroyed Humbaba, who lived in the Forest of Cedar.
- **B615.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: what is this sleep that has taken him? He has lost consciousness and does not hear.

### VIII.3 Death confirmed; Gilgamesh's grief (VIII 57-64, pp. 63-64)
- **B616.** Enkidu does not lift his head.
- **B617.** Gilgamesh feels his heart; it is no longer beating.
- **B618.** He covers his friend's face as one veils a bride.
- **B619.** He circles around him like an eagle.
- **B620.** Like a lioness robbed of her cubs, he paces back and forth.
- **B621.** He tears out his curly hair in clumps.
- **B622.** He rips off his finery and throws it away as if it were taboo.

### VIII.4 The statue commissioned (VIII 65-72, then VIII 73-83 lost; p. 64)
- **B623.** At first light, Gilgamesh sends a summons out across the land.
- **B624.** Gilgamesh → the forgemaster, lapidary, coppersmith, goldsmith and jeweler: make a statue of his friend in his likeness, such as no one has ever made of a friend before.
- **B625.** Gilgamesh, specifying the statue: his friend's limbs are to be of ... (lost). Then, speaking to Enkidu directly: his eyebrows will be of lapis lazuli, his chest of gold, his body of ... (lost).
- **B626.** Eleven lines are lost.

### VIII.5 Gilgamesh's promise to the dead Enkidu (VIII 84-91, p. 64)
- **B627.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he will lay him out on a magnificent bed, a bed of honor.
- **B628.** He will set him at his left on a seat of repose, and the rulers of the underworld will all kiss his feet.

### VIII.6 The treasury: grave goods for Enkidu (VIII 92-130, pp. 64-65)

- **B637.** Someone (the subject is lost) carries all the meat to the rulers of the underworld.

## Tablet IX. The Wanderings of Gilgamesh

### IX.1 Lament in the wild, and the night at the mountain pass (IX 1-12; pp. 68-69)
- **B660.** Narration: Gilgamesh roams the open country, weeping hard over Enkidu (IX 1-2).
- **B661.** Gilgamesh → (no listener): he asks himself whether death will make him the same as Enkidu, and says grief has taken hold of him (IX 3-4).
- **B662.** Gilgamesh: fear of dying is what drives him through the wilds; he means to reach Uta-napishti, whose father was Ubar-Tutu (IX 5-6).
- **B663.** *Retold inside the speech:* he was traveling fast and arrived at a mountain pass at night (IX 7-8).
- **B664.** *Retold:* there were lions, and he was frightened (IX 9).
- **B665.** *Retold prayer:* he looked up to the moon and prayed; his pleas went to Ishtar (her name restored), whom he calls the gods' lamp, and he asked the Moon God and Ishtar (both names restored) to protect him (IX 10-12). The quoted speech ends at IX 12; IX 13 returns to third-person narration.

### IX.2 The dream and the fight with the lions (IX 13-18; p. 69)
- **B666.** He lies down and later wakes from a dream (IX 13). *Dream:* what he dreamed does not survive.
- **B667.** After a lost word or two, he takes heart in the moon's presence and is glad to be alive (IX 14).
- **B668.** He grips his axe and pulls his sword (the word restored) from his belt (IX 15-16).
- **B669.** He drops among the lions (the word restored) like a shot arrow, striking, killing and scattering them (IX 17-18).

### IX.3 Gap filled from the Old Babylonian Sippar tablet: lion skins, wells, and Shamash (IX 19-37 lost; text from Sippar tablet column i 2′-15′; p. 69)
- **B670.** He wears their hides and eats their meat (Si i 2′). "Their" points back to lines now lost; placed by George after the lion fight, it reads as the lions' ⚠️.
- **B671.** He digs (conjectural) wells where none had been before, and drinks from them while he chases the winds (Si i 3′-4′).
- **B672.** Shamash is troubled; bending down (uncertain), he addresses Gilgamesh (Si i 5′-6′).
- **B673.** Shamash → Gilgamesh: he asks where all this wandering is leading and tells him that the life he is after is beyond his reach (Si i 7′-8′).
- **B674.** Gilgamesh → Shamash (whom he calls a hero): after so much roaming, will he find too little rest in the Netherworld? He will be asleep there for years without end (Si i 9′-12′).
- **B675.** Gilgamesh → Shamash: he wants his eyes to look on the sun and take their fill of light. If the darkness is "hidden" (uncertain), how much light is left? When will the dead ever see sunlight? (Si i 13′-15′).

### IX.4 Mashu's twin mountains and the scorpion-men (IX 38-59; pp. 70-71)
- **B676.** Narration: Gilgamesh reaches Mashu, a pair of mountains that keep watch every day over the sun's rising (the word "sun" restored). Their peaks hold up (verb restored) the structure of the sky, and their roots go down to the Netherworld (IX 38-41).
- **B677.** Scorpion-men guard the gate. They inspire dread, one look from them kills, and their glow is so terrible it overpowers the mountains; they watch over the sun as it rises and as it sets (IX 42-45).
- **B678.** At the sight of them Gilgamesh hides his face in terror. Then he steadies himself and goes closer (IX 46-47).
- **B679.** Scorpion-man → his mate: the newcomer's body is made of divine flesh (IX 48-49).
- **B680.** Mate → scorpion-man: he is two parts god and one part man (IX 50-51).
- **B681.** Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh (addressed as king and as divine flesh; restored): he asks how Gilgamesh managed so long a road, how he got into his presence, and how he crossed rivers that are dangerous to cross. He wants to hear about the journey and where Gilgamesh is heading (IX 52-59).

### IX.5 Gilgamesh states his quest; the scorpion-man warns him (about IX 60-90; p. 71)
- **B682.** [IX 60-74, about 15 lines: lost.]
- **B683.** Gilgamesh → scorpion-man: he is looking (conjectural) for the way (conjectural) to his ancestor Uta-napishti, who took part in the gods' assembly and gained (restored) everlasting life. He expects Uta-napishti to reveal to him (conjectural) the truth about death and life (IX 75-77).
- **B684.** Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh: no one like him has ever come before (restored), and no one has ever made the mountain journey (conjectural) (IX 78-81).
- **B685.** Scorpion-man: the way through the mountain lasts (conjectural) twelve double-hours, all of it in thick darkness, without light (IX 82-83).
- **B686.** Scorpion-man, very broken: phrases about the sun's rising, the sun's setting, another "setting," and someone being sent out (about IX 84-87).
- **B687.** Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh: broken questions about how he will manage and whether he will go in (about IX 88-90). The four printed lines after the marker IX 85 stand for five line numbers, so exact numbers in IX 85-90 cannot be fixed.

### IX.6 The end of Gilgamesh's reply; the scorpion-man lets him pass (IX 125-135; pp. 71-72)
- **B688.** [IX 91-124, about 34 lines: lost. George's note says only that the text picks up at the end of Gilgamesh's reply.]
- **B689.** Gilgamesh → scorpion-man, end of the reply: broken lines that mention sorrow, his face burned (restored) by cold and sun, exhaustion, and a closing "now you…" (IX 125-128).
- **B690.** Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh (the address restored): he tells him to go. He wishes that Mashu's mountains let him through, that the mountains and hills guard his way, that they help him on in safety, and that the mountain gate open for him; every one of these verbs is conjectural (IX 129-135).

### IX.7 The Path of the Sun: twelve double-hours in the dark (IX 136-170; pp. 72-73)
- **B691.** Having heard (conjectural) the scorpion-man and taken his words to heart (restored), Gilgamesh sets out (verb restored) on the Sun God's road (IX 136-138).
- **B692.** First double-hour (the rest of the line lost): thick darkness, no light (restored), and he cannot see what is behind him (restored) (IX 139-141).
- **B693.** The same at the second (IX 142-144), third (IX 145-147), fourth (IX 148-150) and fifth (IX 151-153) double-hours.

- **B832.** Wife → Uta-napishti (called "the Distant"): wake him up! Let him go home safely the way he came, back through the gate he came out by (XI 215-218).
- **B833.** Uta-napishti → wife: people are liars, and he will lie to her. She should bake him a loaf for each day, set the loaves by his head, and keep count of his days asleep on the wall (XI 219-222).
- **B834.** She does it: a loaf a day by his head, and each day marked on the wall (XI 223-224).
- **B835.** The first loaf has dried out; the second has gone leathery; the third is soggy; the fourth has turned white; the fifth has gray mold; the sixth is freshly baked; the seventh is still on the coals. Then Uta-napishti touches him, and he wakes (XI 225-230).

- **B838.** Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: what is he to do, where can he go? A thief has seized his flesh (conjectural); Death lives in his bedroom, and wherever he turns, Death will be waiting (about XI 243-246).

### XI.11 Ur-shanabi is banished; Gilgamesh is washed and given royal robes (XI 247-270; pp. 94-95)
- **B839.** Uta-napishti → Ur-shanabi (the boatman): may the landing-place turn him away (restored) and the ferry reject him! He who used to come and go on this shore is banished from it now (XI 247-249). No reason is given ⚠️.
- **B840.** Uta-napishti → Ur-shanabi: the man he brought here is covered in matted hair, and animal skins have spoiled the beauty of his body. Ur-shanabi is to take him to the washtub and have him scrub his hair clean (XI 250-254).
- **B841.** Uta-napishti: he should throw off the skins for the sea to carry away and soak his body until it is clean and fair; he should get a new headcloth and put on royal robes worthy of his rank (XI 255-258).
- **B842.** Uta-napishti: until he reaches his city and the end of his journey, the robes must stay spotless and as good as new (XI 259-261).
- **B843.** Ur-shanabi takes him to the washtub. Gilgamesh washes his hair clean and throws off the skins, which the sea carries away; his body is soaked fair; he puts on a new headcloth (restored) and the royal robes (XI 262-267).
- **B844.** The wish for the robes is repeated, in quotation marks with no speaker named (partly restored) (XI 268-270) ⚠️.

### XI.12 Departure; the wife asks for a parting gift; the secret of the plant (XI 271-286; p. 95)
- **B845.** Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi board the boat, push off, and work it themselves (XI 271-272).
- **B846.** Wife → Uta-napishti (called "the Distant"): Gilgamesh got here through hard labor and hardship; what is Uta-napishti giving him to take home? (XI 273-275).
- **B847.** Gilgamesh takes up a punting-pole and brings the boat back near the shore (XI 276-277). The text does not say whether he heard her ⚠️.
- **B848.** Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: he came through labor and hardship, so what should he be given for the journey home? Uta-napishti will reveal a hidden matter, a secret of the gods (XI 278-282).
- **B849.** Uta-napishti: a certain plant resembles (verb restored) a box-thorn, with prickles like a "dog rose" (uncertain) that will prick (restored) whoever picks it (conjectural). If he can get hold of it, he will be young again as he once was (all conjectural) (XI 283-286).

### XI.13 The dive for the plant (XI 287-293; p. 95)
- **B850.** The moment he hears this, Gilgamesh opens up a channel (restored); the rest of the line is lost (XI 287-288).
- **B851.** He ties heavy stones to his feet (restored), and they drag him down (a word lost) to the Ocean Below (XI 289-290).
- **B852.** He takes hold of the plant and pulls it up (restored), lifting it (conjectural) (XI 291).
- **B853.** He cuts the heavy stones loose from his feet (restored), and the sea throws him up onto its shore (XI 292-293).

### XI.14 Gilgamesh names the plant and plans to test it (XI 294-300; pp. 95-96)
- **B854.** Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi (the boatman): this is the "Plant of Heartbeat," and it can give a man back his strength (XI 294-296).
- **B855.** Gilgamesh: he will carry it to Uruk-the-Sheepfold and try it out first by feeding some to an old man (XI 297-298).
- **B856.** Gilgamesh: if the old man becomes young, he will eat it himself and be young again as he used to be (XI 299-300).

### XI.15 The journey home begins; the pool; the snake (XI 301-307; p. 96)
- **B857.** After twenty leagues they eat; after thirty they halt for the night (XI 301-302).
- **B858.** Gilgamesh comes across a pool of cool water and goes down into it to bathe (XI 303-304).
- **B859.** A snake smells the plant's scent, comes up silently (restored) and carries it off (XI 305-306).
- **B860.** As it turns back, it sheds its skin (XI 307).

### XI.16 Gilgamesh weeps: the "Lion of the Earth" and the lost landmark (XI 308-318; p. 96)
- **B861.** Gilgamesh sits down and weeps, tears running down his face (XI 308-309).
- **B862.** Gilgamesh → the boatman Ur-shanabi: he asks for whose sake (restored) his arms worked so hard and his heart's blood drained away. He has won nothing for himself; all he has done is a good turn to the "Lion of the Earth" (XI 310-314).
- **B863.** Gilgamesh: the tide is rising far and wide by now. When he opened the channel he left the tools behind; what is there to mark the spot for him? If only he had gone back and left the boat beached! (XI 315-318).

- **B864.** After twenty leagues they eat; after thirty they make camp (XI 319-320).
- **B865.** They reach Uruk-the-Sheepfold (XI 321).

- **B867.** Gilgamesh: the city covers one square mile, the date-groves one, the clay-pits one, and Ishtar's temple half, so Uruk spreads over three and a half square miles (XI 327-328). The epic ends here.

## Appendix A. Tablet XII (attached to the series, but not part of the epic)

George gives Tablet XII in his chapter 2, after the Sumerian poem it translates, "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" (p. 134). In it Enkidu is alive again and goes down to the Netherworld, which contradicts Tablets VII-VIII. Its beats are numbered separately (A1, A2, ...). Full record: `01 .../Sumerian 4 - Gilgamesh and the Netherworld (and Tablet XII).md`.

### XII.1 Gilgamesh laments the lost ball and mallet (XII 1-5, p. 146)
- **A1.** Gilgamesh (lament): today he wishes he had left his ball at the carpenter's workshop; he calls on the carpenter's wife, like a mother to him, and daughter, like a little sister, wishing he had left it there (XII 1-3).
- **A2.** Today ball and mallet have both fallen into the Netherworld (XII 4-5).

### XII.2 Enkidu volunteers; Gilgamesh's rules (XII 6-30, p. 146)
- **A3.** Enkidu → Gilgamesh (as "master"): asks why he wept so heartsick and promises to bring the ball and mallet back up from the Netherworld today (XII 6-9).
- **A4.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: if he goes down he must obey these instructions (XII 10-12). The rules and reasons (XII 13-27):
    - no clean clothing, or he will be recognized as a stranger;
    - no sweet oil from a flask, or the smell will draw a crowd;
    - no throwstick, or those it hits will surround him;
    - no staff in his hand, or the shades will tremble;
    - no sandals, so as to make no noise;
    - no kissing the wife or son he loves, no striking the wife or son he hates, or the Netherworld's outcry will catch him.
- **A5.** Gilgamesh → Enkidu: the Mother of Ninazu lies there, her shining shoulders uncovered, her breasts bare like stone flasks (XII 28-30).

### XII.3 Enkidu goes down, breaks every rule, and sees the Mother of Ninazu (XII 31-50, pp. 146-147)
- **A6.** Going down, Enkidu disregards Gilgamesh's instructions (XII 31-32).
- **A7.** Clean clothes: he is recognized as a stranger. Oil: the smell draws them around him (XII 33-36).
- **A8.** Throwstick: the shades tremble (an extra, indented line) and those hit surround him (XII 37-38).
- **A9.** Staff: the shades tremble (XII 39-40).
- **A10.** Sandals: he makes noise (XII 41-42).
- **A11.** He kisses the wife and son he loved and strikes the wife and son he hated (XII 43-46).
- **A12.** The Netherworld's outcry seizes him (XII 47).
- **A13.** The Mother of Ninazu lies there, shoulders uncovered, breasts bare like stone flasks (XII 48-50).

### XII.4 Enkidu does not return; Gilgamesh petitions Enlil, Sîn and Ea (XII 51-78, pp. 147-148)
- **A14.** Narration: Enkidu does not come back up to the world above. Not Namtar, not Asakku, not Nergal's merciless officer took him, and he did not fall in battle; the Netherworld took him (XII 51-54).
- **A15.** The king, weeping for his servant Enkidu, goes alone to Ekur (XII 55-56).
- **A16.** Gilgamesh → Enlil (as Father): his ball and mallet dropped into the Netherworld today; Enkidu went down for them and the Netherworld took him; then the refrain (not Namtar, Asakku or Nergal's officer; not battle) (XII 57-62).
- **A17.** Enlil does not say a word in reply (XII 63).
- **A18.** Gilgamesh goes alone to Ur, Sîn's temple, and repeats the plea to Sîn as Father (XII 64-70).
- **A19.** Sîn, too, says nothing (XII 71).
- **A20.** Gilgamesh goes alone to Eridu, Ea's temple, and repeats the plea to Ea as Father (XII 72-78).

### XII.5 Ea has Shamash open a chink; Enkidu's shade comes up (XII 79-87, p. 148)
- **A21.** Ea helps Gilgamesh (XII 79).
- **A22.** Ea → Shamash: as he climbs into the sky, he might perhaps open a small crack in the Netherworld and bring Enkidu's shade up as a phantom (XII 80-83).

## Appendix B. Episodes found only in older versions

The tablets and poems below tell parts of the story differently from the standard version, or tell episodes it lacks. Their full play-by-play and a table of differences are in the files named.

- **The Old Babylonian epic** (`01 .../Old Babylonian fragments (chapter 3).md`), about the 18th century BC. Its ending is lost: its last surviving scene is the boatman and the punting-poles. Episodes it has that the standard version lacks or tells differently:
    - **The tavern-keeper's advice** (Sippar tablet; scene OB.38). She tells Gilgamesh that the gods kept life for themselves and gave death to mankind. She advises him to eat his fill, enjoy himself day and night, dance, wear clean clothes, wash, cherish the child holding his hand and take pleasure in his wife. He refuses because he is still grieving Enkidu. The text never names her.
    - **On the Yale tablet:** Gilgamesh says only the gods live forever and he will make his name if he falls. He kneels to Shamash and weeps before Lugalbanda. The elders tell him to dig a well at every camp, pour cool water for Shamash and wash his feet in Humbaba's river. The god Wer, not Enlil, appointed Humbaba.
    - **On the Ishchali tablet:** Gilgamesh hesitates while Humbaba's auras escape, Enkidu answers with a saying about a bird and its chicks, and a servant is killed with Humbaba.
    - **On the Iraq Museum tablet,** Enkidu urges Gilgamesh to fell the forest, saying nothing can shame him. **In a Hamran dream tablet,** Enkidu, not Gilgamesh, goes pale with terror and is comforted.
- **Other early fragments** (`01 .../Other early fragments - Ugarit, Hattusa, Emar, Megiddo, Sealand (chapter 4).md`):
    - The Sealand tablet puts the gods Sîn and Ea in place of Gilgamesh and Enkidu, and Ur in place of Uruk.
    - At Ugarit, Gilgamesh wrestles fifty companions.
    - At Megiddo, Enkidu may say his friend had no fear. Only "had no" is readable.
- **The five Sumerian poems** (the `01 .../Sumerian 1-5` files), older than the epic:
    - **"Gilgamesh and Akka":** a war between Uruk and Kish; Gilgamesh frees the captured king Akka. It has no counterpart in the epic.
    - **"Gilgamesh and Huwawa":**
        - Fifty men go to the mountain.
        - Gilgamesh trades his sisters and gifts for Huwawa's seven auras, then strikes him.
        - Enkidu alone beheads Huwawa.
        - Enlil scolds them and hands out the auras, and no one is sentenced to die.
    - **"Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven":**
        - Inanna offers Gilgamesh a role in her temple, not marriage, and his mother advises him.
        - He feasts while his minstrel brings the news.
        - He kills the Bull with an axe, and the horns go to Inanna.
    - **"Gilgamesh and the Netherworld":**
        - Enkidu, here Gilgamesh's servant, goes down to fetch Gilgamesh's ball and mallet and is held there.
        - His ghost comes back up and reports how the dead fare.
        - Tablet XII translates its second half.
    - **"The Death of Gilgamesh":**
        - A deathbed dream: the gods review his life, and Enki rules that since the Flood no mortal may live forever.
        - Gilgamesh is to be a judge in the Netherworld.
        - He is buried in a tomb built in the drained bed of the Euphrates, with his household, and the river is turned back over it.
        - The surviving lines stop before the death itself.
