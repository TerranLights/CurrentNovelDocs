---

**The analysis (originally PROPOSED).** The author asked where the Doorway to Act #2, the Midpoint and the Doorway to Act #3 fall in the original epic, judged by each landmark's function rather than by percentages. The definitions come from `reference/story-structure-definitions.md`:
- **Doorway #1 / Break into Two:** the hero commits; the door closes behind him; it follows the Debate.
- **Midpoint:** a False Victory or False Defeat, or Bell's Mirror Moment ("Who am I? What have I become? What must I become?", or "I'm probably going to die"); where the B-story converges.
- **Doorway #2 / Break into Three:** the moment of clarity about what to do and who to become; it follows All Is Lost and the Dark Night.

Beat numbers refer to `03 - Every beat of the epic, in order.md`.

**These are read with Gilgamesh as the protagonist**, because the original is built around him. Enkidu's own landmarks fall elsewhere. For example, his door closes when the herd flees from him and he can no longer run with it (I.11, B66-70).

## Doorway to Act #2: candidates
1. **The vow at the assembly, sealed by the send-off (II.14 → III.10-11; B187-195, B254-262). My pick.**
   - **The Debate before it fits well.** Enkidu warns him (II.11), the senior advisers counsel against the trip (II.15), and Gilgamesh laughs it off: is he now supposed to change his mind out of fear? (B203-204).
   - **The vow locks the door.** He bolts Uruk's seven gates, takes the throne in the street and announces the expedition to the whole city (B187-195). He also promises a double New Year festival when he returns. After that, backing down would be public disgrace.
   - **The departure enacts it.** The regents entrust the king to Enkidu, and the two set out (B254-262).
   - **He names the new world himself:** "a battle I do not know, a road I do not know" (B193).
2. **Meeting his equal: the fight with Enkidu and the friendship (II.7-II.10; B142-164).**
   - **For:** this is where Gilgamesh's old world ends. He has been the unrivaled tyrant, and suddenly he isn't.
   - **Against:** it happens *to* him. There is no Debate of his own before it and no choice, so it reads better as the end of the Catalyst than as a Doorway.
3. **The edge of the Cedar Forest (V.1-V.3; B329-351).**
   - **For:** this is Campbell's threshold, with a guardian behind it, and Gilgamesh's terror at the edge (V.2) is a last chance to turn back.
   - **Against:** by then the road and its five fearful dreams have already taken them out of the ordinary world. Placing the Doorway here would leave Act 2 almost nothing but the fight.

## Midpoint: candidates
1. **The Bull of Heaven triumph, which turns that same night into the doom dream (VI.9-VI.15 → VII.1; B490-525). My pick.**
   - **The False Victory.** They kill the Bull, wash in the Euphrates and drive through Uruk while the people watch.
   - **A Mirror Moment, nearly literally.** Gilgamesh asks his palace girls, "who is the finest of men?", and the answer comes back: Gilgamesh (B512-513). It is the false self's highest point, reflected straight back at him.
   - **The turn in the same scene.** That night Enkidu dreams the gods in council deciding that one of them must die (B516-518, B519-525).
   - **Convergence.** The divine plane has run alongside the story until now (Shamash's help, Enlil's forest, Ishtar's grievance). Here it lands on the heroes.
2. **Ishtar's proposal and Gilgamesh's refusal (VI.2-VI.5; B445-467).**
   - **For:** this is a Mirror Moment about identity. In listing the fates of Ishtar's lovers, Gilgamesh defines himself as a mortal who will not become a god's plaything. It is also the point where the opposition becomes the gods themselves.
   - **Against:** the refusal doesn't yet cost him anything. The bill arrives with the doom dream, so the turn is weaker.
3. **Humbaba's death (V.15-V.20; B416-439).**
   - **For:** a False Victory with the price written into it. Humbaba curses them: neither will grow old, and only Gilgamesh will bury Enkidu (B418). Enkidu then fears Enlil's question (V.17).
   - **Against:** the story keeps rising in the same register through Tablet VI, so the real turn hasn't happened yet.
4. **Enkidu's death and Gilgamesh's realization (VII.14 → VIII.3 → IX.1; B593-622, B660-662).**
   - **For:** this is Bell's "I'm probably going to die" word for word: "shall I not die like Enkidu?" (B661). It is a False Defeat that changes the story's question from "can we win fame?" to "can I escape death?" That matches Fox's rule that the middle question must differ from the opening one.
   - **Against:** a friend's death carries every hallmark of All Is Lost, including the "whiff of death." Placing it here forces the All Is Lost later, onto the sleep test or the snake.

## Doorway to Act #3: candidates
1. **The resolve to reach Uta-napishti (IX.1; B660-662). My pick, and it pairs with Midpoint 1.**
   - **What precedes it.** All Is Lost is Enkidu's death. The Dark Night is the lament, the funeral and Gilgamesh roaming the wild in mourning (VIII-IX.1).
   - **The Self-Revelation.** Gilgamesh sees himself honestly as mortal for the first time: grief and fear have taken hold of him (B661). That is Truby's psychological self-revelation, with the facade of the two-thirds-god hero stripped away.
   - **What to do.** He now knows: reach Uta-napishti (B662).
   - **Act 3 then fits the Five-Point Finale:**
     - Gather the Team: Ur-shanabi.
     - Storm the Castle: the Waters of Death.
     - High-Tower Surprise: Uta-napishti turns out to be "just like me" (B773), and the sleep test fails.
     - Dig Down Deep: the dive for the plant.
     - Executing the New Plan: the plan fails, and the lesson lands at Uruk's wall.
   - **Weakness.** The clarity is only half true: he knows what to do but not yet who to become. That suits a tragic structure, in which the hero's Act 3 plan has to fail before the truth arrives.
2. **The plant, after the sleep test (XI.10 → XI.12-XI.14; B829-856). This pairs with Midpoint 4.**
   - **What precedes it.** All Is Lost is "Death lives in my bedroom; wherever I turn, Death will be waiting" (B838). The Dark Night is brief: he is washed and robed for the journey home (XI.11).
   - **The turn.** The wife's intercession brings the secret of the plant, and he acts decisively by diving for it (B850-856).
   - **Weakness.** In George's text he plans to test the plant on an old man and then eat it himself (B855-856). That is clarity of plan with no change in who he is. It reads more like Weiland's Later False Peak than a Doorway.
3. **The wall of Uruk, after the snake (XI.15-XI.17; B857-867).**
   - **What precedes it.** All Is Lost is the snake taking the plant. The Dark Night is Gilgamesh weeping: he has won nothing for himself, only done a good turn to "the Lion of the Earth" (B861-863).
   - **The turn.** The clarity is shown, not told: he shows Ur-shanabi the walls (B866-867).
   - **Weakness.** Doorway, finale and Closing Image all collapse into the last few lines. Even so, this is the reading that best matches the author's wish for an ambiguous ending (Author Statements, 2026-10-25), because the epic never says what Gilgamesh understood.
4. **The crossing of the Waters of Death (X.5-X.7; B726-745).**
   - **For:** this is the most literal "doorway of no return" in the poem. He smashes the Stone Ones, his easy way across, cuts the punting-poles and crosses using his own garment as a sail.
   - **Against:** it has no clarity, so it is better as a Finale step (Storm the Castle).
