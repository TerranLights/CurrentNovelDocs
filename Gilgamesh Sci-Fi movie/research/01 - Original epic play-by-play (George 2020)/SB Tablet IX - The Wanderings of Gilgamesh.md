# Tablet IX. The Wanderings of Gilgamesh: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

## George's synopsis (p. 68)
Enkidu's death has made Gilgamesh face the fact that he, too, must die. Still mourning, he quits Uruk and roams far and wide after Uta-napishti, the one man who escaped death, hoping to learn how it was done. At the rim of the world stand the twin mountains of sunrise and sunset, and a scorpion-man guards the passage beneath them. The guard warns him of the risk, fails to turn him back, and lets him in. Gilgamesh must get through the Sun's own road before the Sun comes up behind him; he makes it out with no time to spare and steps into a garden whose trees are made of gems.

## Scenes
Key: "restored" means the words are in George's square brackets; "conjectural" means his italic square brackets (his own guess to fill the context); "uncertain" means his italics on surviving text (insecure reading or rendering). Line numbers are George's margin numbers (printed every five lines); numbers in between are counted from the nearest marker, and "about" marks a count that cannot be fixed exactly (lost lines, or one verse printed as two lines or two as one).

### IX.1 Lament in the wild, and the night at the mountain pass (IX 1-12; pp. 68-69)
- **Where and when:** open country, some time after Enkidu's funeral (Tablet VIII). No day or hour is given for the lament. Inside his speech, Gilgamesh recalls reaching a pass in the mountains after dark.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, alone. He speaks, but no listener is named: it is a lament voiced while he roams.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narration: Gilgamesh roams the open country, weeping hard over Enkidu (IX 1-2).
  2. Gilgamesh → (no listener): he asks himself whether death will make him the same as Enkidu, and says grief has taken hold of him (IX 3-4).
  3. Gilgamesh: fear of dying is what drives him through the wilds; he means to reach Uta-napishti, whose father was Ubar-Tutu (IX 5-6).
  4. *Retold inside the speech:* he was traveling fast and arrived at a mountain pass at night (IX 7-8).
  5. *Retold:* there were lions, and he was frightened (IX 9).
  6. *Retold prayer:* he looked up to the moon and prayed; his pleas went to Ishtar (her name restored), whom he calls the gods' lamp, and he asked the Moon God and Ishtar (both names restored) to protect him (IX 10-12). The quoted speech ends at IX 12; IX 13 returns to third-person narration.
- **Specifics:** Uta-napishti's father: Ubar-Tutu (IX 6). Time: night. Place: a mountain pass. Animals: lions, number not stated. The moon. Ishtar's title: lamp of the gods (IX 11). Sîn is the Moon God (Glossary, p. 224).
- **Short quotation:** "I, too—shall I not die like Enkidu also?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 9, p. 41).
- **Gaps:** in IX 11, Ishtar's name and the article before "lamp" are restored, and "lamp" itself is an uncertain reading. In IX 12, the opening call to Sîn and Ishtar is restored; only the plea for safety is plain text. So neither god's name in the prayer actually survives.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the lions and the prayer as a plan, not a past event: if he comes to the mountain gates in the dark and meets lions, he will lift his head and pray to the Moon-god (Tablet 9, pp. 41-42).
- **Repetitions:** his question about ending up like Enkidu (IX 3) returns in each telling of the Tablet X lament, where he asks whether he too must lie down like his friend (X 70, 147, 247). Fear of death as the reason for his wandering is also part of that lament (X 62, 139, 239).

### IX.2 The dream and the fight with the lions (IX 13-18; p. 69)
- **Where and when:** the same pass, the same night (the words for "that night" are restored), then after he wakes. The moon is mentioned.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and the lions. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. He lies down and later wakes from a dream (IX 13). *Dream:* what he dreamed does not survive.
  2. After a lost word or two, he takes heart in the moon's presence and is glad to be alive (IX 14).
  3. He grips his axe and pulls his sword (the word restored) from his belt (IX 15-16).
  4. He drops among the lions (the word restored) like a shot arrow, striking, killing and scattering them (IX 17-18).
- **Specifics:** axe; sword (restored); belt; arrow simile; the moon.
- **Short quotation:** "Lance-like leapt he amongst them" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 9, p. 42).
- **Gaps:** IX 13 opens with restored words. IX 14, the line right after the dream, begins with a loss. In IX 16 the sword is restored, and in IX 18 the lions. Then the tablet breaks: IX 19-37 (about 19 lines) are missing; see IX.3.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the line after the dream as something seen in it, "[Saw he] . . . which were rejoicing in life," not Gilgamesh himself growing glad (Tablet 9, p. 42).
- **Repetitions:** the two-line arming formula (axe taken in hand, sword drawn from belt; IX 15-16) recurs before the attack on the Stone Ones (X 93-94) and before the cutting of the poles (X 164-165). The arrow simile (IX 17) recurs in the Stone Ones attack (X 96).

### IX.3 Gap filled from the Old Babylonian Sippar tablet: lion skins, wells, and Shamash (IX 19-37 lost; text from Sippar tablet column i 2′-15′; p. 69)
- **What this passage is:** George fills part of the gap with lines from an Old Babylonian tablet said to come from Sippar (his note, p. 69). It is not standard-version text, and the margin numbers here are the Sippar tablet's own (column i, lines 2′-15′). In chapter 3 George dates the tablet to the eighteenth or seventeenth century BC: a library tablet bought in Baghdad in 1902, reportedly from Sippar (p. 192). He prints the same lines there on p. 193. Thompson 1928 also places this Old Babylonian passage at the end of his Tablet 9 (p. 43), with a different reading of its opening.
- **Where and when:** the wilds. No time is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; Shamash, the Sun God. Both speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. He wears their hides and eats their meat (Si i 2′). "Their" points back to lines now lost; placed by George after the lion fight, it reads as the lions' ⚠️.
  2. He digs (conjectural) wells where none had been before, and drinks from them while he chases the winds (Si i 3′-4′).
  3. Shamash is troubled; bending down (uncertain), he addresses Gilgamesh (Si i 5′-6′).
  4. Shamash → Gilgamesh: he asks where all this wandering is leading and tells him that the life he is after is beyond his reach (Si i 7′-8′).
  5. Gilgamesh → Shamash (whom he calls a hero): after so much roaming, will he find too little rest in the Netherworld? He will be asleep there for years without end (Si i 9′-12′).
  6. Gilgamesh → Shamash: he wants his eyes to look on the sun and take their fill of light. If the darkness is "hidden" (uncertain), how much light is left? When will the dead ever see sunlight? (Si i 13′-15′).
- **Specifics:** hides worn and meat eaten (by context, the lions'); new wells; chasing winds; Shamash's title, hero; the Netherworld; sun, light, darkness, sunbeams.
- **Short quotation:** "the life which thou seekest / Thou shalt not find" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 9, p. 43).
- **Gaps:** the standard version's IX 19-37 (about 19 lines) is still lost; the Old Babylonian lines fill only part of it. The Sippar column's first line is gone (p. 193). Si i 3′: the verb for digging is conjectural. Si i 4′: the pronoun is conjectural and the verb for drinking uncertain. Si i 5′: the bending-down phrase is uncertain. Si i 10′: the word for roaming is uncertain. Si i 14′: "hidden" is uncertain.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the opening lines as part of a speech: someone of the wild has put on their pelts and eats their flesh, and there will never be a crossing where none has been, "so long as the gale driveth water." George instead has new wells dug while chasing the winds (Tablet 9, p. 43).
- **Repetitions:** later on the same Old Babylonian tablet, the tavern-keeper opens with the same question and warning that Shamash gives here (Si iii 1-2, p. 194). George says her longer lecture there has no place in the standard Tablet X (p. xlv).

### IX.4 Mashu's twin mountains and the scorpion-men (IX 38-59; pp. 70-71)
- **Where and when:** at the gate of Mashu's twin mountains. George's note marks the point where Tablet IX's own text picks up again (p. 70). No hour or travel time is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; the scorpion-men guarding the gate; one scorpion-man and his mate speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narration: Gilgamesh reaches Mashu, a pair of mountains that keep watch every day over the sun's rising (the word "sun" restored). Their peaks hold up (verb restored) the structure of the sky, and their roots go down to the Netherworld (IX 38-41).
  2. Scorpion-men guard the gate. They inspire dread, one look from them kills, and their glow is so terrible it overpowers the mountains; they watch over the sun as it rises and as it sets (IX 42-45).
  3. At the sight of them Gilgamesh hides his face in terror. Then he steadies himself and goes closer (IX 46-47).
  4. Scorpion-man → his mate: the newcomer's body is made of divine flesh (IX 48-49).
  5. Mate → scorpion-man: he is two parts god and one part man (IX 50-51).
  6. Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh (addressed as king and as divine flesh; restored): he asks how Gilgamesh managed so long a road, how he got into his presence, and how he crossed rivers that are dangerous to cross. He wants to hear about the journey and where Gilgamesh is heading (IX 52-59).
- **Specifics:** Mashu means "Twin Peaks" (Glossary p. 223). Two mountains; peaks that support heaven; base in the Netherworld; a gate; scorpion-men (male) and one scorpion-man's female mate; a deadly glance; overpowering radiance; on guard at sunrise and sunset; Gilgamesh addressed as king and divine flesh (IX 53); dangerous rivers (IX 56). Figure 10 (p. 70): scorpion-men on a cylinder-seal impression, early first millennium BC, Assyria (List of Figures), captioned with IX 45.
- **Short quotation:** "Two parts of him god-(like), / (Only) a third of him human." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 9, p. 42).
- **Gaps:** IX 39 (the sun) and IX 40 (the verb for holding up the sky) are restored. In IX 53 the address to King Gilgamesh is restored. In IX 54-56 the "how did you…" opening of each of the three questions is conjectural; the plain text is only the long road, being in his presence, and the dangerous crossing. IX 57-59 lose their openings; "journey" and "face" are conjectural and "where" is uncertain. A lacuna follows: IX 60-74 (about 15 lines).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** a stronger reaction: Gilgamesh turns pale with alarm and grovels senseless before them (Tablet 9, p. 42). George has him cover his face and then pull himself together.
- **Repetitions:** the scorpion-man asks twice to hear of the journey (IX 57, 59), and Shiduri makes the same request (X 28). The divine-flesh description recurs at IX 130 (restored) and X 7.

### IX.5 Gilgamesh states his quest; the scorpion-man warns him (about IX 60-90; p. 71)
- **Where and when:** the gate of Mashu, the same encounter after a break. George's note says that when the text returns, Gilgamesh is in the middle of explaining his quest (p. 71).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and the scorpion-man; both speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [IX 60-74, about 15 lines: lost.]
  2. Gilgamesh → scorpion-man: he is looking (conjectural) for the way (conjectural) to his ancestor Uta-napishti, who took part in the gods' assembly and gained (restored) everlasting life. He expects Uta-napishti to reveal to him (conjectural) the truth about death and life (IX 75-77).
  3. Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh: no one like him has ever come before (restored), and no one has ever made the mountain journey (conjectural) (IX 78-81).
  4. Scorpion-man: the way through the mountain lasts (conjectural) twelve double-hours, all of it in thick darkness, without light (IX 82-83).
  5. Scorpion-man, very broken: phrases about the sun's rising, the sun's setting, another "setting," and someone being sent out (about IX 84-87).
  6. Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh: broken questions about how he will manage and whether he will go in (about IX 88-90). The four printed lines after the marker IX 85 stand for five line numbers, so exact numbers in IX 85-90 cannot be fixed.
- **Specifics:** Uta-napishti as Gilgamesh's ancestor (IX 75); the gods' assembly (IX 76); death and life (IX 77); twelve double-hours (IX 82); total darkness; sunrise; sunset.
- **Short quotation:** none. Thompson 1928 gives this stretch only as a prose summary (Tablet 9, p. 42).
- **Gaps:** IX 60-74 lost. IX 75: the verb of seeking and the word for road are conjectural. IX 76: the gaining of eternal life is restored. IX 77: the whole clause about being told the secret is conjectural, so "secret" is George's word, not the tablet's. IX 80: "before" and the "one like you" phrase are restored. IX 81: the travel phrase is conjectural. IX 82: the verb is conjectural. IX 83: "light" is restored. IX 84-90 are mostly lost, with full-line breaks after each opening. A long lacuna follows: IX 91-124 (about 34 lines).
- **Thompson 1928 summarizes** the lost part: the way is the Road of the Sun, a journey of twenty-four hours that begins in deep darkness, and the Scorpion-man seems to describe it hour by hour (Tablet 9, p. 42).
- **Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's picture of Uta-napishti as the man who sat in the gods' assembly and found life (IX 76) comes back in his question to Uta-napishti at XI 7. The darkness-without-light line (IX 83) becomes the refrain of IX.7.

### IX.6 The end of Gilgamesh's reply; the scorpion-man lets him pass (IX 125-135; pp. 71-72)
- **Where and when:** the gate of Mashu, the same conversation after a long break. George's note says that what follows is the close of a reply by Gilgamesh (p. 71).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and the scorpion-man; both speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [IX 91-124, about 34 lines: lost. George's note says only that the text picks up at the end of Gilgamesh's reply.]
  2. Gilgamesh → scorpion-man, end of the reply: broken lines that mention sorrow, his face burned (restored) by cold and sun, exhaustion, and a closing "now you…" (IX 125-128).
  3. Scorpion-man → Gilgamesh (the address restored): he tells him to go. He wishes that Mashu's mountains let him through, that the mountains and hills guard his way, that they help him on in safety, and that the mountain gate open for him; every one of these verbs is conjectural (IX 129-135).
- **Specifics:** sorrow; cold and sun; exhaustion; Mashu's mountains; mountains and hills; the mountain gate.
- **Short quotation:** none. Thompson 1928 again summarizes in prose (Tablet 9, pp. 42-43).
- **Gaps:** IX 125-128 each break off after a word or two; the burned-face clause (IX 126) is restored. The speech-introduction lines IX 129-130 are restored. In the blessing every verb phrase is conjectural; what survives is the command to go, the names of the mountains, the words for "in safety," and the gate.
- **Thompson 1928 summarizes** the reply as Gilgamesh accepting the trial "[even though it be] in pain," his face weathered by cold and heat, and the Scorpion-man sending him off with a last word about Mashu and good wishes (Tablet 9, pp. 42-43).
- **Repetitions:** the weathered face (IX 126) matches the Tablet X question about a face burned by cold and sun, put by Shiduri, Ur-shanabi and Uta-napishti (X 44, 117, 217) and answered by Gilgamesh each time (X 51, 124, 224).

### IX.7 The Path of the Sun: twelve double-hours in the dark (IX 136-170; pp. 72-73)
- **Where and when:** inside the mountain, on the Sun God's road, right after the blessing. The journey is measured in double-hours, from one to twelve.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, alone. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Having heard (conjectural) the scorpion-man and taken his words to heart (restored), Gilgamesh sets out (verb restored) on the Sun God's road (IX 136-138).
  2. First double-hour (the rest of the line lost): thick darkness, no light (restored), and he cannot see what is behind him (restored) (IX 139-141).
  3. The same at the second (IX 142-144), third (IX 145-147), fourth (IX 148-150) and fifth (IX 151-153) double-hours.
  4. On reaching (conjectural) the sixth, and on reaching the seventh: the same again (IX 154-159).
  5. At the eighth he starts to hurry (rest lost); still dark, still no view behind (IX 160-162).
  6. At the ninth, something (lost) about the north wind and something (lost) about his face; then the darkness refrain (restored) (IX 163-166).
  7. On reaching the tenth (the number restored), something (lost) is close by (IX 167-168).
  8. At the eleventh (largely conjectural), only one double-hour of road is left (IX 169).
  9. At the twelfth (restored), he comes out before the Sun does (IX 170).
- **Specifics:** the Sun God's road (IX 138); twelve stations, one per double-hour; darkness; no light; no view behind; hurrying from the eighth; north wind and his face at the ninth; something near at the tenth; one double-hour left at the eleventh; out ahead of the Sun at the twelfth.
- **Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928 summarizes this passage in prose (see below).
- **Gaps:** each station's first line breaks off after the number. The refrain is often restored: at stations one and two its second half, at three both refrain lines entirely, at four the noun "darkness" and everything after it. At six, "On" is uncertain and "reaching" conjectural. Station nine is broken around the wind and face words. At ten the number itself is restored. At eleven the opening and the "journey remained" phrase are conjectural, the number is restored, and only the words for "one double-hour" are plain text. At twelve the opening with the number and his name is restored; his coming out ahead of the Sun is plain text.
- **Thompson 1928 summarizes:** each two-hour stage is dark until the eighth is passed; by the ninth he seems to meet "the first glimmer of light," and at the twelfth he reaches "the full blaze of the sun" (Tablet 9, p. 43).
- **Repetitions:** a three-line pattern for each station (the number; darkness with no light; no view behind) runs from station one to seven. Stations eight and nine add a new detail before the refrain. Stations ten to twelve drop the refrain and get shorter: two lines for ten, one each for eleven and twelve. The refrain picks up the scorpion-man's warning at IX 83.

### IX.8 The garden of jewel-trees (IX 171-196; p. 73)
- **Where and when:** just outside the tunnel's far end, the moment he comes out. George's synopsis speaks of a garden of gems (p. 68); his introduction calls it the Garden of Jewels, at the far end of the Path of the Sun and close to Shiduri's tavern (pp. xxxiii-xxxiv). The surviving lines call the trees the gods' trees.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; at the end, an unnamed woman watching him. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. After a loss: there is radiance. The moment he sees the gods' trees, he heads straight for them (IX 171-172).
  2. One tree, of carnelian, carries fruit in clusters of grapes, beautiful to see (IX 173-174).
  3. Another, of lapis lazuli, is in leaf and heavy with fruit, splendid to look at (IX 175-176).
  4. [Lacuna, marked with asterisks: about IX 177-183.]
  5. Fragments of a catalogue: a cypress (uncertain); a cedar; leaf-stalks of pappardilû-stone; sea coral (uncertain) and some gemstone; crystal vials (uncertain) growing (conjectural) in place of thorns and briars (IX 184-188).
  6. He touches (uncertain) a carob: it is (conjectural) abashmu-stone. Agate and haematite follow, and the rest is lost (IX 189-190).
  7. [Lacuna, marked with asterisks: about IX 191-194.]
  8. As Gilgamesh strolls about (in wonder, conjectural), a woman raises her head (restored) to watch him (IX 195-196). She is not named here. Tablet X opens with Shiduri watching him from far off (X 1-10), and George's synopsis of Tablet X has the goddess catch sight of him ⚠️.
- **Specifics:** radiance; the gods' trees; a carnelian tree with grape clusters; a lapis lazuli tree with leaves and fruit; cypress; cedar; leaf-stalks of pappardilû-stone; sea coral; gemstone; crystal instead of thorns and briars; a carob of abashmu-stone; agate; haematite.
- **Short quotation:** "Bearing its fruit (all) ruby, and hung about with (its) tendrils." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 9, p. 43).
- **Gaps:** IX 171-172 open with losses. Two lacunae: about IX 177-183 and about 191-194. In IX 184-190, uncertain: cypress, coral, vials, the verb of touching; conjectural: the verb of growing and "it was"; pappardilû and abashmu are printed in italics as untranslated Akkadian words. IX 195 "in wonder" is conjectural; in IX 196 "her head" is restored. The tablet ends at about IX 196 (last printed marker IX 195).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the first tree's fruit as ruby, not carnelian, and its foliage as azure; he speaks of a single Tree of the Gods and suggests, as his own guess, that it may be the vine from which Siduri makes her wine (Tablet 9, p. 43).
- **Repetitions:** none in what survives.

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ The standard text is lost at IX 19-37, IX 60-74, IX 91-124, about IX 177-183 and about IX 191-194. Only the first gap is partly filled, and that with older Old Babylonian text from the Sippar tablet, not the standard version (IX.3).
- ⚠️ The dream at IX 13 has no surviving content (IX.2).
- ⚠️ Both gods' names in the prayer (IX 11-12) are restorations, and "lamp" is an uncertain reading.
- ⚠️ The hides and meat of Si i 2′ have no surviving owner; they follow the lion fight only because George places the Sippar text there.
- ⚠️ The word "secret" in Gilgamesh's statement of his quest (IX 77) is conjectural.
- ⚠️ The scorpion-man's account of the sun's rising and setting inside the mountain (IX 84-90) is almost all lost, as is IX 91-124.
- ⚠️ Every verb in the scorpion-man's blessing (IX 132-135) is conjectural.
- ⚠️ The number at the tenth station (IX 167) and most of the eleventh (IX 169) are restored or conjectural.
- ⚠️ The woman of IX 196 is not named in the surviving lines; calling her Shiduri rests on Tablet X and on George's summaries.
- ⚠️ Tablet IX's last preserved line is about IX 196, and these pages do not say how many lines followed. George gives the range of tablet lengths as 183 to 326 lines (p. xxx).
- Several garden words are uncertain readings (cypress, coral, vials, the verb of touching), and two stone names are left untranslated (pappardilû, abashmu).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Gilgamesh prays to Sin before he sleeps and wakes from an encouraging dream; George 2020 has him pray when he sees the lions (IX 9-12), and the dream's content is lost; only his gladness in the moon's presence after waking survives (IX 13-14).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says he kills the lions and dresses in their skins; George 2020 has the killing in the standard text (IX 17-18) but the skins only from the Old Babylonian Sippar lines that fill the gap (Si i 2′).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) places Mashu at the western end of the earth; George 2020's text says the mountains guard the sun's rising every day (IX 39), the scorpion-men guard it at both sunrise and sunset (IX 45), and his synopsis places the Sun's setting and rising at the same mountains (p. 68). - Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the husband tries to turn Gilgamesh back and the wife, in sympathy, steps in and (following Benjamin Foster) lets him pass; George 2020 gives the mate only the two-parts-god line (IX 50-51), and it is the scorpion-man himself who blesses Gilgamesh and sends him on (IX 129-135).

## Leads (observations on the text's own structure only)
- Lead: the lion episode uses the same arming and attack formulas (axe in hand, sword from belt, the arrow simile) that return in Tablet X for the attack on the Stone Ones and the cutting of the poles.
- Lead: the twelve-station countdown in IX.7 is the tablet's longest repeated pattern; it takes up the scorpion-man's warning in IX.5 about twelve double-hours of darkness.
- Lead: the scorpion-man and his mate supply the divine-flesh description (IX 49, 53, 130) and the two-parts-god line (IX 51); the divine-flesh description returns in the narration of Tablet X (X 7).
- Lead: the scorpion-man's questions about the long road and his wish to hear of the journey (IX 54-59) are the first in a chain of challenges to the traveler that continues with Shiduri, Ur-shanabi and Uta-napishti in Tablet X.
