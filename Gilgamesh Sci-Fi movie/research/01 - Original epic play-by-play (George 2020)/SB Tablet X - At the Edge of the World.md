# Tablet X. At the Edge of the World: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

## George's synopsis (p. 74)
Past the garden, on the shore, lives an old and wise goddess. Seeing a menacing stranger far off, she takes him for a hunter and locks her tavern door; at the sound Gilgamesh threatens to force his way in. She asks his name and business. He tells her about his friend's death and his own terror of dying, and wants help getting across the sea to Uta-napishti. She tells him the search is pointless and the Waters of Death deadly, but in the end says where the ferryman Ur-shanabi and his crew, the Stone Ones, can be found. Gilgamesh charges at them. Once the fighting stops, he explains himself to Ur-shanabi and asks for help. Ur-shanabi points out that by smashing the Stone Ones Gilgamesh has made his own crossing harder, and has him cut enormously long punting-poles to drive the boat instead. They set out, pushing with the poles; once every pole is spent, Gilgamesh rigs the ferryman's garment as a sail, and they get across the Waters of Death. On landing, Gilgamesh tells Uta-napishti his story. Uta-napishti reminds him what a king owes and talks about death that no one escapes and life that does not last.

## Scenes
Key: "restored" means the words are in George's square brackets; "conjectural" means his italic square brackets (his own guess to fill the context); "uncertain" means his italics on surviving text (insecure reading or rendering). Line numbers are George's margin numbers (printed every five lines); numbers in between are counted from the nearest marker, and "about" marks a count that cannot be fixed exactly (lost lines, or one verse printed as two lines or two as one).

### X.1 Shiduri sees Gilgamesh coming and bars her gate (X 1-16; p. 74)
- **Where and when:** Shiduri's tavern on the seashore. No hour is given; it follows Gilgamesh's walk in the jewel garden (IX.8), with no interval stated.
- **Who:** Shiduri, the tavern-keeper; Gilgamesh, still far off. Shiduri speaks, to herself.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narration: Shiduri keeps a tavern on the seashore (the second line, about where she lives, is conjectural). She owns potstands and vats (of gold, conjectural) and is wrapped in hoods and veils ("veiled with" conjectural; "veils" uncertain) (X 1-4).
  2. Gilgamesh comes roaming (the rest of the line lost), dressed in a pelt and frightening (conjectural) to see. He has the gods' flesh in his body (conjectural) but grief in his heart (restored), and his face is that of a traveler from far away (X 5-9).
  3. The tavern-keeper watches him from a distance and, talking to herself, thinks it over: this must be a hunter of wild bulls; where can he be from, heading straight for her gate? (X 10-14).
  4. She bars the gate, bars it again, and climbs up onto the roof (X 15-16).
- **Specifics:** her title, tavern-keeper; the seashore; potstands; vats (of gold, conjectural); hoods; veils; Gilgamesh in a pelt; the gods' flesh; a far traveler's face; a wild-bull hunter; her gate; the roof. Glossary: Shiduri means "She is my Rampart," a wise goddess who kept an alehouse at the world's rim (p. 224). The appendix gives the Akkadian of X 10, where her title is _sābītum_ (pp. 218-219).
- **Short quotation:** "Look'd in the distance the maker of wine, (and) a word in her bosom / Quoth she, in thought with herself" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 44).
- **Gaps:** conjectural: X 2's place phrase, X 3's gold vats, X 6's "to look on," X 7's "his body," and X 4's "veiled with" (with "veils" uncertain). Restored: X 8's "his heart." X 5 ends in a loss. George's appendix uses X 10-16 as a worked example of rebuilding a passage from two British Museum manuscripts, MSS K and f (pp. 211, 213), and notes that her slow pondering (X 12-14) gives way to a quicker rhythm as she hurries (p. 220).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** her guess differently: she takes him for "one who would ravish (?) [a woman]," not a wild-bull hunter. He calls her "the maker of wine" and has her bar her postern, her inner door and her chamber (Tablet 10, p. 44).
- **Repetitions:** the watcher formula (watching from afar, talking to oneself, thinking it over; X 10-12) is used again for Uta-napishti watching the boat (X 184-186). The far traveler's face (X 9) is part of the question put three times later in the tablet (X 43, 116, 216).

### X.2 Gilgamesh threatens to break down the door (X 17-22; p. 75)
- **Where and when:** below the tavern, with Shiduri on the roof. Straight after X.1.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks; Shiduri listens.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh hears something (the object is lost; George's synopsis says he hears the noise, p. 74). He lifts his chin and turns (uncertain) toward her (conjectural) (X 17-18).
  2. Gilgamesh → Shiduri: he asks why she barred the gate the moment she saw him, points out that she barred it and climbed to the roof, and threatens to break down the door and smash the bolts (restored) (X 19-22).
- **Specifics:** gate; roof; door; bolts (restored); his chin raised.
- **Short quotation:** "O, I'll smite [thy] portal" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 44).
- **Gaps:** X 17 ends in a loss; in X 18 "turned" is uncertain and the direction conjectural; in X 20 the verb of barring, the gate and "me" are restored; X 21's middle phrase is restored; X 22's bolts are restored. A lacuna follows (asterisks): about X 23-24.
- **Thompson 1928 restores** what he hears as "the sound [of her shutting(?)]" (Tablet 10, p. 44).
- **Repetitions:** Gilgamesh throws the narrator's words about barring the gate and climbing to the roof (X 15-16) back at her (X 21), and she repeats them in her answer (X 26-27).

### X.3 Shiduri questions him; his deeds; his lament for Enkidu, first telling (X 25-71; pp. 75-76)
- **Where and when:** the tavern gate, after a short break (about X 23-24). The text does not say whether Shiduri comes down from the roof ⚠️.
- **Who:** Shiduri and Gilgamesh; both speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Shiduri → Gilgamesh: broken lines repeating that she barred the gate and climbed to the roof; her reason is lost. She asks to hear about his journey (conjectural) (X 25-28).
  2. Gilgamesh → Shiduri (almost all restored): he and Enkidu together climbed the mountains, caught the Bull of Heaven and slew it, wiped out Humbaba of the Cedar Forest, and slew lions in the passes (X 29-34).
  3. Shiduri → Gilgamesh: if (restored) he and Enkidu (conjectural) are the ones who killed the Guardian, destroyed Humbaba of the Cedar Forest, killed lions in the passes and slew the Bull that came down from heaven, why does he look as he does? She lists it all: hollow cheeks, a sunken face, a wretched mood, wasted looks, grief in his heart, the face of a far traveler, skin burned by cold and sun, and roaming the wild dressed as a lion (X 35-45).
  4. Gilgamesh → Shiduri (this whole answer restored): he turns each point back on her as a question: why should he not look like this? (X 46-52).
  5. Gilgamesh, lament (restored): Enkidu, his friend, was a runaway wild ass, an upland donkey, a panther of the open country; Gilgamesh loved him dearly, and they faced every danger together; the fate of mortals (conjectural) caught up with him (X 53-57).
  6. Gilgamesh, retold (restored): he wept over him for six days and seven nights and kept the body from burial until a maggot fell from its nose (X 58-60).
  7. Gilgamesh: then he grew afraid (conjectural: that he would die too) and, dreading death, took to the wild. His friend's fate was more than he could bear (conjectural), so he roams the wild on a far road, a far path (X 61-66).
  8. Gilgamesh: how can he stay silent or keep quiet? The friend he loved has become clay. Must he not lie down the same way and never get up again, for all time? (X 67-71).
- **Specifics:** the deeds: the mountains climbed; the Bull of Heaven; Humbaba of the Cedar Forest; lions in the mountain passes. Shiduri adds "the Guardian" as a separate item and describes the Bull as having come down from heaven (X 36, 39). Enkidu's epithets: wild ass, upland donkey, panther. Six days and seven nights (restored); a maggot from the nostril (restored); dressed as a lion; turned to clay; for all eternity.
- **Short quotation:** "Comrade (and) henchman, who chased the wild ass, the pard of the desert" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 45).
- **Gaps:** Shiduri's reason (X 26-28) is lost. The list of deeds (X 30-34) is restored except the words for the mountains, the Cedar Forest and the lions. In her reply, the words naming him and Enkidu (X 36) are conjectural. Gilgamesh's answer and the first half of the lament (X 47-62) are wholly restored; George's note on conventions says long breaks can sometimes be filled securely from parallel passages (p. lvi), and these lines survive better in the later tellings (X 119-148, 219-248). In all three tellings the "doom of mortals" phrase, the clause about his own death and the "too much" phrase are conjectural, and the maggot line is restored. That line is plain text only on the Old Babylonian Sippar tablet (chapter 3).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the mourning as six days, "a se'nnight," until he could consign the body to burial, with no maggot in the Assyrian lines; in the Old Babylonian lines he prints from the Berlin tablet, Enkidu "like a worm hath lain on his face" (Tablet 10, p. 45).
- **Repetitions:** the question (X 40-45) is put again, almost word for word, by Ur-shanabi (X 113-118) and by Uta-napishti (X 213-218). Gilgamesh's answer and lament (X 46-71) are repeated to Ur-shanabi (X 119-148) and to Uta-napishti (X 219-248). One difference: here the deeds come before the question (X 30-39) and are not repeated inside the lament; in the other two tellings they sit inside the lament, after the wild-ass lines (X 128-131, 228-231). Shiduri lists the deeds in a different order from Gilgamesh (Guardian, Humbaba, lions, Bull, against mountains, Bull, Humbaba, lions).

### X.4 The road to Uta-napishti: Shiduri's warning and directions (X 72-91; pp. 76-77)
- **Where and when:** the tavern, the same conversation.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Shiduri; both speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Shiduri: he asks which road leads to Uta-napishti and what marks it, pressing her twice for the landmark. If the crossing can be made, he will cross the ocean; if not, he will keep roaming the wild (X 72-77).
  2. Shiduri → Gilgamesh: there has never been a way over, and since ancient times no one has crossed the ocean. Only the hero Shamash crosses it; besides the Sun God, who does? (X 78-82).
  3. Shiduri: the crossing is dangerous and the route full of hazards, and halfway across the Waters of Death bar the way. Even if he gets over the ocean, what will he do at the Waters of Death? (X 83-86).
  4. Shiduri: there is Ur-shanabi, Uta-napishti's boatman, with the Stone Ones, out in the forest where he "strips a pine" (uncertain). Gilgamesh should go and show himself to him; if it can be done, he should cross with him, and if not, he should turn back (X 87-91).
- **Specifics:** the road to Uta-napishti; its landmark; the ocean; ancient times; the hero Shamash, the only one who crosses; the Waters of Death, halfway; Ur-shanabi, Uta-napishti's boatman; the Stone Ones; a pine; the forest.
- **Short quotation:** "Warrior Shamash doth cross it, 'tis true, but who besides Shamash / Maketh the traverse?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 46).
- **Gaps:** nearly complete. X 88 "strips a pine" is uncertain; a short phrase in X 90 is restored.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "sails (?)" and a word _urnu_, which Ur-shanabi plucks in the forest, where George has the Stone Ones and the pine; his footnote calls the word one of the epic's greatest philological problems, perhaps "paddles," and doubts any link with "stones" (Tablet 10, p. 46 and n. 3). Before this request Thompson also prints, from the Old Babylonian tablet, the tavern-keeper's longer advice (eat well, be merry day and night, wear clean clothes, wash, cherish child and wife; p. 46), which George says the standard version leaves out (p. xlv).
- **Repetitions:** the request (X 73-77) is repeated almost word for word to Ur-shanabi (X 150-154). Shiduri turns his "if it can be done / if not" formula back on him (X 90-91), but ends with "turn back" where he said he would roam the wild.

### X.5 Gilgamesh attacks Ur-shanabi and smashes the Stone Ones (X 92-108; p. 77)
- **Where and when:** the forest, then the water and the bank. It follows straight on from Shiduri's directions.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Ur-shanabi, the Stone Ones. No one speaks; Gilgamesh shouts.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Hearing her, Gilgamesh grabs his axe, draws his sword from his belt (the belt restored), creeps forward and charges down on them (restored) (X 92-95).
  2. He drops among them like an arrow; his shout rings through the forest (X 96-97).
  3. Ur-shanabi sees him and puts on his aura; he takes up an axe and trembles before him (X 98-99).
  4. Gilgamesh hits his head, seizes his hair and pins his arm against his chest (X 100-101). Only pronouns are used; by context the head, hair and arm are Ur-shanabi's ⚠️.
  5. The Stone Ones had closed up the boat; the Waters of Death did not frighten them (X 102-103).
  6. Gilgamesh wades (verb uncertain) into the ocean, the broad sea, and brings the boat, with the Stone Ones aboard, to a halt in the water (X 104-105).
  7. He smashes the Stone Ones and throws them into the river, makes the boat fast with a rope, and (after a lost word) sits down on the bank (X 106-108).
- **Specifics:** axe; sword; belt; a shout; Ur-shanabi's aura; Ur-shanabi's axe; the boat, closed up by the Stone Ones; the ocean, the broad sea; the river; a rope; the bank. The water is called both the Ocean (X 104) and the river (X 106).
- **Short quotation:** "[taketh] (his) axe in his [hand], awhile he draweth Glaive from his baldric (?)" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 47).
- **Gaps:** X 94 "from" and "belt" and X 95 "them" are restored; X 104's verb is uncertain; X 108 begins with a loss, so who sits down is not stated ⚠️. George's preface says new pieces in the second edition filled out the passage where the Stone Ones are smashed (p. xii).
- **Thompson 1928 has** almost none of this fight: he says the rest of the column is too damaged and that Gilgamesh meets Ur-Shanabi and, for some reason, destroys the boat's "sails (?)" (Tablet 10, p. 47).
- **Repetitions:** the arming formula (X 93-94) and the arrow simile (X 96) repeat the lion fight (IX 15-17). Ur-shanabi's trembling with his axe (X 99) is thrown back at him by Gilgamesh in X.6 (X 110).

### X.6 Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi: second telling, and the order to cut punting-poles (X 109-162; pp. 78-79)
- **Where and when:** the bank, right after the fight.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi; both speak. The Stone Ones are already smashed.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi: he mocks him for raising an axe while trembling, and says he will not fight the battle Ur-shanabi was looking for (X 109-111).
  2. Ur-shanabi → Gilgamesh: the question formula, second time: hollow cheeks … dressed as a lion (X 112-118). Unlike Shiduri, he does not begin with the deeds.
  3. Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi: the answer and lament, second telling: why should he not look so; his friend the wild ass, upland donkey and panther; the deeds (mountains, Bull, Humbaba, lions); the friend he loved, his partner in every danger, overtaken by the fate of mortals (conjectural); six days and seven nights of weeping; the body kept until the maggot; fear of death; the far road; turned to clay; must he not lie down too and never rise (X 119-148).
  4. Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi: he asks the way to Uta-napishti (restored) and what marks it. If possible he will cross the ocean; if not, he will roam the wild (restored) (X 149-154).
  5. Ur-shanabi → Gilgamesh: his own hands have blocked his crossing (conjectural). He smashed the Stone Ones and dumped them in the river (restored); the Stone Ones are broken and the pine has not been stripped (pine uncertain, stripping conjectural) (X 155-158).
  6. Ur-shanabi → Gilgamesh: he must take his axe to the woods and fell three hundred (restored) punting-poles, each five rods long, trim them, fit each one with a knob, and bring them to him (conjectural) (X 159-162).
- **Specifics:** Ur-shanabi's axe; the fight declined; the deeds; six days; Ur-shanabi's title, the boatman (X 119, 149, both restored); 300 poles (restored); 5 rods each (plain text); a knob (George: a boss) on each; the pine; the forest.
- **Short quotation:** "Gilgamish, 'tis thine own hand hath hinder'd [thy crossing the Ocean]" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 47).
- **Gaps:** in this telling the wild-ass lines and the deeds (X 126-131) are wholly restored, and much of the rest of the lament has restored halves. X 156: the object of hindering is conjectural. X 157: the end of the line is restored. X 158: "pine" is uncertain and the stripping conjectural. X 160: the verb and the number are restored, so 300 is a restoration here and again at X 166. X 162: the end is conjectural.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the poles as five _gar_ long each, with knobs of bitumen and sockets, and gives no number of poles (Tablet 10, p. 48); his footnote compares the modern South Mesopotamian punting-pole, a bamboo with a bitumen knob at one end (p. 48 n. 1). In his text Ur-shanabi's complaint is that Gilgamesh destroyed the "sails(?)" (p. 47).
- **Repetitions:** X 112-118 repeat X 40-45; X 119-148 repeat X 46-71 with the deeds moved inside the lament; X 149-154 repeat X 72-77. Ur-shanabi's order (X 159-162) is carried out in the same words in X.7 (X 164-168).

### X.7 The poles, the voyage, the Waters of Death and the sail (X 163-183; p. 80)
- **Where and when:** the forest, then the boat on the ocean, then the Waters of Death. Durations: three days of sailing cover a month and a half's journey (X 171); the poles run out at 120 double-furlongs (X 180).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi. Ur-shanabi gives orders; Gilgamesh does not speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh hears, grabs his axe, draws his sword (restored), goes into the forest, cuts three hundred (restored) poles of five rods each, trims them, fits the knobs, and brings them to Ur-shanabi (conjectural) (X 163-168).
  2. The two board the boat (restored), push off, and work it (restored) themselves (X 169-170).
  3. In three days they cover a month and a half's journey, and Ur-shanabi reaches the Waters of Death (restored) (X 171-172).
  4. Ur-shanabi → Gilgamesh: get to work (uncertain) and take the first pole (restored)! He must not let his hand touch the Waters of Death, or it will be "lamed" (uncertain) (X 173-175).
  5. Ur-shanabi → Gilgamesh: he calls out the poles by number: the second to fourth, fifth to seventh, eighth to tenth, then the eleventh and twelfth (X 176-179).
  6. At 120 double-furlongs Gilgamesh has used up every pole (X 180).
  7. Then he (the name Ur-shanabi restored) takes off his clothing, Gilgamesh pulls off his (the possessive restored) garment, and with his arms raised he forms a yard-arm (X 181-183).
- **Specifics:** axe; sword; forest; 300 poles (restored), 5 rods each, a knob each; the boat; 3 days for a month and a half's journey; the Waters of Death; the hand must not touch them; 12 poles counted aloud; 120 double-furlongs; clothing; garment; raised arms as a yard-arm.
- **Short quotation:** "Let not the Waters of Death touch thy hand" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 48).
- **Gaps:** restored: the sword phrase (X 165), the verb and number (X 166), the boat (X 169), the crewing verb (X 170), "Death" (X 172), the first pole (X 174) and the object of laming (X 175). Conjectural: the end of X 168. Uncertain: the opening command (X 174) and "lame" (X 175). In X 181 the name Ur-shanabi is restored, and in X 182 the possessive that says whose garment it was ⚠️. George's synopsis says the ferryman's garment (p. 74).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the ending differently: Gilgamesh stops poling "with twice-sixty (thrusts)," ungirds his loins and sets up "the mast in its socket" (Tablet 10, p. 48); George has 120 double-furlongs and a yard-arm made with raised arms. Thompson gives the timing as a month and a half's course seen "on the third day" (p. 48).
- **Repetitions:** X 163-168 carry out X 159-162 almost word for word, with the arming formula added (X 164-165). The boarding formula (X 169-170) recurs at the departure from Uta-napishti's shore (XI 271-272). The pole count runs one (X 174), then three, three, three and two (X 176-179), twelve in all.

### X.8 Uta-napishti watches the boat come (X 184-about 203; pp. 80-81)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's shore. He watches the boat approach from far off, after the poles have run out and the garment has gone up (X.7); no interval is stated.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti, speaking to himself; Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi, seen at a distance.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti watches them (restored) from far off, talking to himself and thinking it through (X 184-186).
  2. Uta-napishti → himself: why are the boat's Stone Ones (restored) all smashed, and why is someone aboard who is not its master? The newcomer is not one of his own people; something "on the right" (the rest lost); he looks, and the man is not his (X 187-191).
  3. [Lacuna, marked with asterisks: about X 192-197.]
  4. Uta-napishti, broken (probably still to himself ⚠️): fragments about the boatman and, twice, about the man he is watching (X 198-200).
  5. [Lacuna, marked with asterisks: about X 201-203.]
- **Specifics:** the boat's Stone Ones, smashed; a passenger who is not its master; not one of his men; "on the right"; the boatman.
- **Short quotation:** "(This) is no mortal who cometh" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 48).
- **Gaps:** X 187's "Stone Ones" is restored; X 190 and X 198-200 break off mid-line; two lacunae (about X 192-197 and about X 201-203). What he concluded is lost.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "sails(?)" where George has the Stone Ones, and "no mortal" where George has a man who is not his (Tablet 10, p. 48).
- **Repetitions:** X 184-186 reuse Shiduri's watcher formula (X 10-12). He says twice that the man is not his (X 189, 191), and twice that he is watching him (X 199-200).

### X.9 Landing; greeting; the lament, third telling (X 204-248; pp. 81-82)
- **Where and when:** the quay on Uta-napishti's shore, on landing.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Uta-napishti; both speak. Ur-shanabi came on the boat and is presumably there, but he is not mentioned ⚠️.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh comes near (conjectural) to the quay (X 204). [Lacuna, asterisks: about X 205-206.]
  2. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: he greets him with a wish for long life (the word restored), naming him as Ubar-Tutu's son (the end of the name restored); a broken line mentions someone "after the Deluge"; the rest is lost (X 207-211).
  3. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: the question formula, third time (X 212-218).
  4. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: the answer and lament, third telling, matching the one to Ur-shanabi: wild ass, upland donkey, panther; the shared deeds; the fate of mortals; six days and seven nights of weeping; the body kept until the maggot; fear of death; the far road and the far path; how can he keep quiet; his friend turned to clay; must he lie down too and never rise, for all time (X 219-248).
- **Specifics:** the quay; Uta-napishti as Ubar-Tutu's son; the Deluge (its first mention in the standard Tablets IX-X, in a broken context ⚠️); the lament's details as in X.3.
- **Short quotation:** "Lay me down [like him], throughout all eternity [never returning]?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 45; the closing line of the lament as he gives it in the first telling).
- **Gaps:** X 204's verb is conjectural; lacuna about X 205-206; in X 208 the long-life word and the end of Ubar-Tutu's name are restored; X 209-211 are broken, so what he said about the Deluge is lost. This third telling is the best preserved of the three (for example, X 220-222, 228 and 244-248 are mostly plain text), though the "doom of mortals" phrase, the clause about his own death and the maggot line are still restored or conjectural here (X 234, 237-238).
- **Thompson 1928 has** only a summary here: Uta-Napishtim asks in exactly the same words as Siduri and Ur-Shanabi, down to the closing question about lying down forever (Tablet 10, p. 48).
- **Repetitions:** X 212-218 repeat X 40-45 and X 113-118; X 219-248 repeat X 119-148 (and X 46-71, with the deeds inside the lament).

### X.10 Gilgamesh tells his own journey (X 249-265; pp. 82-83)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's shore, the same meeting. A new speech with a new purpose: instead of asking for the road, as he did with Shiduri and Ur-shanabi, Gilgamesh describes what the search has cost him.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Uta-napishti.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: he had resolved to find Uta-napishti the Distant, the one people talk about, so he wandered through every country, crossed hard mountains again and again, and crossed every sea (X 249-253).
  2. He has had too little sweet sleep and has punished himself by staying awake; he has packed his sinews with grief; what has all that labor gained him? (X 254-257).
  3. *Retold:* his clothes were worn out before he even reached the tavern-keeper. He killed (restored) bear, hyena, lion, panther, cheetah (uncertain), deer and ibex, the wild beasts and game; he ate their meat and skinned (uncertain) them (X 258-261).
  4. He wishes the gate of sorrow shut from now on and its door sealed (conjectural) with tar and pitch; let the dancing no longer be interrupted (conjectural) on his account; for him, happy and carefree (the rest lost) (X 262-265).
- **Specifics:** Uta-napishti the Distant, famous among men (X 250); every land; hard mountains; every sea; sleeplessness; worn-out clothes; the animals: bear, hyena, lion, panther, cheetah (uncertain), deer, ibex; meat; hides; the gate of sorrow; tar and pitch; dancing.
- **Short quotation:** "Nor hath (ever) my face had its fill of gentle sleep (?)" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 49).
- **Gaps:** X 259's verb is restored; the cheetah (X 259) and the skinning (X 261) are uncertain; in X 263 the opening is uncertain and the sealing clause conjectural; in X 264 the interrupting verb is conjectural; X 265 breaks off.
- **Thompson 1928 lists** the animals as "Owl, bat, lion, pard, wild cat, deer, ibex," has him dress himself in their pelts, and mentions only in summary a line about bolting her gate "with pitch and bitumen" (Tablet 10, p. 49).
- **Repetitions:** his sinews packed with grief (X 256) are turned back on him by Uta-napishti (X 299), and his question about what his labor gained (X 257) is echoed at X 297.

### X.11 Uta-napishti's counsel, part one: the king and the fool; the gods of the night; the temples (X 266-289; p. 83)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's shore, his answer to X.10.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti speaks to Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: why does he keep chasing (restored) grief? He is a blend of divine and human flesh, shaped by the gods (restored) in the likeness of his father and mother (X 266-269).
  2. Uta-napishti: did anyone ever build a palace (restored) for a fool, seat him on a council throne and tell him to sit? The fool gets the dregs of the yeast rather than fresh (conjectural) ghee, and bran and grist rather than fine flour (conjectural). He wears a rag (uncertain) rather than good clothes (conjectural), and old rope (conjectural) rather than a belt. With no advisers (uncertain) to guide him (conjectural), he runs his affairs without counsel (the rest lost) (X 270-277).
  3. Uta-napishti: a broken line telling Gilgamesh to give thought to someone, and a question about who is master (conjectural) of them all (X 278-279).
  4. X 280 is lost. Then broken lines: the moon, and certain gods ("of the night," conjectural); the moon travels at night; the gods stay awake; wakeful and sleepless; something laid down in ancient times (X 281-285).
  5. Uta-napishti: broken lines asking him to consider something and mentioning his support; then a condition: if the gods' temples have no (conjectural) provisioner (uncertain), the goddesses' temples… (X 286-289).
  6. [Lacuna, marked with asterisks: about X 290-295.]
- **Specifics:** a blend of divine and human flesh; his father and mother; palace; throne; the council; the fool's fare (yeast dregs, bran, grist) against ghee and fine flour; a rag against fine clothes; old rope against a belt; advisers; the moon; the gods of the night; temples of the gods and of the goddesses; a provisioner.
- **Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928's text is lost here, and he notes nothing connected until his Column VI (Tablet 10, p. 49).
- **Gaps:** restored: "chase" (X 267), "gods" (X 269), "palace" (X 270). Conjectural: "fresh" (X 272), the fine flour (X 273), the fine garments (X 274), the old rope (X 275), the guiding phrase (X 276), "who is" (X 279), "of the night" (X 281), "have no" (X 288). Uncertain: "rag" (X 274), the verb of girding (X 275), "advisers" (X 276), "provisioner" (X 288). X 277-289 are broken at the end of nearly every line. George's reading of the passage from X 280 on (introduction, p. xliv): as the moon and stars (the "gods of the night") keep the regular cycle of months and years, so a king must keep the temples stocked with their regular offerings. His reading of the king-and-fool contrast (pp. xliv-xlv): Gilgamesh, roaming in skins and eating raw meat, is acting like a fool rather than a king, and part of a king's duty is to help those who cannot help themselves.
- **Repetitions:** the blended-flesh line (X 268) restates the divine-flesh descriptions (IX 49, X 7) and the two-parts-god line (IX 51).

### X.12 Uta-napishti's counsel, part two: on Death (X 296-322; pp. 84-85)
- **Where and when:** the same speech, after the gap of about X 290-295. The tablet ends with it.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti speaks to Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: Enkidu (conjectural) was carried off to his fate (X 296).
  2. But Gilgamesh (restored) has labored, and to what end? He wears himself out with endless toil, loads his sinews with grief, and only brings his own end closer (X 297-300).
  3. Humans are snapped off like reeds in a reed-bed; the handsome youth and the lovely girl alike are carried off by Death too soon (conjectural), in their prime (conjectural) (X 301-303).
  4. No one sees Death, no one sees Death's face (restored), no one hears (restored) Death's voice; savage Death simply cuts people down (X 304-307).
  5. People keep building houses and making nests; brothers keep dividing their inheritance; feuds keep breaking out in the land (uncertain) (X 308-311).
  6. The river keeps rising and bringing floods; a mayfly drifts on the water, its face turned toward the sun's face, and suddenly nothing is there (X 312-315).
  7. The abducted and the dead share one lot. Yet no one has ever drawn Death's likeness, and the dead have never greeted a living man in this land (X 316-318).
  8. The Anunnaki, the great gods, met in assembly, and Mammitum, maker of destiny, decreed fates with them. They fixed both Death and Life, but they keep the day of Death to themselves (X 319-322).
- **Specifics:** a reed in a reed-bed; the young man and the young woman; Death personified (face unseen, voice unheard, a killer); houses; nests; brothers' inheritance; feuds; the river in flood; the mayfly; the sun's face; the Anunnaki, the great gods; Mammitum, maker of destiny (a name of the Mother Goddess, Glossary p. 223); an assembly; Death and Life fixed; the day of Death hidden. Figure 11 (p. 84): a third-century BC tablet fragment from Babylon, British Museum Rm 751+34853+35546 (List of Figures; MS f in the appendix, p. 213), captioned with X 315.
- **Short quotation:** "Death, (aye), and Life they determine; of Death is the day not revealéd." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 10, p. 49).
- **Gaps:** conjectural: the opening that names Enkidu (X 296) and the "too soon … prime" phrase (X 303, where "all" is uncertain). Restored: "But you" (X 297), "of Death" (X 305), the verb of hearing (X 306). Uncertain: "the land" (X 311). George's background (pp. xlvi-xlvii): in the poem of Atram-hasis, from the Deluge onward death follows life as a matter of course, and he calls this the mythological background of the end of Uta-napishti's speech; he adds that the key passage of that poem is broken and the point is inferred "by force of argument."
- **Thompson 1928 has** only the end of this speech, from the house-building lines on: he reads a "Kulilu-bird [to] Kirippu-bird" line where George has the mayfly, and adds that the sleeping and the dead, servant and master, are alike once their span is reached (Tablet 10, p. 49). X 296-307 are missing from his text.
- **Repetitions:** four lines in a row begin "ever" (X 308-311), followed by the river line (X 312). Three lines begin with "no one at all" (X 304-306). Uta-napishti reuses Gilgamesh's own words from X 256-257 (X 297-299).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ Shiduri's reason for barring the gate (X 26-28) is lost.
- ⚠️ The first telling of the lament (X 47-62) is wholly restored. In all three tellings the "doom of mortals" phrase, the clause about his own death and the "too much" phrase are conjectural, and the maggot line is restored.
- ⚠️ The number of punting-poles, three hundred, is restored both times (X 160, 166); their length of five rods is plain text. Thompson 1928 gives no number (Tablet 10, p. 48).
- ⚠️ Who took off his clothing and whose garment became the sail: both the name Ur-shanabi (X 181) and the possessive (X 182) are restored. George's synopsis says it was the ferryman's garment (p. 74).
- ⚠️ In the fight (X 100-101) only pronouns are used, and who sat down on the bank (X 108) is lost.
- ⚠️ The pine-stripping phrase (X 88, 158) is an uncertain reading, and the surviving text does not explain what the Stone Ones and the pine have to do with each other. Thompson 1928 reads "sails (?)" and the obscure word _urnu_ instead (Tablet 10, pp. 46-47).
- ⚠️ What Uta-napishti said to himself after X 191 (about X 192-203) and what Gilgamesh said about the Deluge in his greeting (X 209-211) are lost.
- ⚠️ The second part of Uta-napishti's counsel (X 278-289) is broken at the end of nearly every line, and about X 290-295 is lost. George's reading of it, temple provisioning, is his inference (p. xliv).
- The effect of the Waters of Death on a hand is given only by an uncertain verb, "lame" (X 175).
- George's preface says new second-edition sources inform Tablet X, the Stone Ones passage among them (p. xii).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Shiduri at first takes him for a murderer or thief because of how he looks; George 2020 has her take him for a hunter of wild bulls (X 13), and Thompson 1928 for a man who would ravish a woman (Tablet 10, p. 44).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Gilgamesh destroys "stone charms" that Urshanabi keeps with him; George 2020 has the Stone Ones, a crew who closed up the boat and were unafraid of the Waters of Death (X 102-103).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Urshanabi has him cut down 120 trees for punting poles; George 2020 has three hundred poles (the number restored) of five rods each (X 160, 166), and 120 is the number of double-furlongs at which the poles ran out (X 180).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Utnapishtim tells him that fighting the common human fate is futile and lessens life's joys; George 2020 has Uta-napishti say that his ceaseless toil and grief bring his own end closer (X 297-300), with no mention of joys in the surviving lines.

## Leads (observations on the text's own structure only)
- Lead: the tablet rests on a triple repetition. Shiduri, Ur-shanabi and Uta-napishti put the same question about his looks, and Gilgamesh gives the same answer and lament each time (X 40-71, 112-148, 212-248).
- Lead: the first telling is arranged differently (the deeds come before the question, and Shiduri recites them back to him); the second and third tellings put the deeds inside the lament.
- Lead: Gilgamesh asks twice, almost word for word, for the road and its landmark (X 72-77, 149-154); with Uta-napishti the request gives way to an account of the journey (X 249-265).
- Lead: the watcher formula (watching from afar, talking to oneself, thinking it over) frames both arrivals: at Shiduri's tavern (X 10-12) and at Uta-napishti's shore (X 184-186).
- Lead: orders are followed by their execution in the same words (the poles, X 159-162, then 164-168), the same pattern as Tablet XI's washing scene (XI 253-261, then 262-270).
- Lead: Uta-napishti hands Gilgamesh's own words about himself (X 256-257) back to him (X 297-299).
