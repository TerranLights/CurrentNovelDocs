# Sumerian 1. Gilgamesh and Akka ("The envoys of Akka"): play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 2 ("The Sumerian Poems of Gilgamesh"), pp. 99-105. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**Line numbers** are George's margin numbers (printed every fifth line); the poem runs to line 114. Scenes are numbered S1.1, S1.2, and so on. Speeches are summarized, not quoted; George's wording appears only in a few short quotations.

## Chapter 2 context (George's chapter introduction, pp. 98-99)
- Student scribes in eighteenth-century BC Babylonian schools copied five Sumerian poems about Gilgamesh. Modern editors are still piecing them together from hundreds of tablet fragments held in museums in many countries (p. 98).
- In George's view the poet of the Babylonian epic knew these poems, or close relatives of them, in outline at least, though it cannot be shown that he worked from them directly. He may have known them from school, from a Sumerian oral tradition (if one survived), or from an Akkadian one (p. 98).
- The clear borrowings, as George lists them: the Huwawa poem became the far longer Cedar Forest expedition of Tablets III-V; the Bull of Heaven poem was lightly reworked into Tablet VI; and Enkidu's funeral in Tablet VIII echoes Gilgamesh's funeral in "The Death of Gilgamesh" (pp. 98-99).
- Literary Sumerian is more figurative than Akkadian, so the plain sense of a line is sometimes hard to see. George allowed himself "small liberties" of phrasing to keep the translation readable (p. 99).
- Some poems exist in longer and shorter recensions; some tablets skip repeated passages and leave the reader to fill them in from memory. George prints the fullest form even when no single tablet has all of it. Where the customary line count is based on a shorter version, he indents the extra lines and leaves them out of the count (p. 99).

## George's synopsis (pp. 99-101, paraphrased)
- In antiquity the poem was called by its opening words, "The envoys of Akka." It is the shortest Sumerian Gilgamesh poem, the best preserved, and a common school exercise (p. 99).
- It is the only one of the five with **no clear match anywhere in the Akkadian material**. The one shared motif, consulting first the elders and then the young men, turns up in a different setting in Tablet II of the standard version (pp. 99-100).
- Subject: how Uruk came to dominate Kish. Akka, king of Kish, sends messengers, evidently demanding that Uruk submit. Gilgamesh calls the assembly and urges the elders to fight instead (p. 100).
- His proposal is introduced by three puzzling lines about emptying wells, which recur three times. The usual reading takes them as Akka's actual demand that Uruk draw water for Kish; George instead takes them as a picture of the endless labor that would follow the loss of independence (p. 100).
- The elders say submit. Gilgamesh disregards them and asks the young men, who say fight. They open with a proverb that George explains as: serving a king's whims is as painful and unpredictable as standing behind a donkey. They praise Gilgamesh and foresee Kish's defeat. Gilgamesh then tells Enkidu, **his servant in this tradition**, to get ready for war (p. 100).
- Akka soon besieges Uruk. Gilgamesh calls for a volunteer to go out and throw Akka's plans into confusion. Birhurturra of the royal guard steps forward (George: the reading of his name is uncertain). He is seized at once outside the city, beaten and taken to Akka (p. 100).
- Uruk's steward shows himself on the wall; Akka asks if he is Gilgamesh. Birhurturra says no, adding that if it were, there would be a battle that Akka would lose, ending in his capture. For this cheek he is beaten again (p. 100).
- Gilgamesh then mounts the wall. His radiance protects the weak, and the young men march out of the gate with Enkidu at their head. Akka asks the same question; this time the answer is yes, and the predicted sequence follows exactly, ending in Akka's defeat and capture (pp. 100-101).
- At the close Gilgamesh speaks to Akka as to a superior and recalls that Akka once sheltered him. Akka asks to be repaid, and Gilgamesh sends him home to Kish (p. 101).

## Scenes

### S1.1. The envoys of Kish; the elders say submit (lines 1-17, p. 101)
**Where and when:** Uruk, the elders' assembly. Opening of the poem; no time of day.
**Who:** Akka's envoys (they arrive but have no speech); Gilgamesh; the elders in assembly. Speakers: Gilgamesh, the elders.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Envoys sent by Akka, son of Enmebaragesi, travel from Kish to Gilgamesh at Uruk (1-2). The poem does not give their message.
2. Gilgamesh puts the matter to the elders of his city and asks for their advice (3-4).
3. Gilgamesh → elders: he recites the three well lines, then argues that Uruk should refuse to bow to Kish and should fight (5-8).
4. The elders in assembly → Gilgamesh: they recite the same three well lines but conclude the opposite, that Uruk should submit to Kish and not fight (9-14).
5. Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab, relying on the goddess Inanna, disregards the elders' answer (15-17).

**Specifics:** Akka's father is Enmebaragesi (1). The envoys come from Kish (2). The wells: the land's wells, its shallow wells, and its deep wells with hoisting ropes (5-7). The rival power is called "the house of Kish" (8, 14). Gilgamesh's title: lord of Kullab (15). Goddess: Inanna (16).

**Short quotation:** "let us wage war!" (8): the end of Gilgamesh's refrain, which the young men repeat (23, 29); the elders invert it (14).

**Gaps:** none; the passage is complete.

**Repetitions:** the three well lines occur three times (5-7, 11-13, 20-22; George, p. 100). The elders repeat Gilgamesh's four lines (5-8) with the conclusion reversed (11-14).

**Note (George, p. 100):** the common reading of the well lines is a literal demand that Uruk's men haul water for Kish; George's own reading is a figure for endless toil after a loss of independence. The poem does not decide between them.

**Compared with the standard version:** the war with Kish has **no counterpart in the standard version**; Akka, Enmebaragesi and Kish are absent from it. George names only the assembly motif as shared (pp. 99-100). The nearest parallel is in Tablet II, where Gilgamesh shuts Uruk's seven gates, calls the assembly and addresses the elders (Old Babylonian Yale tablet, Y 172-187, which George uses to fill a gap in Tablet II, pp. 19-20), and the senior counselors reply (II 287-299, p. 21; their advice continues at III 1-12, p. 22).
- **Events:** the standard version's question is the Cedar Forest journey against Humbaba, not war or surrender. Enkidu there also argues against the plan before the elders and young men (II 272-286, pp. 20-21); in this poem he is not at the assembly.
- **Who speaks and the outcome:** in both, Gilgamesh speaks to the elders first and the young men second, and the elders oppose him. Here he simply ignores them, trusting Inanna. In the standard version he laughs and turns to Enkidu (II 300-303, p. 21), and the elders later give him advice for the road (III 1-12).
- **Names:** Inanna here, Ishtar in the standard version. "Lord of Kullab" is the Sumerian poems' title for Gilgamesh; the standard version calls the city "Uruk-the-Sheepfold" or "Uruk-the-City."

### S1.2. The young men say fight (lines 18-39, pp. 101-102)
**Where and when:** Uruk, the young men's assembly, right after the elders' meeting. No time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; the young men in assembly. Speakers: Gilgamesh, the young men.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh takes the matter to the young men of the city, asking their advice a second time (18-19).
2. Gilgamesh → young men: the well lines and the call to fight, word for word as before (20-23).
3. The young men in assembly → Gilgamesh (24-39):
   - they quote a proverb: standing guard, waiting in attendance, escorting the king's son, holding a donkey by its rump; who has the breath for such work? (25-28). George's gloss: serving a royal master is painful and unpredictable, like standing behind a donkey (p. 100);
   - they vote for war, not submission (29);
   - they praise the city: the great gods shaped Uruk, which they call the gods' smithy, and Eanna, a house that came down from heaven; its wall is like a cloudbank lying on the ground, and its high dwelling was founded by An; all of it is in Gilgamesh's care, as its king and warrior (30-35);
   - they hail Gilgamesh as a crusher of heads (uncertain) and a prince loved by An, and scoff at Akka: what can he do to frighten them? His army is small, its rearguard a mob, and it will not stand against them (36-39).

**Specifics:** the proverb's four tasks: guard duty, waiting in attendance, escorting the king's son, holding a donkey's hindquarters (25-27). Uruk the gods' smithy (30); Eanna, a house descended from heaven (31); the wall a cloudbank on the earth (33); the high dwelling founded by An (34). Gilgamesh is their king and warrior (35), crusher of heads and prince loved by An (36). Akka's army: small, with a rabble at the rear (38).

**Short quotation:** "a cloudbank resting on the earth" (33): the image recurs in Akka's mouth at 108.

**Gaps:** none. "Crusher of heads" (36) is printed in italics, marking the rendering as uncertain.

**Repetitions:** 20-23 repeat 5-8 exactly; the young men's war call (29) repeats Gilgamesh's (8, 23). Akka later repeats four of the praise lines (30, 33, 34 and the "in your charge" clause of 35) at 107-110, omitting Eanna and the great gods (31-32).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Gilgamesh's speech to the young men of Uruk in Tablet II (II 258-271, p. 20).
- **Events:** in the standard version Gilgamesh asks the young men to bless his journey and promises them a doubled New Year festival when he returns (II 258-271). No answer from them survives; instead Enkidu speaks against the journey to both elders and young men (II 272-286, pp. 20-21). Here the young men answer at length in his favor. The donkey proverb and the praise of Uruk as the gods' smithy have no counterpart.
- **Who speaks:** the young men speak here but not in the surviving standard text.
- **Outcome:** here their backing settles the question. In the standard version the decision is not theirs; they reappear only as a crowd running after Gilgamesh as he leaves (III 213, p. 27).
- **Names and epithets:** An here (Anu in the standard version). In the standard version the young men are described as experts in combat (II 261, 273), and Uruk's wall is praised in the prologue (I 11-23, pp. 1-2) in other terms: its likeness to a strand of wool, its fired bricks, and foundations laid by the Seven Sages.

### S1.3. Gilgamesh orders Enkidu to arm (lines 40-47, p. 102)
**Where and when:** Uruk, right after the young men's answer. No time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu, his servant. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The young men's words delight Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab, and lift his mood (40-41).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, his servant: get the battle gear and weapons ready and take up arms again (42-44).
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (continued): the weapons are to spread terror and a dreadful aura, so that fear of Gilgamesh overwhelms Akka when he comes and his judgment fails him (45-47).

**Specifics:** Enkidu is called Gilgamesh's servant (42). Gear: equipment, battle arms, weapons of war (43-44). The effects wished on Akka: terror, a dread aura, fear, confusion of mind (45-47).

**Short quotation:** "his judgement undone" (47): the phrase recurs at 58; its first half, about confounded good sense, is applied to Uruk at 50.

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** 47 recurs at 58; the confounding of good sense recurs at 50, applied to Uruk.

**Compared with the standard version:** loosely parallels the arming at the forge in Tablet II (Old Babylonian Yale tablet, Y 162-171, pp. 18-19; the standard text's lead-in is II 240, p. 19).
- **Events:** in the standard version both heroes go to the forge, where smiths cast new weapons for the expedition. Here Gilgamesh just orders Enkidu to ready the war gear; there is no forge and no smith.
- **Names and roles:** servant here; friend and equal in the standard version.
- **Numbers:** the standard version weighs the weapons (axes of three talents each, dagger blades of two talents, ten talents carried by each hero, Y 165-171). This poem gives no numbers.
- **Who speaks:** only Gilgamesh here; in the standard version Gilgamesh proposes the forge (II 240) and the two go together, hand in hand.

### S1.4. The siege; Birhurturra volunteers (lines 48-58, p. 103)
**Where and when:** Uruk under siege. The interval is given only by a formula of "not five days ... not ten days" (48), which George's synopsis renders as "Very soon" (p. 100).
**Who:** Akka (arrives with his army, no speech here); Gilgamesh; the city's warriors; Birhurturra. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Birhurturra.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Within that short span Akka, Enmebaragesi's son, besieges Uruk (48-49).
2. Now it is Uruk whose good sense is thrown into confusion (50), the same effect Gilgamesh had wanted for Akka at 47.
3. Gilgamesh → the city's warriors: he has picked every one of them; let a brave man volunteer to go out to Akka (51-54).
4. Birhurturra, the king's bodyguard, bows to his king (55-56).
5. Birhurturra → Gilgamesh: he will go to Akka and throw his mind and judgment into confusion (57-58).

**Specifics:** the five-days / ten-days time formula (48). Akka again named as Enmebaragesi's son (49). Birhurturra's post: royal bodyguard (55). George: the reading of the name Birhurturra is uncertain (p. 100), and so is its meaning (Glossary, p. 221).

**Short quotation:** "I will go to Akka" (54, 57): the words Gilgamesh asks for, then the words the volunteer says.

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** 58 repeats 47. The volunteer's pledge (57) echoes the call for one (54).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** It has no siege of Uruk, no Birhurturra and no call for a volunteer.

### S1.5. Birhurturra captured; the Steward on the wall (lines 59-83, pp. 103-104)
**Where and when:** the city gate, then before Akka outside the walls, within sight of the rampart. Straight after S1.4; no time of day.
**Who:** Birhurturra; unnamed captors (the text just says "they" and does not say whose men they are); Akka; the Steward of Uruk (seen on the wall, silent). Speakers: Birhurturra (his first words to Akka are not given), Akka.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Birhurturra leaves by the city gate and is seized while still in the gateway (59-61).
2. He is beaten all over, from head to foot (62).
3. He is brought before Akka and begins to speak to him (63-64). His words are not recorded.
4. Before he is done, the Steward of Uruk climbs onto the wall and shows his head above the rampart (65-66).
5. Akka sees him and asks Birhurturra, whom he calls slave, whether this is his king (67-69).
6. Birhurturra → Akka: it is not. He describes what would follow if it were, listing the king's features (a fearsome brow, eyes like a bison's, a lapis-lazuli beard, fine fingers): a myriad would fall and a myriad rise, a myriad would roll in the dust, every nation would be overwhelmed, the land's mouths would fill with dust, the king would cut down the horns of the boats, and he would take Akka prisoner in the middle of his army (70-81). This is a **conditional prediction**; it comes true in S1.6.
7. They beat him again from head to foot (82-83). George: this second beating is for his impudence (p. 100).

**Specifics:** captured in the gateway (61). The Steward is never named (65). The king's features: brow, bison eyes, lapis-lazuli beard, fine fingers (72-75). "A myriad," three times (76-77). The horns of the boats, plural here (80). Akka's title, king of Kish (81). Akka calls Birhurturra "Slave" (69).

**Short quotation:** "is that man your king?" (69): Akka's question, asked again at 91.

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** the beating line (62) recurs at 83. The prediction (76-81) recurs as fact at 94-99.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** The only shared detail is Gilgamesh's lapis-lazuli beard: the standard version describes his bearded cheeks as dark as lapis lazuli (I 59, p. 3). There is no steward, no captured envoy and no prophecy of battle in it.

### S1.6. Gilgamesh on the wall; the battle; Akka captured (lines 84-99, p. 104)
**Where and when:** Uruk's wall and rampart, the city gate and the field beyond, in Akka's view. Straight after S1.5; no time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (on the wall); the old and young of Kullab; Uruk's young men; Enkidu; Akka; Birhurturra; the armies. Speakers: Akka, Birhurturra.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Following the Steward, Gilgamesh climbs the wall (84).
2. His dread aura spreads over Kullab's old and young alike, and arms Uruk's young men (85-86; "put" is restored). George's synopsis says his glory shelters the weak (p. 100).
3. The young men take up position in the road at the gate, and Enkidu goes out (the word "from" is uncertain) through the gate (87-88). George's synopsis: Enkidu leads them out (p. 100).
4. Gilgamesh shows his head above the rampart (89).
5. Akka looks up, sees him, and asks Birhurturra the same question (90-91).
6. Birhurturra → Akka: yes, this time it is his king (92).
7. Everything happens as he foretold: the myriads fall, rise and roll in the dust, the nations are overwhelmed, the land's mouths fill with dust, the horns of the boat are cut down, and Akka, king of Kish, is taken prisoner among his own troops (93-99).

**Specifics:** "(his)" before "dread aura" (85) is George's supplied word. Kullab's old and young (85). Weapons of war (86). The door of the city gate and the roadway (87). "A myriad," three times (94-95). The horns of the boat, singular here (98; plural at 80).

**Short quotation:** "That man is indeed my king!" (92)

**Gaps:** "put" (86) is restored; "from" (88) is an uncertain rendering. ⚠️ Lines 98-99 do not name who cuts down the horns and who takes Akka prisoner; the nearest names are Gilgamesh (89) and Akka (90). George's synopsis says only that Akka is defeated and captured (p. 101).

**Repetitions:** 91 repeats 69. 94-99 repeat the prediction of 76-81 as statements of fact, with "boats" changed to "boat."

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Gilgamesh never fights a human army in it, and it has no siege, no sortie and no captured king.

### S1.7. Gilgamesh repays Akka and frees him (lines 100-112, pp. 104-105)
**Where and when:** after the battle; no place or time of day is given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Akka, now a prisoner. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Akka. In the book the second and third speeches are headed only by printed speaker names ("Akka:", "Gilgamesh:", pp. 104-105), with no narrative lead-in.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab, → Akka: he hails Akka by five military ranks in a row, lieutenant, captain, commander, general and field marshal, each with "my" (100-103). George: he speaks to Akka as to a superior (p. 101).
2. Gilgamesh → Akka (continued): Akka once gave him breath and life, took a fugitive into his embrace, and fed a runaway bird with grain (104-106). This is a **recollection** of an earlier kindness that the poem never narrates. George: Akka had once given him refuge (p. 101).
3. Akka → Gilgamesh: he repeats the praise of Uruk (the gods' smithy, the wall like a cloudbank on the earth, the high dwelling founded by An), all now in Gilgamesh's charge, and asks to be repaid for his kindness (107-110).
4. Gilgamesh → Akka: with the Sun God as witness, he hereby repays the old favor (111).
5. He sends Akka home to Kish a free man (112).

**Specifics:** the five ranks (102-103). The fugitive, the runaway bird, the grain (105-106). The Sun God, unnamed here, stands witness (111). Akka returns to Kish (112).

**Short quotation:** "a runaway bird" (106)

**Gaps:** "repay me" (110) is restored; the rest is intact.

**Repetitions:** Akka's 107-110 repeat the young men's praise of Uruk (30, 33, 34, 35), leaving out the Eanna and great-gods lines (31-32).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Akka is not in it, and it never mentions Gilgamesh having once been sheltered as a fugitive. The question of sparing a captive does arise in the standard version, with the opposite result: the captured Humbaba begs for his life, Enkidu argues against mercy, and Humbaba is killed (about V 166-206 and V 285-290, pp. 42-45). Wikipedia's synopsis points to this shared theme (see Open questions).

### S1.8. Closing praise (lines 113-114, p. 105)
**Where and when:** the narrator's closing words, outside the story.
**Who:** the narrator, addressing Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narrator → Gilgamesh: the narrator hails him as lord of Kullab and declares his praise sweet (113-114).

**Specifics:** "lord of Kullab" once more (113). The poem ends at 114.

**Short quotation:** "sweet is your praise!" (114)

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** the same closing formula ends "Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven," where it praises Inanna instead (Ma 140, p. 131).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** The standard version has no closing praise; it ends with Gilgamesh inviting Ur-shanabi to climb and inspect Uruk's wall, in lines repeated from the prologue (end of Tablet XI, pp. 96-97; Tablet I 18-23, p. 2).

## Elements list
### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Gilgamesh:** lord of Kullab (S1.1, S1.3, S1.4, S1.7, S1.8); Uruk's king and warrior, "_crusher of heads_" (uncertain rendering), prince beloved of An (S1.2); as Birhurturra lists his features: fearsome brow, bison eyes, lapis-lazuli beard, fine fingers (S1.5); his dread aura (S1.6).
- **Akka:** son of Enmebaragesi (S1.1, S1.4); king of Kish (S1.5, S1.6); hailed by Gilgamesh as lieutenant, captain, commander, general and field marshal (S1.7). The Glossary calls him possibly Gilgamesh's nephew (p. 221).
- **Enkidu:** Gilgamesh's servant (S1.3); goes out of the gate at the head of the young men (S1.6). The Glossary describes him as Gilgamesh's favored servant in the Sumerian tradition (p. 222).
- **Birhurturra:** the royal bodyguard (S1.4); Akka calls him "Slave" (S1.5, S1.6). Reading and meaning of the name uncertain (p. 100; Glossary, p. 221).
- **The Steward of Uruk:** unnamed; climbs the wall and is taken for the king (S1.5, S1.6).
- **Enmebaragesi:** named only as Akka's parent (S1.1, S1.4). The Glossary: apparently Gilgamesh's elder sister (in the Huwawa poem), though historically an early ruler of Kish who is assumed to have been a man (p. 222).
- **The envoys of Akka** (S1.1); **the elders** of Uruk in assembly (S1.1); **the young men** of Uruk in assembly (S1.2, S1.6); **the city's warriors** (S1.4); **Kullab's old and young** (S1.6); **the narrator** (S1.8).

### Places
- **Kish** (S1.1, S1.5, S1.7), also "the house of Kish" (S1.1, S1.2). Glossary: the early center of power in northern Babylonia (p. 223).
- **Uruk**, called the gods' smithy (S1.2, S1.7). **Kullab**, a part of Uruk (S1.1, S1.6; Glossary, p. 223).
- **Eanna**, a house descended from heaven (S1.2). Glossary: the temple of Ishtar and Anu in Uruk (p. 222).
- **Uruk's wall and rampart**, likened to a cloudbank on the ground (S1.2, S1.7); the Steward and then Gilgamesh climb it (S1.5, S1.6).
- **The high dwelling** founded by An (S1.2, S1.7).
- **The city gate:** its gateway, its door, the road in front of it (S1.5, S1.6).
- **The wells of the land**, shallow and deep (S1.1, S1.2).

### Objects and materials
- Battle gear and weapons of war (S1.3, S1.6).
- Hoisting ropes on the deep wells (S1.1, S1.2).
- The horns of the boats (S1.5) / of the boat (S1.6).
- Lapis lazuli (the king's beard, S1.5); grain (S1.7).

### Creatures
- A donkey, in the young men's proverb (S1.2).
- A bison, for the king's eyes (S1.5).
- A runaway bird, in Gilgamesh's words to Akka (S1.7).

### Gods and what they do
- **Inanna:** Gilgamesh trusts in her (S1.1); she does not act.
- **An:** founded Uruk's high dwelling; Gilgamesh is a prince he loves (S1.2, S1.7).
- **The great gods:** shaped Uruk and Eanna (S1.2); the gods of the "smithy" epithet (S1.2, S1.7).
- **The Sun God**, unnamed here (Utu in the other Sumerian poems): witness to the repayment (S1.7).
- No god speaks or acts directly.

### Numbers and time
- The "not five days ... not ten days" formula between the war vote and the siege (S1.4).
- "A myriad" three times in the prediction and three in the fulfillment (S1.5, S1.6).
- The three well lines, said three times (S1.1, S1.2).
- Two assemblies (S1.1, S1.2); two beatings (S1.5); one question asked twice (S1.5, S1.6); five military ranks (S1.7).
- The poem has 114 lines.

### Dreams and omens
- No dreams.
- Birhurturra's conditional prediction (S1.5), fulfilled line for line (S1.6).
- The dread aura Gilgamesh wants his weapons to spread (S1.3), and the aura that covers Kullab (S1.6).

### Customs and rituals
- Envoys sent to demand submission (S1.1; George, p. 100).
- Consulting the elders' assembly, then the young men's, before war (S1.1, S1.2).
- A proverb introduced as a common saying (S1.2).
- A volunteer bowing to the king (S1.4).
- Paying back an old favor by freeing a captured king, with the Sun God as witness (S1.7).
- A closing praise formula (S1.8).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **The well lines** (5-7, 11-13, 20-22): the usual reading is a literal demand that Uruk's men draw water for Kish; George reads them as a figure for endless toil after surrender (p. 100). The poem does not settle it.
- ⚠️ **The envoys' demand** is never stated. George infers that they came to demand Uruk's submission (p. 100).
- ⚠️ **Birhurturra:** reading and meaning of the name uncertain (p. 100; p. 221).
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings** (italics): "crusher of heads" (36); "from" in Enkidu's going out of the gate (88).
- **Restorations:** "put" (86); "repay me" (110).
- ⚠️ **Unnamed subject:** lines 98-99 do not say who cuts down the horns of the boat and captures Akka.
- ⚠️ **Birhurturra's first words to Akka** (64) are not given; the Steward appears before he finishes (65).
- ⚠️ **Akka's earlier favor** (104-106): the poem says Akka once sheltered Gilgamesh as a fugitive but never says when or why.
- ⚠️ **Family ties:** the Glossary calls Akka possibly Gilgamesh's nephew (p. 221) and Enmebaragesi apparently Gilgamesh's elder sister (from "Gilgamesh and Huwawa"), while noting that historically Enmebaragesi was an early king of Kish, assumed male (p. 222). This poem names Enmebaragesi only as Akka's parent.
- ⚠️ **The horns of the boat(s)** (80, 98) are not explained, in the text or by George.
- **History behind the poem:** George's introduction says tradition credits Gilgamesh's military skill with ending Kish's dominance in the north (p. xxxii), and that the Sumerian poems were probably first written down under the Third Dynasty of Ur, perhaps from older oral songs (pp. xxi-xxii).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) calls the poem "Gilgamesh and Aga," with the incipit "The envoys of Aga" or "Akka"; George 2020 uses only the form Akka.
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the Sumerian poems write the hero's name as "Bilgames"; George 2020's translation uses "Gilgamesh" throughout and does not mention the form Bilgames.
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) names two themes shared with the standard version's Humbaba story: counsel from the elders and the question of mercy to captives. George 2020 names only the assembly motif (pp. 99-100); the mercy theme is noted here under S1.7 from George's own Tablet V.

## Leads (observations on the text's structure only)
- **Lead:** the poem runs on exact repetition: the well lines three times; the war call three times (once reversed); Akka's question twice, answered first no with a prediction, then yes with the same lines as fulfillment.
- **Lead:** the confounding of good sense comes up three times: wished on Akka (47), turned on Uruk (50), promised by Birhurturra (58).
- **Lead:** the praise of Uruk is spoken twice to Gilgamesh, first by the young men (30-35), then by Akka when he asks to be repaid (107-110).
- **Lead:** two identical beatings bracket Birhurturra's speech to Akka (62, 82-83).
