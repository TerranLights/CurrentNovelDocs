# Tablet VII. The Death of Enkidu: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

Pages 53-61 (heading and synopsis on p. 53; text from p. 54). Preserved: VII 1, 37-123, 130-177, 182-208, 251-267. The tablet ends with about thirty more lost lines (George, p. 61).

**Line numbers:** taken from George's margin numbers, read from the EPUB markup (the extracted text shows each margin number only as a bare `>`). Ranges between printed numbers are counted line by line, and "about" marks a count that cannot be pinned to a printed number.

**Sources inside this tablet:** VII 2-36 are lost in the standard version. George fills the gap with a prose rendering of a fragmentary Hittite passage, which was based on an older version of the Babylonian poem (p. 54). Scenes VII.1 and VII.2 depend on it and are marked [Hittite].

## George's synopsis (p. 53)
In a dream, Enkidu sees the gods in council sentence him to death. In feverish distress he imagines in front of him the great cedar door he made for Enlil's temple, and curses it for failing to win him the god's favor. Next he turns on the trapper and the prostitute, the means by which he was brought into civilization, and blames them as well. Shamash talks him out of it, and he goes back and blesses the prostitute instead. In a second dream the Angel of Death drags him down to the Netherworld and he is shown a vision of hell. After he tells Gilgamesh the dream he falls ill. On his deathbed he complains to Gilgamesh that his death is shameful compared with dying in battle. He dies.

## Scenes

### VII.1 Enkidu's first dream: the gods in council (VII 1; VII 2-36 lost, content supplied from the Hittite; p. 54)
- **Where and when:** continues straight from VI.15 (Enkidu rose in the night to tell his dream). The Hittite passage opens with daybreak, so the telling happens at dawn. The place is not named.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh. In the dream: Anu, Enlil, Ea and "celestial Shamash"; Anu, Enlil and Shamash speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [Standard version, VII 1] Enkidu → Gilgamesh: why were the great gods in counsel?
  2. [Hittite] Dawn breaks. Enkidu tells Gilgamesh, whom he calls "my brother," that he had a dream that night.
  3. [Hittite, dream retold] Anu, Enlil, Ea and celestial Shamash hold an assembly.
  4. [Hittite, dream] Anu → Enlil: because these two killed the Bull of Heaven and killed Humbaba, the guardian of the cedar-forested mountains, one of the two must die.
  5. [Hittite, dream] Enlil: Enkidu is to die, not Gilgamesh.
  6. [Hittite, dream] Shamash → Enlil ("the hero Enlil"): wasn't it at Enlil's word that they killed the Bull and Humbaba? Must innocent Enkidu die now?
  7. [Hittite, dream] Enlil, angry, → Shamash: Shamash marched along with them every day as if he were their comrade.
- **Specifics:** "the great gods in counsel"; daybreak; Anu, Enlil, Ea, celestial Shamash; the Bull of Heaven; Humbaba, guardian of the mountains "dense-wooded with cedar"; "innocent Enkidu"; Enlil's anger.
- **Short quotation:** "Let Enkidu die, but let not Gilgamesh die!" (Hittite passage, p. 54).
- **Gaps:** VII 2-36 are lost in the standard version. George replaces them with the Hittite passage, which is itself fragmentary; "[I had]," "[held assembly]," "[guarded]," "-[wooded]" and "[let one of them die!]" are restorations within it. The name is printed *Humbaba* with an asterisk: by George's convention (pp. lvi-lvii) the asterisk means an older form of the name (Huwawa) has been changed to the later one for consistency.
- **Repetitions:** none.

### VII.2 Enkidu weeps before his "brother" (Hittite passage, within the lost VII 2-36; p. 54)
- **Where and when:** directly after he tells the dream; same place.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [Hittite] Enkidu lies down in front of Gilgamesh, his tears running like streams.
  2. [Hittite] Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his brother is dear to him. He will never again be raised up to see his brother; he will sit among the dead and cross their threshold, and never set eyes on his brother again.
- **Specifics:** tears "like streams"; "the threshold of the dead"; "my brother."
- **Gaps:** "[flowed]," "[never]," "[Among]," "[I shall cross,]" and "[shall I set]" are restorations within the Hittite passage. After this, George notes that the standard-version text picks up again with Enkidu's delirium at VII 37.
- **Repetitions:** "brother" repeated within the lament.

### VII.3 Enkidu curses the cedar door (VII 37-63, pp. 54-55)
- **Where and when:** not stated. It comes after the lost lines, so the interval since VII.2 is unknown. Gilgamesh is within earshot (VII 64-66).
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to the door (an object). Gilgamesh listens.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu raises his eyes, as though toward the door, and talks to it as if to a man (both phrases are conjectural restorations).
  2. Enkidu → door: it is a door of the woodland and has no sense; he has the understanding it lacks.
  3. Enkidu → door, retelling how it was made: he searched twenty leagues for the finest timber until he found a tall cedar, a tree without rival in the Forest of Cedar.
  4. Its measurements: six rods high, two rods wide, one cubit thick. Its pole and its pivots, top and bottom, are a single piece.
  5. He made it, lifted it and hung it upright in Nippur.
  6. Enkidu → door: had he known it would repay him like this, he would have taken his axe to it and floated it on a raft down to Ebabbara.
  7. He would have taken it to Ebabbara, Shamash's temple, set the cedar up in its gate and placed the Lion-Bird and a bull colossus in the doorway as guardians at its entrance.
  8. A broken passage: he would have done something to the city for Shamash, and something in Uruk, because Shamash heard what he said and, in a time of danger, gave him a weapon.
  9. Enkidu → door, the curse: as the one who made it and raised it, he can also curse it and tear it down. May a king who comes after him hate it, or hang it where no one can see it, and erase Enkidu's name from it and write his own.
- **Specifics:** "door of the woodland"; twenty leagues; a tall cedar; six rods (height), two rods (breadth), one cubit (thickness); pole and pivots "all of a piece"; Nippur (glossary, p. 223: Enlil's cult center; George's synopsis calls the door one made "for Enlil's temple"); an axe; a raft; Ebabbara, "the temple of Shamash" (glossary, p. 222: "Shining House," at Larsa); the Lion-Bird (glossary, p. 223: the Anzû, a lion-headed eagle or a flying stallion); a bull colossus (conjectural); a weapon from Shamash; Enkidu's name written on the door.
- **Short quotation:** "may he remove my name and write upon you his own!" (VII 63).
- **Gaps:** conjectural restorations include "as though to the door," "with a man," "finest," "in the forest," "in the Forest of Cedar," "repay me," "in the gate," "bull colossus," "and placed them as guardians at," "peril," "also can curse you" and "where you cannot be seen." "[sense]," "[cubit]," "[To]," "[you]," "[up]" and "[In]" are certain restorations. VII 55-56 are broken (the city, Shamash, Uruk). "He gave me" (VII 58) is italic (uncertain).
- **Repetitions:**
  - The measurements and the "all of a piece" line repeat what Enkidu said while making the door in Tablet V (about V 316-317), where he also said the Euphrates would carry it to Enlil's house in Nippur.
  - "Had I but known, O door" opens two parallel lines (VII 47-48).

### VII.4 Gilgamesh weeps and promises prayers and a gold statue (VII 64-83, pp. 55-56)
- **Where and when:** directly after the curse on the door.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. "He" tears off his garments and throws away his finery, and as he listens his tears flow quickly. ⚠️ The line does not name its subject; the next couplet says it is Gilgamesh who is listening to Enkidu and weeping.
  2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: (broken) Enkidu, who had understanding and reason, now speaks madness?
  3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: why does his heart speak madness? The dream was extraordinary and the anxiety great. His feverish lips buzzed like flies; the misgivings were great and the dream rare.
  4. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a saying: grief is what the dead leave to the survivor; the dead leave sorrow to those still living.
  5. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a promise of prayer: he will beg the great gods, seek out Shamash, appeal to Enkidu's god, pray to Anu, father of the gods, in Enkidu's presence, have the great counselor Enlil hear him, and win favor with Ea.
  6. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a promise of a statue: he will make Enkidu a statue in gold without limit. The next line is lost.
- **Specifics:** garments and finery (conjectural); tears; "madness"; buzzing like flies; Anu, "father of the gods"; Enlil, "great counsellor"; Ea; Shamash (conjectural); "your god" (Enkidu's own god); a statue of gold "without limit."
- **Short quotation:** "To the one who survives is left grief for a legacy" (VII 75).
- **Gaps:** "his garments" and "his finery" (VII 64) are conjectural. VII 65-66 "tears were flowing" is restored. VII 69 is broken. In VII 70-72, "do you," "now speak" and "your feverish lips" are conjectural and "understanding" and "madness" are italic. In the prayer, "Shamash," "your presence," "to Anu" and "may my entreaty find favour with Ea" are conjectural, and "In," "I will pray" and "great" are italic. VII 83 is lost.
- **Repetitions:** the gold statue promised here is commissioned at VIII 65-72.

### VII.5 Enkidu refuses the offering: Enlil's word is fixed (VII 84-89, p. 56)
- **Where and when:** directly after Gilgamesh's promise.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he should give no silver, no gold, no gemstones.
  2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: what Enlil has said, the gods cannot take back. What he orders, he does not erase; what he lays down, he does not erase.
  3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his own fate is fixed; people go to their doom before their time.
- **Specifics:** silver, gold, gemstones (conjectural); Enlil's word; "fixed" destiny; doom "before their time."
- **Gaps:** "[My friend]" is restored. "Gemstones," "Enlil," "what he" and "is my destiny" are restorations, most of them conjectural. "Give," "The word" and "can rescind" are italic (uncertain). VII 87 has a lost word.
- **Repetitions:** "he doesn't erase" ends two lines in a row (VII 86-87).

### VII.6 At dawn, Enkidu curses the hunter (VII 90-99, p. 56)
- **Where and when:** "at the very first glimmer of brightening dawn," under the rays of the rising sun. ⚠️ The text does not say whether this is the same dawn as in VII.1 or a later one.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Shamash. The hunter is spoken of, not addressed, and is not said to be present.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. At first light Enkidu lifts his head and laments to Shamash, his tears running in the sunlight.
  2. Enkidu → Shamash: he pleads for his precious life.
  3. Enkidu → Shamash, a curse on the hunter (the trapper) who "let me not get as much as my friend": may the hunter not get as much as his friend; may Shamash destroy his profit and cut his income; may his share be reduced in Shamash's presence; may the god of any house he enters leave it by the window.
- **Specifics:** dawn; the sun's rays; the hunter, "the trapper-man"; profit, income, share; a house and its god; the window.
- **Gaps:** "[as for]" (VII 94) is restored; "The house" and "its god" (VII 99) are conjectural; "[After]" (VII 100) is restored.
- **Repetitions:** the dawn formula "At the very first glimmer of brightening dawn" (VII 90) returns four times in Tablet VIII (VIII 1, 65, 92, 213). The curse turns the hunter's own words back on him ("not get as much as his friend").

### VII.7 Enkidu curses Shamhat (VII 100-131, with VII 124-129 lost; pp. 56-57)
- **Where and when:** directly after the curse on the hunter.
- **Who:** Enkidu addresses Shamhat ("Come, Shamhat"). ⚠️ The text does not say she is present.
- **Play-by-play** (the curse is condensed here into groups):
  1. After cursing the hunter to his heart's content, Enkidu decides to curse Shamhat the harlot too.
  2. Enkidu → Shamhat: he will fix her destiny with a great curse that lasts forever and takes effect at once.
  3. No home or family: she will never get a home she can enjoy, never live among a family, never sit in the young women's quarters.
  4. Ruined clothes: the ground will soil her best garment and drunkards will dirty her festive gown; she will never have a beautiful household.
  5. A bare dwelling: her home will be a potter's pit, with no bedroom, family shrine or hearth and no fine furniture; her couch will be a hard bench.
  6. Living rough: she will sit in the dust of the crossroads, sleep among ruins and stand in the shade of the city wall, and thorns and briars will tear her feet.
  7. Abuse and neglect: drunk and sober men alike will hit her face, wives will take her to court, no builder will plaster her roof, an owl will roost in her room, and no banquet will be held in her house.
  8. Six lines are lost.
  9. The reason: she made him weak when he was undefiled; she weakened him in the wild.
- **Specifics:** a potter's clay pit; bedroom, family shrine, hearth; bed, chair and table; a hard bench; the young women's chamber; her finest garment and festive gown; the drunkard; the dust of the crossroads; a field of ruins; the shade of the rampart; thorn and briar; wives as plaintiffs; an unplastered roof; an owl; no banquets; "undefiled"; "in the wild."
- **Short quotation:** "Come, Shamhat, I will fix your destiny" (VII 102), a formula that comes back at VII 151.
- **Gaps:** nearly every line has restored words. Conjectural ones include "midst," "chamber," "A household full of," "clay pit," "hearth" and "Wives." Certain restorations include "the harlot," "you shall not," "the drunkard," "The dust of the cross-," "A field of ruins," "Thorn and," "Drunk and," "The roof of your house," "In your chamber," "Never in your house shall," "you made" and "weak, who was undefiled." "In the" (VII 107) and "hard bench" are italic. VII 124-129 are lost (three asterisks).
- **Repetitions:** the curse is a long run of parallel "you shall / you shall not" clauses. "Undefiled" ends both of the last two lines.

### VII.8 Shamash rebukes Enkidu and foretells his funeral (VII 132-147, pp. 57-58)
- **Where and when:** at once ("straight away"); a voice from the sky.
- **Who:** Shamash speaks to Enkidu as a voice from the sky.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Shamash hears Enkidu's words, and a voice calls out from the sky at once.
  2. Shamash → Enkidu: why curse Shamhat? She fed him bread fit for a god and ale fit for a king, dressed him in a splendid garment and gave him handsome Gilgamesh as his companion.
  3. Shamash → Enkidu, a prophecy: Gilgamesh, his friend and brother, will lay him out on a magnificent bed of honor, place him at his left on a seat of repose, and the rulers of the underworld will all kiss his feet.
  4. Prophecy: Gilgamesh will have the people of Uruk mourn and lament him, and fill the fair people with grief.
  5. Prophecy: after Enkidu is gone, Gilgamesh will let his hair grow matted in mourning and wander the wild dressed in a lion's skin.
- **Specifics:** a voice from the sky; bread fit for a god; ale fit for a king; a splendid garment; "the handsome Gilgamesh"; "your friend and your brother"; a magnificent bed, a bed of honor; on his left; a seat of repose; "the rulers of the underworld"; the people of Uruk ("so bonny"); matted hair; a lion's skin; the wild.
- **Gaps:** "[will]," "[On]," "[he will]," "[the rulers]," "[he will have]," "[so bonny]," "[clad]" and "[wild]" are restorations.
- **Repetitions:**
  - "Bread fit for a god / ale fit for a king" echoes Gilgamesh's question to Ishtar (VI 26-28).
  - The prophecy (VII 139-147) is repeated in the first person by Gilgamesh at VIII 84-91, where George restores most of it from these lines.
  - The prophecy's lion skin and wandering are taken up in Tablet VIII's lament and Tablet IX's opening (Gilgamesh wanders the wild weeping; see those files).

### VII.9 Enkidu calms down and blesses Shamhat (VII 148-161, p. 58)
- **Where and when:** directly after Shamash's words.
- **Who:** Enkidu addresses Shamhat (her presence is again not stated).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu hears "Shamash the hero," and his angry, furious heart grows calm.
  2. Enkidu → Shamhat: he will fix her destiny again; the mouth that cursed her will now bless her.
  3. Blessing: governors and noblemen will love her. Men a league away will slap their thighs, and men two leagues away will shake out their hair.
  4. Blessing: soldiers will be quick to unbuckle their belts for her and will give her obsidian, lapis lazuli, gold and gold earrings.
  5. Blessing: Ishtar, the ablest of the gods, will get her into the house of a man whose home is secure and whose wealth is piled high, and his wife, mother of seven though she is, will be abandoned for her.
- **Specifics:** "Shamash the hero"; governors and noblemen; one league, two leagues; slapping thighs, shaking out hair; a soldier's belt; obsidian, lapis lazuli, gold; gold earrings; Ishtar, "the ablest of gods"; a wealthy man's household; a wife who is "mother of seven."
- **Gaps:** "[heard]," "[his heart]," "[Shamhat, I will fix your destiny!]," "[My]," "[that]," "[you]," "[Governors]," "[At one league off]," "[at two leagues off]," "[be slow]," "[give you]," "-[rings]," "[the ablest]," "[is secure]" and "[For you]" are restorations. VII 149-150 each open with a lost word.
- **Repetitions:** the "fix your destiny" formula from VII 102 returns at VII 151. The blessing answers the curse point by point in kind (home, wealth, finery, men's favor). George's illustration 8 (p. 58) is captioned with VII 159.

### VII.10 Enkidu's second dream, part 1: seized by the dark-faced man (VII 162-182, with VII 178-181 lost; pp. 58-59)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu is lying alone, thinking. He tells of a dream he had "in the course of the night." ⚠️ The text does not say which night, or how long after VII.9.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh ("his friend"). In the dream: Enkidu, a man with a darkened face, and Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu, troubled, lies by himself and broods.
  2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he had a dream during the night.
  3. [Dream] The heavens thundered, the earth echoed back, and he was standing between them.
  4. [Dream] There was a man with a darkened face and features as fierce as a Lion-Bird's. His hands were a lion's paws and his claws an eagle's talons. He grabbed Enkidu by the hair and overpowered him.
  5. [Dream] Enkidu hit him, but he sprang back like a skipping rope; he hit Enkidu and overturned him like a raft.
  6. [Dream] He trampled Enkidu like a mighty wild bull and drenched his body with poisonous slaver.
  7. [Dream] Enkidu called to his friend to save him (the rest of the line is lost).
  8. [Dream] Gilgamesh was afraid of the man, and did something (lost).
  9. Four lines are lost.
  10. [Dream] The man struck him and turned him into a dove.
- **Specifics:** thunder and echo; standing between heaven and earth; a darkened face; a Lion-Bird's features; a lion's paws; an eagle's talons; seized by the hair; a skipping rope; a capsized raft; a wild bull; poisonous slaver; a dove.
- **Short quotation:** "Save me, my friend!" (VII 176).
- **Gaps:** "[began to ponder]" (VII 163) is restored. "Drenching" (VII 175) and "He struck me and" (VII 182) are conjectural. VII 176-177 end in lost words. VII 178-181 are lost (three asterisks).
- **Repetitions:** the exchange of blows is set as a matched pair (VII 172-173: he strikes, the man strikes back).
- ⚠️ **The man is not named in the surviving lines.** George's synopsis (p. 53) and introduction (p. lii) call him the Angel of Death. His glossary entry for Namtar (p. 223) gives "Doom," the minister of Ereshkigal and angel of Death, but the text here does not use the name.

### VII.11 Enkidu's second dream, part 2: the House of Dust (VII 183-208, then VII 209-250 lost; pp. 59-60)
- **Where and when:** inside the dream; the Netherworld ("the house of darkness," "the House of Dust").
- **Who:** Enkidu, still telling Gilgamesh the dream. In the dream: the man who seized him; the dead kings and priests; Etana; Shakkan; Ereshkigal; Belet-ṣeri, who speaks.
- **Play-by-play** (all dream, retold):
  1. The man tied Enkidu's arms like a bird's wings and led him captive to the house of darkness, the seat of Irkalla.
  2. Description of that house: no one who goes in comes out; the road there allows no return; those who live there have no light, eat dust and clay, are dressed like birds in coats of feathers, and live in darkness.
  3. Dust lay thick on the door and bolt, and a deathly silence hung over the House of Dust.
  4. Inside, Enkidu saw crowns heaped in piles: the crowned kings who had ruled the land since ancient times, who had once served roast meat, baked bread and cool water from skins at the tables of Anu and Enlil.
  5. Priests of several kinds were there: *en*-priests, *lagar*-priests, lustration-priests, *lumahhu*-priests and the *gudapsû*-priests of the great gods.
  6. Etana was there, and Shakkan, and Ereshkigal, queen of the Netherworld. In front of her sat Belet-ṣeri, the Netherworld's scribe, holding a tablet and reading aloud to her.
  7. She lifted her head, saw him, and asked who had fetched this man, who had brought this fellow here.
  8. The rest of the vision is lost (about VII 209-250; George's note, p. 60).
- **Specifics:** arms tied like a bird's wings; the house of darkness; Irkalla (glossary, p. 223: "Great City," a name of the Netherworld and of its queen); the path of no return; dust and clay as food; coats of feathers; door and bolt under dust; the House of Dust; crowns stacked in piles; the tables of Anu and Enlil; roast meat, baked bread, cool water from skins; five classes of priest; Etana (glossary, p. 222: a legendary king of Kish who rode an eagle to heaven but stayed mortal, an officer of the Netherworld court); Shakkan (glossary, p. 224: god of gazelles, wild asses and other animals); Ereshkigal, "the queen of the Netherworld"; Belet-ṣeri, "the scribe of the Netherworld" (glossary, p. 221: "Lady of the Desert," who keeps tally for Ereshkigal); a tablet.
- **Short quotation:** "the house which none who enters ever leaves" (VII 185).
- **Gaps:** "[He trussed]," "[and bolt the dust lay thick,]," "[of Dust was poured a deathly quiet.]," "[heads]," "[at the]," "[there was]," "[Belet]-," "[a tablet]," "[She raised]," "[Who was]" and "[Who was it]" are restorations; "this fellow" is conjectural. VII 209-250 are lost. George's linking note says only that the rest of the vision is missing and that Enkidu ends by commending himself to Gilgamesh.
- **Repetitions:** "In the House of Dust that I entered" opens two stanzas (VII 193, 198). "There was/were" introduces each group of the dead.
- ⚠️ **Who "she" is (VII 206):** the pronoun follows the line about Belet-ṣeri reading "in her presence," so it could be Belet-ṣeri or Ereshkigal; the text does not settle it.
- ⚠️ **Italics:** the priestly titles are italicized. George's stated meaning for italics is an uncertain reading or rendering (p. lvi); here they are untranslated Akkadian or Sumerian terms.

### VII.12 Enkidu asks to be remembered; Gilgamesh's comment (VII 251-253, p. 60)
- **Where and when:** the end of the dream speech, after the lost lines.
- **Who:** Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh. Then a line attributed to Gilgamesh (George prints the speaker's name, "Gilgamesh:"); no listener is named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he went through every hardship with Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh should remember him and not forget what he endured.
  2. Gilgamesh: his friend has seen a vision that will never be equaled.
- **Specifics:** "all hardships"; remembering.
- **Gaps:** "[endured]," "[with you]," "[me, my friend]" and "[forget]" are restored; "be equalled" is conjectural. The lines just before are lost (VII 209-250).
- **Repetitions:** none.

### VII.13 Twelve days of sickness (VII 254-261, pp. 60-61)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu's bed. It starts "the day he had the dream" and the text counts the days through a twelfth day.
- **Who:** Enkidu. No speech.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. On the day of the dream his strength gives out.
  2. Enkidu is laid low and lies sick for one day, then a second.
  3. He lies in bed and his sickness worsens on the third and fourth days.
  4. It keeps worsening on the fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth and tenth days.
  5. The eleventh and twelfth days (the rest of the line is lost); Enkidu lies in bed (the rest is lost).
- **Specifics:** the day of the dream; a count of days from one to twelve; his bed; the sickness worsening.
- **Gaps:** "[his strength]," "[and then a second]," "[lay]," "[his sickness *worsened*]," "[the sickness of Enkidu *worsened*]" and "[and a tenth]" are restorations. "Worsened" is italic each time it appears (uncertain). VII 260-261 end in lost words.
- **Repetitions:** the day count is a fixed refrain (days, then "the sickness of Enkidu worsened").

### VII.14 Enkidu's complaint: no death in battle; the lost death scene (VII 262-267, then about 30 lines lost; p. 61)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu's sickbed, after the twelve days (no exact day given).
- **Who:** Enkidu calls for Gilgamesh and speaks to him.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu calls Gilgamesh to him.
  2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: his god has turned against him; he is not dying like a man who falls in battle.
  3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he was afraid of combat, but ... (the rest of the line is lost).
  4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: a man who falls in combat makes his name, but he will not fall in combat and so will not make his name.
  5. The rest of the tablet, about thirty lines, is lost. George says it no doubt described Enkidu's final death throes (p. 61). The death itself is therefore not in the surviving text; Tablet VIII opens with Gilgamesh mourning, and his heart is found not beating at VIII 58.
- **Specifics:** "my god"; death in battle versus death in bed; fear of combat; "making a name."
- **Gaps:** almost every key phrase is a conjectural restoration: "and spoke to his friend," "My god," "I do not die," "makes his name," "I do not fall" and "combat, and shall make not my name." "[falls]" is a certain restoration. VII 265 ends in lost words. About thirty closing lines are lost.
- **Repetitions:** "falls in combat" is set up as a pair (the one who does, and he who does not).

## Elements list

### Characters (epithets as given)
- **Enkidu:** "innocent Enkidu" (Shamash in the Hittite dream, VII.1); called "my brother" and calls Gilgamesh "my brother" (Hittite, VII.1-VII.2); "my friend" throughout. VII.1-VII.14.
- **Gilgamesh:** "the handsome Gilgamesh," "your friend and your brother" (Shamash, VII.8). VII.1-VII.14.
- **Shamhat:** "the harlot" (VII.7, VII.8). Addressed in VII.7 and VII.9; her presence is not stated.
- **The hunter:** "the trapper-man" (VII.6). Spoken of, not present.
- **The dark-faced man of the dream:** not named; George's synopsis calls him the Angel of Death (VII.10, VII.11).
- **The dead in the House of Dust:** the crowned kings of old; *en*-, *lagar*-, lustration-, *lumahhu*- and *gudapsû*-priests; Etana (VII.11).
- **Belet-ṣeri:** "the scribe of the Netherworld" (VII.11).
- **A later king:** wished on the door in the curse (VII.3).
- **People of Uruk:** "so bonny," foretold as mourners (VII.8).
- **In the curse and the blessing:** the drunkard, wives as plaintiffs, a builder, governors, noblemen, soldiers, a rich man and his wife, "mother of seven" (VII.7, VII.9).

### Places
- The house of the gods' assembly (dream, VII.1; not named).
- The Forest of Cedar; Nippur, where the door hangs; Ebabbara, temple of Shamash (VII.3).
- Uruk (VII.3, broken; VII.8).
- The young women's chamber, a potter's pit, the crossroads, a field of ruins, the rampart (in the curse, VII.7).
- The underworld (VII.8).
- In the dream: between heaven and earth; the house of darkness, seat of Irkalla, the House of Dust (VII.10, VII.11).
- Enkidu's bed (VII.13, VII.14).

### Objects and materials
- The cedar door: six rods by two rods by one cubit; pole and pivots; an axe; a raft; the Lion-Bird and bull colossus as gate guardians; Enkidu's name written on it (VII.3).
- A weapon given by Shamash (VII.3).
- Garments and finery torn off; a statue of gold "without limit" (VII.4).
- Silver, gold, gemstones, refused (VII.5).
- The window (VII.6).
- In the curse: her finest garment and festive gown; bedroom, family shrine and hearth; bed, chair and table; a hard bench; thorn and briar; an unplastered roof (VII.7).
- Bread fit for a god, ale fit for a king, a splendid garment; a magnificent bed of honor; a seat of repose; a lion's skin (VII.8).
- A belt; obsidian, lapis lazuli, gold; gold earrings (VII.9).
- In the dream: coats of feathers; dust and clay as food; door and bolt; crowns in piles; roast, baked bread, cool water in skins; a tablet (VII.11).

### Creatures
- The Lion-Bird (VII.3, VII.10).
- A bull colossus (VII.3).
- Flies, in a simile (VII.4).
- An owl, in the curse (VII.7).
- In the dream: lion's paws, eagle's talons, a wild bull, a dove, birds (VII.10, VII.11).
- The Bull of Heaven, recalled (VII.1).

### Gods and what they do
- **Anu:** in the dream, proposes that one of the two heroes die (VII.1). Gilgamesh plans to pray to him (VII.4). The dead once served at his table (VII.11).
- **Enlil:** in the dream, picks Enkidu to die and is angry with Shamash (VII.1). The door hangs in his city (VII.3). Enkidu says his word cannot be revoked (VII.5). The dead once served at his table (VII.11).
- **Ea:** at the assembly (VII.1); Gilgamesh will seek his favor (VII.4).
- **Shamash:** in the dream, defends "innocent Enkidu" (VII.1). He gave Enkidu a weapon (VII.3). Enkidu prays to him at dawn (VII.6). He rebukes Enkidu as a voice from the sky and prophesies the funeral (VII.8).
- **Enkidu's own god:** "your god" (VII.4); "my god has taken against me" (conjectural, VII.14).
- **Ishtar:** in the blessing, "the ablest of gods," will open a rich man's house to Shamhat (VII.9).
- **Ereshkigal:** queen of the Netherworld, seen in the dream (VII.11).
- **Belet-ṣeri:** reads from a tablet before Ereshkigal and asks who brought Enkidu (VII.11).
- **Shakkan:** present in the House of Dust (VII.11).
- **The rulers of the underworld:** will kiss Enkidu's feet (prophecy, VII.8).

### Numbers and time
- Night and daybreak (Hittite, VII.1).
- Twenty leagues; six rods, two rods, one cubit (VII.3).
- "At the very first glimmer of brightening dawn" (VII.6).
- "For all eternity," "now and forthwith" (VII.7).
- One league, two leagues; "mother of seven" (VII.9).
- "In the course of the night" (VII.10).
- Days one through twelve of the sickness (VII.13).
- About 30 lines lost at the end (VII.14).

### Dreams and omens
- **First dream:** the gods' assembly condemns Enkidu (VII.1). Its content survives only through the Hittite passage.
- **Gilgamesh's reading of it:** an extraordinary dream that causes great anxiety (VII.4).
- **Second dream:** Enkidu is seized, turned into a dove and led to the House of Dust (VII.10, VII.11). Gilgamesh calls it a vision that will never be equaled (VII.12).
- **Prophecy:** Shamash foretells Enkidu's funeral and Gilgamesh's mourning (VII.8).

### Customs and rituals
- An assembly of the gods deciding a fate (VII.1).
- Building and hanging a temple door; a door inscribed with its maker's name, and the threat that a later king will replace the name (VII.3).
- Tearing off garments in grief; a promised gold statue; prayer to the great gods (VII.4).
- A dawn appeal to Shamash; cursing in the god's presence; a house god leaving by the window (VII.6).
- Fixing a destiny by curse, then by blessing (VII.7, VII.9).
- Laying the dead on a bed of honor, at the left, on a seat of repose; public mourning; matted hair and a lion's skin as mourning (prophecy, VII.8).
- In the Netherworld: crowns piled up; priests by rank; a scribe reading out a tablet before the queen (VII.11).
- The contrast between dying in battle and making a name, and dying of sickness (VII.14).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **VII 2-36 are lost.** The council of the gods is known only from a Hittite passage based on an older Babylonian version (George, p. 54), so the standard version's own wording and details are unknown.
- ⚠️ **The timing of the dawn in VII.6:** the same morning as the first dream, or later? Not stated.
- ⚠️ **Who tears his garments (VII 64):** the subject is not named in that line; the following couplet points to Gilgamesh.
- ⚠️ **The broken lines about Shamash and a weapon (VII 55-58):** the sense is only partly recoverable.
- ⚠️ **Whether Shamhat or the hunter is present** for the curses and the blessing is not stated.
- ⚠️ **VII 124-129 and VII 178-181 are lost.**
- ⚠️ **The dark-faced man is unnamed in the text;** "Angel of Death" is George's synopsis label.
- ⚠️ **"She raised her head" (VII 206):** Belet-ṣeri or Ereshkigal.
- ⚠️ **VII 209-250 are lost:** most of the vision of the Netherworld (George, p. 60). His introduction says these lines are now "almost entirely lost" (p. lii).
- ⚠️ **Which dream is meant** by "the day he had the dream" (VII 254)? By position, the second.
- ⚠️ **Enkidu's last speech (VII 262-267) is mostly conjectural restoration.**
- ⚠️ **Enkidu's death is not preserved.** It falls in the roughly thirty lost lines at the end (George, p. 61). His synopsis states "He dies."

## Leads (structure only)
- **Lead:** Enkidu passes through three objects of blame in turn (the door, the hunter, Shamhat), and the third curse is reversed by a blessing in the same form ("I will fix your destiny").
- **Lead:** the tablet holds two dreams: the first of the gods above, the second of the Netherworld below. In the first, Shamash speaks up for Enkidu; in the second, a Netherworld official asks who brought him.
- **Lead:** Shamash's prophecy (VII 139-147) is fulfilled almost word for word by Gilgamesh in VIII 84-91, and his promise of a gold statue (VII 82) is fulfilled at VIII 65-72.
- **Lead:** the door's measurements repeat Tablet V's description (about V 316-317) at the moment Enkidu turns against the door.
- **Lead:** George's introduction (p. xlix) ties the gods' sentence to Humbaba's dying curse in Tablet V: Enkidu dies young and without a family. Compare Tablet V at about V 280 (see that file).
- **Lead:** the sickness is counted day by day up to twelve, and the count breaks off in damage.
