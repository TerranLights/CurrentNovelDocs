# Chapter 4. Miscellaneous Babylonian Fragments (Ugarit, Hattusa, Emar, Megiddo, Sealand): play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 4 (pp. 197-208). A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How this file is organized.** Chapter 4 collects early Babylonian Gilgamesh pieces found outside Babylonia (Syria, Anatolia, Palestine), plus the Sealand tablet from Babylonia. Each find-site gets its own heading with George's note on the tablet (paraphrased), then its scenes, numbered M.1, M.2 and so on straight through the chapter. Line numbers belong to each fragment. Page numbers are the printed pages.

**Notes on the text used.** The prime mark (´) on a line number, such as i 5´, means George counts from the first surviving line of a broken column (here "i" and "ii" are columns). Italics in the book (shown here with ⚠️ or as "*uncertain*") mean an insecure decipherment or rendering; italics inside brackets mean an uncertain or conjectural restoration (Note on the Translation, p. lvi). Chapter 4 has no footnotes. In older poetry one verse may run over two or three tablet lines (p. lv), so where a line number is not one George prints it is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about" if the count is unsure.

**A warning about the comparisons.** Where the standard version's own lines are lost, George fills the gap in chapter 1 with older text. In those places the comparison is with that older text, not with independent standard-version wording, and the comparison says so.

**Wording.** The play-by-play below is in my own words; quotations are kept to a few words and cited.

## George's synopsis (chapter introduction, p. 197)
Besides the fragments from Babylonia itself (chapter 3), some early witnesses to the poem come from outside it: Syria, Anatolia and Palestine. They show that Babylonian literature, Gilgamesh included, was known far outside Mesopotamia when cuneiform was in use across the Near East. The group is mixed: some are close to one Babylonian version or another, others more distant. The Sealand tablet, from eastern or southern Babylonia, is added at the end; it carries a one-of-a-kind reworking of the Old Babylonian poem with the names changed.

## Site 1. Ugarit (pp. 197-198)
**The source (George, p. 197), paraphrased:** three fragments and one complete tablet of the Babylonian Gilgamesh were excavated in 1994 at Ras Shamra (ancient Ugarit), on the coast of Syria, in a building identified as Urtenu's house. None is later than the 12th century BC; they were published in 2007. The three fragments belong with SB Tablets IV-V but are very corrupt; one intelligible passage, Humbaba pleading for his life, is used in chapter 1 to restore SB Tablet V, and is not repeated here. The complete tablet holds an odd version of the epic's opening: most lines are known from SB Tablet I, but many come in an unexpected order. George suggests the text "may be the outcome of a faulty memory."

### M.1 The opening praise and the people's complaint (Ugarit 1-15; p. 198)
- **Where and when:** no setting; a narrator's opening.
- **Who:** an unnamed narrator. Gilgamesh is the subject; Uttur-napishti, Ishtar and Anu are named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The narrator praises a man who saw the Deep, the foundation of the land, who knew the right ways and was wise in everything. The same two lines are said again with Gilgamesh's name.
  2. He searched out every seat of power and knew the whole of wisdom.
  3. He journeyed far to Uttur-napishti, crossing the broad sea toward the sunrise, and brought home a story from before the Flood.
  4. He traveled a long way, weary and in pain; his labors were recorded on stone tablets erected for him.
  5. Gilgamesh keeps every young bride from her bridegroom; he is the wild bull and they are his cows.
  6. Ishtar hears their complaint; the uproar reaches all the way to Anu's heaven.
- **Specifics:** the Deep; Uttur-napishti (a name form of Uta-napishti); the Deluge; the sea "to the sunrise"; stone tablets; young brides; Ishtar; "the heaven of Anu."
- **Short quotations:** "he is their wild bull, they are the cows" (Ugarit 13).
- **Gaps:** none; these lines have no brackets.
- **Repetitions:** lines 1-2 are repeated as lines 3-4 with the name Gilgamesh added.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 1-10 and I 68-78 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Missing here: SB I 7 (he saw secret things and found hidden ones); SB's lines on building Uruk's rampart and Eanna (I 11-17).
  - Added or moved here: a line naming the road to Uttur-napishti (SB names Uta-napishti only later, in the paean, I 42); the ocean crossing, which SB has at I 40, is moved into the opening.
  - Wording: SB says he came home weary and found peace (I 9); Ugarit says weary and in pain. SB says his labors were set on a tablet of stone (I 10); Ugarit says stone tablets were erected for him.
  - The tyranny: SB (I 68-76) has him keep son from father, daughter from mother and girl from bridegroom, and calls him their herdsman; Ugarit keeps only the bride and calls him their wild bull.
  - The complaint: in SB it rises to heaven (I 74), Ishtar heeds it (I 78), and the gods go on to appeal to Anu and summon Aruru (I 79-104). Ugarit has the noise reach Anu's heaven and stops there; the tablet is complete, and the creation of Enkidu is not on it.
  - Name: Uttur-napishti vs. Uta-napishti.

### M.2 "Surpassing all other kings"; Gilgamesh told to climb the wall and read the tablet (Ugarit 16-27; p. 198)
- **Where and when:** no setting; the narrator addresses Gilgamesh.
- **Who:** the narrator; Gilgamesh (addressed by name).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The narrator hails him as greater than all other kings, heroic in build, Uruk's brave son, a *butting* (uncertain) wild bull. The pair of lines is repeated, the second time opening with Gilgamesh's name.
  2. Gilgamesh is told to climb Uruk's wall and walk along it, look over its foundations and inspect its brickwork.
  3. He is told to open the cedar tablet-box, undo its bronze clasps, take out the lapis lazuli tablet and read it aloud.
  4. What he is to read: whether the bricks were not kiln-baked, and whether the Seven Sages had not laid the foundations. Then Uruk's measure: a square mile each of city, date-grove and clay-pit, half a square mile for Ishtar's temple, three and a half square miles in all (*expanse* uncertain).
- **Specifics:** Uruk's wall, foundations and brickwork; a box of cedar wood with bronze clasps; a tablet of lapis lazuli; the Seven Sages; 1 + 1 + 1 + 1/2 = 3 1/2 square miles; Ishtar's temple.
- **Short quotations:** "Climb, O Gilgamesh, Uruk's wall" (Ugarit 20).
- **Gaps:** none.
- **Repetitions:** the "Surpassing" couplet, twice (lines 16-19).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 18-30 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Who is addressed: SB speaks to an unnamed listener ("Climb Uruk's wall," I 18); Ugarit tells Gilgamesh himself to climb.
  - Order and content of the reading: SB goes wall (I 18-23), then box and tablet (I 24-28), then "Surpassing" (I 29-30), and what the tablet tells is Gilgamesh's hardships (I 28). Ugarit puts "Surpassing" first, then the wall, then the box, and makes the wall lines and Uruk's measurements the very words read from the lapis tablet.
  - Details: SB tells the listener to [*find*] the box and to lift the lid of its secret (I 24-26); Ugarit says "open" and has no lid line. SB's bull is "on the rampage"; Ugarit's is "*butting*."
  - Missing here: the rest of SB's paean (I 31-48: vanguard and rearguard, the flood-wave, Lugalbanda and Ninsun, his deeds, two-thirds god).

### M.3 Gilgamesh's tyranny over the young men; his giant body (Ugarit 28-38; p. 198)
- **Where and when:** no setting; narration.
- **Who:** the narrator; Gilgamesh (subject); the young men of Uruk.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh wrestled fifty companions one after another, ruled over the young men every day, and kept Uruk's young men in fear for no reason.
  2. His hair grew thick like barley; his teeth shone like the rising sun; his curls were as dark as wool dyed the color of lapis.
  3. His measurements: eleven cubits tall; four cubits across the chest; a foot of a triple cubit; legs a reed-length long; the *curls at* (uncertain) his cheeks a triple cubit.
  4. A broken line about his face ("To … of his face"); the tablet ends.
- **Specifics:** fifty companions; barley; the rising sun; lapis-colored wool; 11 cubits; 4 cubits; triple cubit (foot); reed-length (legs); triple cubit (curls).
- **Short quotations:** "his teeth gleaming like the rising sun" (Ugarit 32).
- **Gaps:** line 38 is broken.
- **Repetitions:** "the young men" twice (lines 29-30).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 49-67 (SB's own text). SB gives the body first and the tyranny after: made by the Lady of the Gods and Nudimmud (I 49-50); eleven cubits tall (I 52); a chest of [four] cubits (I 53); a triple-cubit foot and a leg of half a rod (I 54); a stride of six cubits (George prints the line number "I 55=57"); curls three cubits long, a beard dark as lapis lazuli, hair thick as barley (about I 56-60); then his companions kept on their feet by his contests and the young men harried without cause (I 66-67). Differences:
  - Order: reversed (Ugarit puts the tyranny before the body).
  - Numbers: "fifty" companions only in Ugarit; SB's six-cubit stride is not in Ugarit.
  - Added in Ugarit: teeth shining like the sunrise.
  - Wording: Ugarit's legs "a reed-length" vs. SB's leg "half a rod"; Ugarit compares his curls to lapis-colored wool, SB his beard to lapis lazuli.
  - Missing in Ugarit: the divine makers of his body.

## Site 2. Hattusa (pp. 199-202)
**The source (George, p. 199), paraphrased:** pieces of the Babylonian Gilgamesh have turned up three times at Hattusa, the Hittite capital in central Anatolia (modern Boğazköy, also Boğazkale). (1) In 1906-7, a 13th-century BC tablet, now in Berlin, called here Bo2: its obverse tells Gilgamesh's second dream on the way to the Cedar Forest, parallel to the Old Babylonian Schøyen2 tablet; its reverse holds a badly corrupted version of the Ishtar-and-Bull episode, not given. (2) In 1934, a scrap from the dream episodes, in Ankara, too small to translate. (3) In 1983, pieces of one library tablet, here Bo1, about 1400 BC, with a text like the Old Babylonian edition of the Pennsylvania and Yale tablets; two pieces are given: fragment (a), parallel to Pennsylvania lines 51-102, and fragment (d), parallel to Yale lines 182-95. (Introduction, p. xxviii: some Hattusa material is the oldest of the western group and is very like the Pennsylvania and Yale epic, clearly older than Sîn-leqi-unninni's standard version.)

### M.4 Bo1 (a): the harlot's invitation; the shepherds' camp; bread and ale (Bo1 (a) about 1-16; pp. 199-200)
- **George's synopsis (p. 199), paraphrased:** after the seduction, the prostitute urges Enkidu to leave the wild and join men; she gives him half her clothing to wear; at the shepherds' camp the shepherds admire him; he learns to eat and drink as men do.
- **Where and when:** in the wild, then the shepherds' camp; no time stated.
- **Who:** [the harlot] (named only in restorations), Enkidu, the shepherds. Speakers: the harlot → Enkidu (three times); the shepherds among themselves.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [The harlot] tells Enkidu he is handsome and [*godlike*]; why [roam] with the wild beasts? … He is godlike; [is any man *his equal in splendor*]?
  2. [The harlot] speaks again: she will [take] him to the sheep-pen [where the shepherds camp].
  3. She strips off one garment and he puts it on; [she wears the other].
  4. She [takes his hand] and *walks* (uncertain) ahead of him like a god, [to the camp] by the sheep-pen.
  5. [The shepherds gather]; the crowd [*talks*] among themselves: [he has Gilgamesh's build] but is shorter and [heavier]-boned; [he must be the one born] in the uplands and [raised on] animals' milk.
  6. [They set bread before him]; he looks at the bread, baffled. [They set ale before him]; [he looks at it], baffled.
  7. [The harlot] tells him to eat the bread, [fit for a god], and [drink the ale], fit for a king.
  8. [He eats his fill of bread]; he drinks all seven [jugfuls] of ale.
- **Specifics:** one garment shared; the shepherds' camp and sheep-pen; animals' milk; bread "[fit for a god]"; ale "fit for a king"; seven jugfuls.
- **Short quotations:** "fit for a king!" (Bo1 (a) 15).
- **Gaps:** most of the passage is George's restoration in brackets, filled from the Pennsylvania tablet. Unrestored text is limited to scattered words and half-lines: Enkidu's name, the compliment that he is handsome and godlike, the call "Come, Enkidu," the sheep-pen, the stripped garment, the crowd, the shorter stature and the bone, the uplands, the animals' milk, his bewilderment at the bread, the bread and ale "fit for a king," and the seven jugfuls. The printed verse lines outnumber the tablet line numbers here (George notes that in Syria and elsewhere in the West a verse need not match a tablet line, p. lv), so lines are "about."
- **Repetitions:** "you are like a god" twice; "he was confused" twice (once for bread, once for ale); the harlot's speech formula three times.
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Pennsylvania tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.6-OB.7) and SB II (whose gap George fills from P; SB's own II 36-46). Differences:
  - Added in Bo1: a question whether any man matches his splendor (restored, uncertain).
  - Where she invites him: in P she invites him to Uruk and the temple Eanna; in Bo1 she proposes to take him to the shepherds and their sheep-pen (the word "sheep-pen" is plain text). P's lines about skilled labor and his being banished from the shepherdless regions are absent.
  - The garment: in P she clothes him; in Bo1 he dresses himself in the garment she took off.
  - The food: in P he stares at the bread and does not know how to eat or drink; in Bo1 bread and ale are set out separately and each time he is baffled.
  - Her words: P calls the bread "essential to life" and ale "the lot of the land"; Bo1 calls them fit for a god and fit for a king, the same pair of phrases SB uses in Shamash's rebuke to Enkidu (VII 135-136) and in Gilgamesh's question to Ishtar (VI 27-28, restored).
  - The same as P, against SB: he is shorter (SB II 41: tall) and raised on animals' milk; seven jugfuls.

### M.5 Bo1 (d): Gilgamesh announces the expedition; the elders warn him (Bo1 (d) about 1-8; p. 201)
- **George's synopsis (p. 201), paraphrased:** Gilgamesh tells Uruk's elders of the expedition he plans, and they warn him of its danger.
- **Where and when:** Uruk, before the elders; no time stated.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (implied; the speaker is not named in the surviving lines); [the elders of Uruk] (restored). Speakers: Gilgamesh → elders; elders → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh: [he wants to see the god men talk about], whose [name] the earth [keeps repeating].
  2. Gilgamesh, continued: [he will defeat him in the Cedar Forest] and make the [land] learn [that Uruk's offspring is strong]; [he will set out and cut the cedar]; he will [set up a name] that lasts forever.
  3. [The elders] (restored, uncertain) answer: why does he want [to do this]? Huwawa's ambush is [a battle no one can win]; [*who can stand against his weapons*?]; the [forest] is [wild country] for [*sixty*] (uncertain) leagues.
- **Specifics:** the god of whom men talk (Huwawa); the Cedar Forest; Uruk's offshoot; a name of eternity; Huwawa's ambush; [sixty] leagues.
- **Short quotations:** none; nearly all the wording is restored.
- **Gaps:** almost everything is restored from the Yale tablet. Unrestored are only single words and short pieces: "the earth," "repeat," "learn," "of eternity," the why-question, "Huwawa's ambush," "leagues."
- **Repetitions:** none within the passage.
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Yale tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.17), which George uses to fill SB II at this point; SB's own text is the senior advisers' warning (II 287-299). Differences, resting only on Bo1's plain words ⚠️:
  - "The earth" repeats the god's name (Bo1) vs. "the lands" (Yale).
  - The elders' answer in Bo1 begins with the why-question and the ambush, then his weapons, then the sixty leagues. In Yale it begins with "You are young," then Huwawa's strange look and weapons and the sixty leagues, adds the Deluge-fire-death description, and ends with the why-question and the ambush. SB's own warning (II 289-299) has "You are young" and the Deluge-fire-death lines but no why-question or ambush line. Bo1, as preserved, has neither "You are young" nor the Deluge-fire-death lines.
  - Names: Huwawa (plain in Bo1) vs. Humbaba (SB).

### M.6 Bo2: the second dream on the road: the mountain and the man like a lion (Bo2 obverse i 5´ to about 26´; pp. 201-202)
- **George's synopsis (p. 201), paraphrased:** on the road to the forest Gilgamesh dreams for the second time; Enkidu reads the dream and assures him it bodes well.
- **Where and when:** on the road; a night camp; Gilgamesh wakes at midnight. The beginning of the column is missing.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The two take each other's hand and go on; they [make] camp for the night and lie down.
  2. Sleep, which flows out at night, comes over [Gilgamesh]; at midnight it leaves him.
  3. [He gets up] and tells Enkidu he has had a dream: if Enkidu did not wake him, why [is he awake]? He says it twice, the second time naming Enkidu.
  4. Dream (retold): this second dream outdoes the first. In it a mountain … threw him down and held him fast by the feet …; a dreadful aura overcame [his arms].
  5. Dream, continued: a man [like a lion came toward him], the handsomest in the land, his beauty …; he pulled him out from under the mountain and …; gave him water to drink so that his heart [grew calm]; and set his feet [on] the ground.
  6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he asks, twice, whether the one they are heading for, Huwawa, is not [himself the mountain], strange as he is; then he tells him to *cast aside* (uncertain) his fear … (breaks off).
- **Specifics:** the night camp; sleep that "spills forth" at night; the mountain; the aura; the man like a lion; water to drink; Huwawa.
- **Short quotations:** "My second dream sur[passes] the first" (Bo2 i about 12´).
- **Gaps:** the start of the column is lost. Restored in brackets: the pitching of camp, Gilgamesh's name in i 7´, the words "I have had a dream" and "am I awake?", "my arms," the lion-like man's approach, "calm," Enkidu's speech formula, and half of each "is he not the mountain" line. Lines i 13´-17´ break off at the end; "*cast aside*" is uncertain. Between i 5´ and i 15´ the printed lines outnumber the line numbers, so those lines are "about."
- **Repetitions:** the waking couplet twice; Enkidu's mountain question twice.
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Schøyen2 tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.22), which George uses to fill SB's lost second dream (SB IV after IV 54). George's note there (SB IV, p. 30) also cites a Middle Babylonian paraphrase from Hattusa showing that SB's second dream was a rescue by the Sun God from under an avalanche; he does not say whether that paraphrase is Bo2 ⚠️. Differences:
  - Count: second dream here, as in SB's arrangement; first dream in Schøyen2.
  - Before the dream: here the heroes walk on hand in hand and camp; Schøyen2 just has Gilgamesh lying at rest; SB has the dream ritual (well, flour offering, House of the Dream God).
  - Waking words: here "if you did not wake me, why am I awake?", closer to SB's formula (didn't you call me? why have I woken? IV about 49-54) than to Schøyen2's "why did you not wake me?"
  - The mountain holds him by the feet (here) vs. presses down on him (Schøyen2).
  - Added here: the man gives him water and calms him, and sets his feet on the ground. (In the Harmal dream, chapter 3 file OB.27, the giver of water is Lugalbanda.)
  - Outcome: here Enkidu's explanation breaks off before naming the man; in Schøyen2 the man is Shamash.

## Site 3. Emar (pp. 202-204)
**The source (George, p. 202), paraphrased:** in 1974 excavators at Tell Meskene (ancient Emar), on the middle Euphrates in Syria, found pieces of two Gilgamesh tablets, now in the Aleppo museum, from a scriptorium active in the 13th and early 12th centuries BC. The smaller piece has a few broken lines parallel to SB Tablet V and is not given. The other pieces come from a tablet with the Ishtar-and-Bull story, parallel to SB Tablet VI. (Introduction, p. xxviii: the Emar texts, several centuries younger than the Hattusa ones, are much closer to Sîn-leqi-unninni's text, though whether they come before his work cannot yet be told.)

**George's synopsis (p. 202), paraphrased:** Ishtar, having proposed, is listing the power and riches she will give Gilgamesh. He scorns her and recounts what happened to her past lovers. Furious at the insults, she begs the Bull of Heaven from her father, receives it, and leads it down to Uruk.

### M.7 The end of Ishtar's offer (Emar column i, about 1´-5´; p. 202)
- **Where and when:** in Uruk (implied); the proposal itself is lost.
- **Who:** Ishtar → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Beginning lost.) Ishtar: [when he comes into their house], doorway and footstool will kiss his hands.
  2. Ishtar, continued: [kings will kneel to him]; *lords will come* [*up to him*] (uncertain); they will bring him tribute [from hill country and lowland].
  3. Ishtar, continued: [his goats will bear triplets and his ewes twins]; [no] ox [will equal his at the yoke].
- **Specifics:** house, doorway, footstool; kings and lords; tribute; goats (triplets), ewes (twins); the ox at the yoke.
- **Short quotations:** "kiss [your] hands" (Emar i about 2´).
- **Gaps:** almost all restored from SB. Unrestored: "kiss," "hands," "*lords will come*" (uncertain), the tribute, the bearing of young, the ox.
- **Repetitions:** none.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VI 6-21 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Hands vs. feet: in SB the doorway and footstool kiss his feet (VI 15); here his hands (plain text).
  - Who honors him: SB kings, courtiers and nobles kneel (VI 16); here [kings] (restored) and "lords" who come up to him (uncertain).
  - Missing here: SB's donkey that outruns any mule and the horse that gallops in glory at the chariot (VI 19-20); Emar's run goes from the ewes straight to the ox ⚠️ (the run itself is restored from SB, so this is not certain).
  - Lost here: Ishtar's actual proposal, the chariot of lapis lazuli and gold, the lions and mules, the cedar scent (SB VI 6-14).

### M.8 Gilgamesh's refusal and his insults (Emar column i, about 6´-20´; pp. 202-203)
- **Where and when:** continuing.
- **Who:** [Gilgamesh] → [the lady] Ishtar.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [Gilgamesh answers] Ishtar: if he [marries] her, *will he have to neglect* (uncertain) his body [and clothing], and neglect his food and keep?
  2. (Gap.)
  3. Gilgamesh, continued, a string of insulting comparisons: she is [*frost*] that makes no ice; [a slatted door] letting through breeze [and draught]; [an elephant] … its hoods; pitch [that *dirties the hands*] of whoever carries it; a waterskin that cuts [*its carrier's hands*]; [a battering] ram that knocks down a stone wall; [a shoe that pinches] on the street.
  4. Gilgamesh, continued: [which of her bridegrooms] ever lived to grow old?
- **Specifics:** body, clothing, food; frost and ice; a slatted door; an elephant and "its hoods"; pitch; a waterskin; a battering ram and a stone wall; a shoe; the street.
- **Short quotations:** "did ever grow old?" (Emar i about 20´).
- **Gaps:** a gap after the opening question; most of the insults are restored from SB. Unrestored are pieces of the list: the ice, the breeze, the hoods, the pitch, the waterskin that cuts, the ram and the stone wall, the street, and the closing question about growing old.
- **Repetitions:** the list form ("You, a ...; a ...; a ...").
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VI 22-44 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - The first answer: SB asks where his food and keep will come from and whether she will feed him bread fit for a god and ale fit for a king (VI 25-28); Emar asks whether marrying her means neglecting his body, clothes and food.
  - Missing from Emar's run: SB's "palace that massacres warriors" (VI 35) and the boulder that smashes a stone wall (VI 39) ⚠️ (the run is mostly restored from SB).
  - The ram: SB's ram destroys [*a wall for*] the enemy (VI 40); Emar's ram knocks down a stone wall (plain text), the job SB gives the boulder.
  - The shoe: SB's bites its owner's foot (VI 41); Emar's pinches "walking the street" (plain).
  - The closing question: SB asks which bridegroom of hers lasted forever and which warrior went up to heaven (VI 42-43); Emar asks which ever grew old.

### M.9 Ishtar's past lovers: Dumuzi, the hoopoe, the Sutean, the shepherd (Emar column i, about 21´-35´; p. 203)
- **Where and when:** continuing, after a gap.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh → Ishtar.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [Dumuzi, the lover of her] youth: [year after year] she sentenced him [to lamenting].
  2. She loved the [speckled] hoopoe, soon [*tired of its charms*] (restored, uncertain), struck it and broke [its wing]; it is left crying "My wing!" in [the woods].
  3. She loved the … Sutean; (broken) her house … ; she *made him spend every night* (uncertain) in a tent; she *broke* [*his weapons on the battle*]field (uncertain).
  4. While she lived [in the] fold she loved [the shepherd and herdsman], who never failed to slaughter a sheep for her and [daily] brought her heaps of ember-baked bread; [she struck them and] made [them] wolves.
- **Specifics:** Dumuzi (Tammuz, Ishtar's lover and husband, punished with yearly death; Glossary, p. 221); the speckled hoopoe and its broken wing; a Sutean; a tent; weapons broken in battle; the fold; a sheep killed for her; bread baked in embers; wolves.
- **Short quotations:** "You loved the … Sutean" (Emar i about 28´).
- **Gaps:** the name Dumuzi is itself restored; a gap before this passage; lines i about 28´-30´ are broken.
- **Repetitions:** "You loved ..." opens each lover's story.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VI 44-79 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Dumuzi: SB calls him the husband of her youth (VI 46); Emar the lover (restored).
  - The hoopoe: Emar adds that she quickly tired of it (restored, uncertain).
  - Added in Emar: the Sutean lover, the tent and the broken weapons. No counterpart in SB.
  - Missing from Emar's preserved lines: SB's lion, for whom she dug seven and seven pits (VI 51-52); the horse condemned to whip, spur and lash and its mother Silili (VI 53-57); Ishullanu her father's gardener, whom she turned into a *dwarf* (VI 64-78). In Emar the Sutean stands where SB has the lion and horse ⚠️.
  - The shepherd: SB says he slaughtered kids for her daily (VI 60); Emar says he slaughtered sheep for her. SB turns him into a wolf chased by his own shepherd boys and bitten by his dogs (VI 61-63); Emar makes "[them]" wolves. Only Emar sets this love in the time she lived in the fold.

### M.10 Anu and Ishtar; the Bull of Heaven goes down to Uruk (Emar column ii, about 1´-10´; pp. 203-204)
- **Where and when:** in heaven, then Uruk; after a gap.
- **Who:** [Anu] (name restored in the first speech; plain later), Ishtar; the Bull of Heaven. Speakers: Anu → Ishtar; Ishtar → Anu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Gap: Ishtar's complaint is lost.) [Anu] → Ishtar: well, [wasn't it she who provoked King] Gilgamesh, so that he [spoke slanders] and insults about her?
  2. Ishtar → her father Anu: Father, [please give her the Bull of Heaven], so she [can kill Gilgamesh].
  3. (Gap.)
  4. Anu [hears] her and puts [the Bull of Heaven's nose-rope] in her hands.
  5. (A lost line.) Broken words follow: something did not happen in Uruk; a reed is mentioned; [the river] is reached and falls [seven full cubits].
- **Specifics:** Anu as her father; the Bull of Heaven; its nose-rope (restored); Uruk; a reed; the river; [seven] cubits (restored).
- **Short quotations:** none; the key words are restored.
- **Gaps:** two gaps (before Anu's speech and after Ishtar's request) and a lost line; the Bull, the nose-rope and the seven cubits are all restored from SB. Unrestored: "Lady Ishtar," Gilgamesh's name in Anu's speech, "insults," "her father, Anu," "Father," "Anu," and in the last lines only "Uruk," "a reed," "went," "dropped."
- **Repetitions:** none.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VI 80-118 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Lost in Emar: Ishtar's furious ascent and her weeping complaint to Anu and Antu (VI 80-86).
  - The same in substance: Anu's reply that she provoked Gilgamesh.
  - Not in Emar's preserved lines: SB's "in his dwelling," her threat to break open the Netherworld so that the dead eat the living (VI 96-100), and Anu's condition of seven years' chaff and hay, which she says she has met (VI 101-112). A gap follows her request in Emar, so their absence cannot be proved ⚠️.
  - The descent: SB says the Bull dried up the woods, reed-beds and marshes and lowered the river by seven cubits (VI 115-118); Emar keeps only "in Uruk," "a reed," "went," "dropped."

## Site 4. Megiddo (pp. 204-205)
**The source (George, p. 204), paraphrased:** a chance find of the 1950s at Megiddo (Tell al-Mutasallim) in Palestine, near the spoil heap of the pre-war excavations; now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem. About 14th century BC. It keeps two passages like the later part of SB Tablet VII: the obverse has the second dream of Enkidu, now doomed, in which the Angel of Death captures him and leads him to the Netherworld; the reverse tells of his last decline and death and Gilgamesh's grief.

### M.11 Enkidu recalls their deeds; a dream judged; the dark man of the dream (Megiddo obverse, about 1´-17´; pp. 204-205)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu's sickbed (implied); no time stated. The beginning is missing.
- **Who:** Enkidu; Gilgamesh (addressed, restored); an unnamed second speaker who judges a dream (the quotation marks change) ⚠️. Speakers: Enkidu → Gilgamesh; unnamed speaker; Enkidu (by context).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu [*raises his head*] (uncertain) … and speaks to [*Gilgamesh*] (uncertain).
  2. Enkidu: [he] cut down … (the object is lost) … ; when [*they went up to the Cedar Forest*] and its mountains, and [*destroyed Humbaba, who lived*] in the Cedar Forest, [*captured the Bull of Heaven*] and killed it; at the *shout* (uncertain) of … (broken).
  3. A second speaker (unnamed): [*the dream*] was good and *favorable* (uncertain) …; it was precious, it was good, and it was …; … it was hard.
  4. Enkidu (by context), retelling: in [his] dream [there was a man], small in height, large of …; his face set [like a roaring Lion-Bird]; [hands] that were a lion's [paws] and [claws] that were an eagle's talons …; [*his face was dark*] (uncertain) …; [*of an*] *eagle* (uncertain), his hand … (breaks off).
- **Specifics:** the Cedar Forest and its mountains; [Humbaba] and [the Bull of Heaven] (both restored); a dream man "short of stature"; a Lion-Bird face; lion's paws; eagle's talons.
- **Short quotations:** "short of stature, large of …" (Megiddo obv. 13´).
- **Gaps:** the beginning is missing; nearly every line is broken. Unrestored are pieces only: "cut down," the mountains, the Cedar Forest as Humbaba's home, "slew it," the dream's being "fine" and "precious" and "difficult," the man "short of stature," the set of his face, the lion's paws, the eagle's talons, "his face."
- **Repetitions:** "fine" three times in the judgment of the dream.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII about 69-74 (Gilgamesh comments on Enkidu's first dream), VII 164-171 (the second dream) and VIII 52-54 (Gilgamesh lists their deeds over the dead Enkidu), all SB's own text. Differences:
  - Who recalls the deeds: here Enkidu recalls the Cedar Forest (plain) and, as restored, Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven. In SB's preserved Tablet VII Enkidu does not; the list is spoken by Gilgamesh (VIII 52-54, and three times in Tablet X) ⚠️.
  - "[I] cut down …": the object is lost. In SB VII 37-63 Enkidu speaks bitterly to the cedar door he cut; George does not link the two ⚠️.
  - The judgment of a dream: SB's Gilgamesh calls Enkidu's first dream special and rare and the worry great (VII about 69-74); Megiddo's unnamed speaker calls a dream good, favorable, precious and hard.
  - The dream man: Megiddo makes him short; SB gives no height. Both give him a Lion-Bird face, lion's paws and eagle's talons, and a dark face (SB VII 168; restored in Megiddo).
  - Missing here: SB's opening of the dream, with heaven thundering and Enkidu standing between heaven and earth (VII 166-167).

### M.12 Enkidu's last words; his illness; Gilgamesh's grief (Megiddo reverse, about 1´-19´; p. 205)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu's sickbed; over at least four days; "in the night" is mentioned.
- **Who:** Enkidu, Gilgamesh. Speakers: Enkidu → Gilgamesh; then (presumably) Gilgamesh ⚠️.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu, finishing the dream: [the man grabbed him by] the hair, [*overpowered him*] (uncertain) …; to his friend …; "Take hold, Gilgamesh, …"
  2. Enkidu, continued: [*his friend*] (restored) felt no [*fear*] and did *not abandon* him (uncertain) …; Gilgamesh made it possible for him to wander …; he asks that pure water be [offered] [*in memory of*] (uncertain) his name …; he speaks of his friend as his rescuer …
  3. He lies sick [a first day and] a second, then [a third] and a fourth, in bed …; the illness worsens; his flesh …
  4. Lying in bed, Enkidu summons Gilgamesh …; his cry wakes …
  5. [*Gilgamesh*] sobs (restored, uncertain) like a dove; something of his is *darkened* (uncertain); night is mentioned, and "the foremost of *men*" (uncertain); [he sobs] over his friend …
  6. A speaker (Gilgamesh, presumably): he will mourn … at his side he himself … (breaks off).
  7. The rest is lost.
- **Specifics:** the hair; pure water for his name; the bed; days one to four; the cry; a dove; the night.
- **Short quotations:** "like a dove" (Megiddo rev. 14´).
- **Gaps:** every line is broken. Restored or uncertain: "one day," "a third," "fear," "*desert me*," "Gilgamesh sobbed" (twice). Unrestored pieces include "had no," "he did not," the roaming, the pure water, the friend who rescued him, the second and fourth days, the worsening sickness, the call for Gilgamesh, the cry, the dove, the mourning, "at his side."
- **Repetitions:** "on his bed" three times; "[Gilgamesh sobbed]" twice (restored).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII 171-177 and 255 to about 267, and SB VIII 59-62 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - The friend's courage: in SB Enkidu cries "Save me, my friend!" and then says "You were afraid of him" (VII 176-177). In Megiddo, as George restores it, the friend was without [fear] and did not abandon him, the opposite; but "fear" is restored and the verb is uncertain, and only "had no" and "he did not" are plain ⚠️.
  - Added in Megiddo: Enkidu's thanks for being allowed to roam, the wish for pure water in memory of his name, and his calling Gilgamesh the friend who rescued him. No counterpart in SB.
  - The illness: SB counts the days of worsening to a twelfth (VII 255-260); Megiddo's surviving count reaches a fourth.
  - Missing in Megiddo's lines: Enkidu's last complaint in SB, that he dies not in battle and so makes no name (VII 262 to about 267). Added in Megiddo: Enkidu calls for Gilgamesh and his cry wakes someone.
  - Gilgamesh's grief: Megiddo has him sob "like a dove" (the sobbing restored). In SB, over the dead Enkidu, he veils his friend's face like a bride, circles him like an eagle and paces like a lioness robbed of her cubs (VIII 59-62); in SB a dove appears only as what Enkidu is turned into in his dream (VII 182).

## Site 5. The Sealand tablet (pp. 205-208)
**The source (George, pp. 205-206), paraphrased:** a large fragment whose script places it in the era when the Sealand dynasty ruled southern and eastern Babylonia, roughly the 16th to 15th centuries BC, so it comes from somewhere in their territory. Published in 2007 while in a private collection; at the time George wrote, held by the Museum of the Bible in Washington DC. It tells Gilgamesh's dreams as explained by his mother and Enkidu's civilizing in the shepherds' camp, close to both the Pennsylvania tablet and SB Tablets I-II, and adds little new. Its one big surprise: where other versions have Gilgamesh and Enkidu, the god Sîn stands for Gilgamesh and the god Ea for Enkidu, and the city Uruk becomes nearby Ur, with one exception. Why is unknown. (Sîn is the Moon God, whose cult-center was Ur; Ea is the god of the freshwater Ocean Below; Glossary, pp. 222, 224.)

### M.13 The harlot tells Ea of Sîn's dreams; the rock from the sky; Sîn's wish for a friend (Sealand 1-30; pp. 206-207)
- **Where and when:** the harlot is with Ea (in the wild, by implication); the dream is set in Ur, "before" Ea came from the uplands.
- **Who:** [the harlot] (speaker line restored), Ea; Sîn; Sîn's mother (unnamed). Speakers: the harlot → Ea; Sîn → his mother; the mother → Sîn; Sîn → his mother.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [The harlot → Ea]: let [the *people*] see his face; [*his heart, now wise*,] is looking for a friend; [before] he came from his uplands, Sîn was dreaming in Ur.
  2. Sîn [lies] down, dreams, and gets up to tell his mother.
  3. Dream (retold by Sîn): the sky's stars stood out; a sky-rock dropped before him; he lifted it but it was too heavy; he shoved it but could not move it.
  4. Dream, continued: the land of Ur gathered round him; all the young men together could not lift it; he set his shoulder to it and they helped him push; he lifted it and brought it before his mother.
  5. The mother, being wise, explains: a match for Sîn has been born and raised in the wild; he is as strong as the sky-rock; she will make him join forces with Sîn; people will crowd round him without knowing who he is, and the young men will kiss him; Sîn will see him, rejoice, embrace him and bring him to her.
  6. Sîn replies, wishing for the [friend] to come: [may he strike] him as the sky-rock did, [may] his strength [be great], … and may he ease his heart.
- **Specifics:** the names Sîn, Ea, Ur; "a rock from the sky"; the stars; "the land of Ur"; the young men; the shoulder; the wild.
- **Short quotations:** "Sîn was having dreams in Ur" (Sealand 5).
- **Gaps:** lines 1-4 are mostly restored (the name Ea in them is restored; it is plain later, at lines 80 and 88); "[lay]," "[he]," "[Sîn]," "[a friend]," "[Let him befall]," "[let]," "[be mighty!]" restored; line 30 broken at the start.
- **Repetitions:** the interpretation's see-him-and-embrace-him formula is the Pennsylvania tablet's; "a rock from the sky" three times (dream, interpretation, reply).
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Pennsylvania tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.4) and SB I 214, 224, 242-272 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Names: Sîn for Gilgamesh, Ea for Enkidu, Ur for Uruk.
  - Framing: as in SB (I 242-243), the harlot introduces the dreams by telling Ea that his counterpart dreamed of him before he came; Sealand's restored phrase about his heart seeking a friend recalls SB I 214 and I 224. But George prints the dream itself as narrative, not inside her speech, as in P ⚠️ (the punctuation is George's; Note on the Translation, p. lvi).
  - The object: a rock from the sky, as in SB (I 248), not P's "lump of the sky."
  - The young men: here all of them together cannot lift it; in P they kiss its feet.
  - Bracing: his shoulder (Sealand) vs. his forehead (P).
  - The interpretation: Sealand adds the rock-like strength (as SB I 270), his joining forces with Sîn, and the crowd not knowing who he is. P has none of these.
  - The reply: Sîn answers after the first dream with a wish for the friend to come; in SB Gilgamesh replies only after the second dream, invoking Enlil's command (I 294-297); P has no reply.
  - The mother is unnamed (as in P); SB names her Ninsun.

### M.14 Sîn's second dream: the axe (Sealand 31-42, then a gap of fifteen lines; p. 207)
- **Where and when:** Sîn with his mother; Ur-the-City in the dream.
- **Who:** Sîn → his mother; [the mother] → Sîn.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [Sîn lies down and] dreams; [he gets up to tell] his mother.
  2. Dream (retold): [in a street of] Ur-the-City [an axe lay] with a crowd around it; [he picked it up and set it] at his side; [the axe was odd] in shape; [like a] wife [he loved it], caressed and held it.
  3. [The mother, being wise,] explains: the axe he saw "is a man"; [someone will come to him who will save] his friend; [he will see him and rejoice], [embrace him and bring] him to her.
  4. (A gap of fifteen lines, about 43-57.)
- **Specifics:** Ur-the-City; the axe "strange in shape" (restored); "Like a wife" (restored in part); "a man."
- **Short quotations:** "is a man" (Sealand about 39).
- **Gaps:** most of lines 31-42 is restored. Unrestored: "Ur-the-City," the crowd, "at my side," "in shape," "wife," the caressing, the whole line naming the axe as a man, "his friend," "will rejoice," "before me." About lines 43-57 are lost.
- **Repetitions:** the dream-telling and interpretation formulas of M.13.
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Pennsylvania tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.5), whose lacuna George fills from these very lines, and SB I 273-293 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Order: here (restored) he sets the axe at his side before noting its odd shape and loving it like a wife; in P he sees it, is glad, loves it, then puts it at his side ⚠️ (Sealand's order rests on restorations).
  - Missing here: P's "I saw it and I grew glad."
  - The interpretation ends without P's promise to make him Gilgamesh's equal; SB's Ninsun also promises to make him his equal (I 290).
  - The axe: "a man" (Sealand, P) vs. "a friend" (SB I about 288).
  - Places: Ur-the-City vs. Uruk-the-City.

### M.15 The harlot's invitation to Ea; dressing; the shepherds bow down; the night watch (Sealand 58-91; pp. 207-208)
- **Where and when:** in the wild, then the shepherds' camp, then night.
- **Who:** the harlot (named Shamhat at line 90), Ea, the shepherds and herd-boys. Speakers: the harlot → Ea; the shepherds among themselves.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Harlot → Ea: he is handsome, [just like a god]; why does he [roam the wild like the animals]?
  2. Harlot, continued: he should dress [in a garment], tie on a waistband, arm himself and [*become*] warrior-like; why does he live …? why roam the wild like [an animal]?
  3. Harlot, continued: he ought to see Uruk …, where the people live and [Anu] dwells and the young men wear [*waistbands*]; something about beds every night …; something about festive food …; he no longer belongs in [the shepherdless] wild.
  4. He hears her, and her words please him; a woman's advice [goes to his heart].
  5. She takes off her garment, [clothes him in] one part and puts the other part [on herself].
  6. He puts on the garment …; he rubs himself with oil …
  7. Hand in hand, she [guides him like a god] to the camp of the shepherds [and its sheep-pen].
  8. The shepherds see him and …; they [throw themselves down] before Ea as if he were a god; they bow low [to him]; the herd-boys abase themselves [before him].
  9. Shepherds: the fellow [has] Sîn's [build] but is squatter, [and heavier of] bone; they decide he is [upland]-born.
  10. At night, as the shepherds lie asleep, sleep overcomes them …; Ea keeps watch for them, [awake]; something he kept is lost.
  11. [He is enjoying himself] with Shamhat; what he did not do is lost (the tablet breaks off).
- **Specifics:** garment, waistband, weapon, oil; Uruk (the one place George says keeps its name), "the dwelling [of Anu]"; the shepherds' camp; the herd-boys; Sîn's likeness; "squatter of stature"; the night watch; Shamhat.
- **Short quotations:** "before Ea as before a god" (Sealand 80).
- **Gaps:** a gap of fifteen lines before line 58; many words restored, as shown; lines 61, 63, 65, 68, 69, 75, 76, 79, 87, 89 and 91 break off.
- **Repetitions:** "why do you ... roam the wild" twice (lines 59, 64); the shepherds' homage is said three ways (prostrate, bow down, humble themselves).
- **Compared with the standard version:** the Pennsylvania tablet (chapter 3 file, OB.6-OB.8) and SB I 207-208 and 226-227, SB II 36-62 (SB's own text where it survives). Differences:
  - Order of the civilizing: here the harlot's invitation itself tells him to dress, wear a waistband, take a weapon and be like a warrior, and he dresses and oils himself before he reaches the camp. In P the barber grooms and oils him and he takes a garment and weapon at the camp, after the bread and ale.
  - Missing here: the bread and ale and the barber. The text runs from the shepherds' remark (line 85) straight to the night watch (line 86) with no gap, so these episodes are not in this version.
  - Added here: the shepherds fall prostrate and the herd-boys humble themselves before Ea "as before a god." No counterpart in P or SB.
  - The shepherds' remark: "squatter" (Sealand) vs. "shorter" (P) vs. "tall" (SB II 41); no line about animals' milk.
  - Young men in waistbands: as in Shamhat's second speech in SB (I 227); not in P.
  - Places: Uruk survives here (George's "one exception"), though the dreams are set in Ur.
  - Names: Shamhat (the later form) vs. P's Shamkatum.
  - The same as P: Ea as the shepherds' watchman, then his lovemaking with the harlot.

- **Ishtar:** hears the brides' complaint (M.1); her temple in Uruk (M.2); offers Gilgamesh wealth, is scorned, asks for the Bull (M.7-M.10).

- The night watch over the flocks (M.15).

## Open questions and uncertainties
1. **Ugarit's order (M.1-M.3):** George suggests the jumbled order "may be the outcome of a faulty memory" (p. 197); the last line (38) is broken.
2. **Bo1 (a) and (d) (M.4-M.5):** almost all the text is restored from the Pennsylvania and Yale tablets, so the differences rest on a few plain words. In Bo1 (a) the harlot's destination ("the site of the sheep-pen") is plain but "[to the shepherds' camp,]" is restored.
3. **Bo2 (M.6):** whether George's "paraphrase ... from Hattusa" in the SB IV note (p. 30) is this tablet is not stated; Enkidu's explanation breaks off before naming the man.
4. **Emar (M.7-M.10):** most of the text is restored from SB, so absences (the donkey and horse, the palace and boulder, the lion, horse and Ishullanu, the Netherworld threat, the chaff and hay) are not certain; the Sutean episode is broken.
5. **Megiddo (M.11-M.12):** who speaks the judgment of the dream is not named; what Enkidu "cut down" is lost; the friend's lack of fear and loyalty rest on restorations; Humbaba and the Bull are restored; "Gilgamesh sobbed" is restored.
6. **Sealand (M.13-M.15):** the reason for the names Sîn, Ea and Ur is unknown (George, p. 206); the harlot's first speech and the name Ea in lines 1-4 are restored; fifteen lines are lost after line 42; whether the dream was told inside the harlot's speech depends on George's punctuation.

## Leads (observations about the text's own structure only)
- **The same scenes recur across sites:** the bread-and-ale scene (Pennsylvania, Bo1 (a), SB) is missing from the Sealand tablet; the mountain dream is first in Schøyen2 but second in Bo2 and SB.
- **Phrase pairs that travel:** "fit for a god ... fit for a king" for bread and ale appears in Bo1 (a) (M.4) and in SB VI and VII, but not in the Pennsylvania tablet.
- **The deeds list** (Cedar Forest, Humbaba, Bull of Heaven) is spoken by Enkidu in Megiddo (M.11) but by Gilgamesh in SB VIII and X.
- **Water given in a dream:** Bo2's lion-like man gives Gilgamesh water (M.6), like the Harmal dream's Lugalbanda (chapter 3 file, OB.27).
- **Ugarit repeats couplets with the name added** (M.1, M.2), the same device as SB I 1-4.
