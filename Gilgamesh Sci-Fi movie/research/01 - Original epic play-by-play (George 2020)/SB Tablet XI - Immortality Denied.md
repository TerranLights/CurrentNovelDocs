# Tablet XI. Immortality Denied: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

## George's synopsis (p. 85)
Gilgamesh wants to know how Uta-napishti came to live forever and hears the story: Uta-napishti came through the Flood, and the gods made him immortal for it. Uta-napishti then dares him to go a week without sleep. Gilgamesh fails, and sees in despair that a man who cannot beat Sleep has no chance against Death. Uta-napishti has his ferryman wash Gilgamesh, dress him in clothes more fit for a king, and take him home to Uruk. His wife urges that the departing guest be given the usual parting gift, so Uta-napishti reveals that a coral-like plant on the sea floor can make a man young again. Gilgamesh dives down, brings it up, and leaves for Uruk with Ur-shanabi. When he stops to bathe in an inviting pool, a snake takes advantage of his carelessness and makes off with the "plant." Gilgamesh knows he can never find the place where he dived again, and at last understands that all his labor has come to nothing; he would have done better never to meet Uta-napishti. Reaching Uruk with Ur-shanabi, he echoes the prologue: he tells the ferryman to go up on the walls, which will be his lasting monument, and look out over the human community that keeps the city alive forever.

## Scenes
Key: "restored" means the words are in George's square brackets; "conjectural" means his italic square brackets (his own guess to fill the context); "uncertain" means his italics on surviving text (insecure reading or rendering). Line numbers are George's margin numbers (printed every five lines); numbers in between are counted from the nearest marker, and "about" marks a count that cannot be fixed exactly (lost lines, or one verse printed as two lines or two as one). Scenes XI.2 to XI.9 are Uta-napishti's first-person story, told to Gilgamesh; everything in them is retold, not shown.

### XI.1 "You are just like me": Gilgamesh asks how Uta-napishti found life (XI 1-7; pp. 85-86)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's home, far off where the rivers flow out (XI 205-206), straight after his speech on Death at the end of Tablet X.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Uta-napishti.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti (called "the Distant"): looking at him, he sees nothing different in his shape; Uta-napishti is exactly like him (XI 1-4).
  2. Gilgamesh: he had come fully set on forcing a fight, but face to face with Uta-napishti his hand is held back (XI 5-6).
  3. Gilgamesh: how did Uta-napishti come to stand among the gods in their assembly and win eternal life? (XI 7).
- **Specifics:** the epithet "the Distant" (XI 1); no difference in form; a fight intended and given up; the gods' assembly; eternal life.
- **Short quotation:** "how couldst thou / Stand in th' Assemblage of Gods to petition for life (everlasting)?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 49).
- **Gaps:** none; fully preserved. George cites XI 5-6 as Gilgamesh owning up that he had meant to take the secret by force (p. l).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** XI 5-6 quite differently: Uta-napishti is fit and ready for fighting, yet lies at rest on his back (Tablet 11, p. 49); there is no confession by Gilgamesh.
- **Repetitions:** "just like me" is said twice (XI 3-4). XI 7 echoes what Gilgamesh told the scorpion-man about Uta-napishti sitting in the gods' assembly and finding life (IX 76). With Shiduri and Ur-shanabi his question was where the road lies (X 73, 150); here it is how it was done (XI 7).

### XI.2 The secret of the gods: Shuruppak, the gods' oath, and Ea's message through the reed fence (XI 8-31; p. 86)
- **Where and when:** the frame is Uta-napishti's home. The story is set in Shuruppak, long ago, when the great gods resolved to send the Deluge.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti tells it to Gilgamesh. Inside the story: the great gods (Anu, Enlil, Ninurta, Ennugi, Ea) and Uta-napishti; Ea speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: he will reveal a closely guarded matter, one of the gods' secrets (XI 8-10).
  2. *Retold:* Shuruppak, a city Gilgamesh knows, lies on the bank of the Euphrates. It was an ancient city, once home to the gods, when the great gods made up their minds to bring the Deluge (XI 11-14).
  3. *Retold:* the oath was sworn by Anu, their father; by Enlil, the hero, their counselor; by Ninurta, their chamberlain; and by Ennugi, their sheriff (XI 15-18).
  4. *Retold:* Ea, the prince, swore along with them, but then repeated what they had said to a reed fence (XI 19-20).
  5. *Retold speech,* Ea → the reed fence and the brick wall: he calls on fence and wall in turn to listen and take note (XI 21-22).
  6. Ea, through the fence → the man of Shuruppak, Ubar-Tutu's son: tear the house down and make a boat of it; let wealth go and look to survival; give up possessions and save life; load the seed of every living thing aboard (XI 23-27).
  7. Ea: the boat must be built with all its measurements equal, as long as it is broad, and roofed over the way the Ocean Below is covered (XI 28-31).
- **Specifics:** Shuruppak (modern Fara, between Nippur and Uruk, Glossary p. 224); the Euphrates; the gods once lived there; Anu, father; Enlil, hero and counselor; Ninurta, chamberlain; Ennugi, sheriff; Ea, prince; an oath; a reed fence; a brick wall; the man of Shuruppak, Ubar-Tutu's son; house; boat; the seed of all living things; length equal to breadth; a roof modeled on the Ocean Below.
- **Short quotation:** "O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! / Wall, wall! Hearken, O Reed-hut, consider, O Wall!" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 50).
- **Gaps:** none; fully preserved.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** a reed-hut, not a reed fence; Ninurta as the gods' herald and Ennugi as their leader (?); Ea under the name Nin-igi-azag; and a boat whose beam and length must be "in due correspondence," to be launched on the deep, in place of George's equal sides and roof modeled on the Ocean Below (Tablet 11, p. 50).
- **Repetitions:** Uta-napishti's opening promise to reveal a hidden matter and a secret of the gods (XI 9-10) is repeated almost word for word when he tells of the plant (XI 281-282). Ea calls on fence and wall twice within XI 21-22. George's background: in the Atram-hasis poem the gods, Ea included, swore to keep the Deluge secret, and Ea warned Atram-hasis by a trick (p. xlv).

### XI.3 Uta-napishti asks what to tell his city; Ea's cover story (XI 32-47; pp. 86-87)
- **Where and when:** Shuruppak, in the story, straight after Ea's message.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti and Ea, both speaking, as Uta-napishti retells it.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold,* Uta-napishti → Ea (his master): he has understood and will obey; but what is he to tell his city, the people and the elders? (XI 32-35).
  2. *Retold,* Ea → Uta-napishti (his servant): this is what he must say to them (XI 36-38).
  3. Ea, supplying the words (a speech inside a speech inside a speech): Enlil has turned against him, so he can no longer live in their city or set foot on (restored) Enlil's land; he must (restored) go down to the Ocean Below and live with his master Ea, who will then rain plenty on them: plenty (restored) of birds, a "riddle" (uncertain) of fish, a rich harvest (verb conjectural), bread-cakes showered down in the morning and wheat pouring down in the evening (XI 39-47).
- **Specifics:** the city, the crowd, the elders; Enlil's land; the Ocean Below; rain of plenty; birds; fish; harvest; bread-cakes at morning; wheat at evening.
- **Short quotation:** "’Tis me alone (?) whom Enlil so hateth that I in your city / No (more) may dwell" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 50).
- **Gaps:** restored: "on" (XI 41), "I must" (XI 42), the opening word for plenty (XI 44). Uncertain: "riddle" (XI 44). Conjectural: the verb of providing (XI 45).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the promise as plenty of fowl and fish, a big harvest and a "plentiful rainfall (?)," with no bread-cakes and no wheat, and marks two of these lines as difficult (Tablet 11, p. 50 and n. 3).
- **Repetitions:** the bread-cakes-and-wheat couplet (XI 46-47) returns twice as the sign of the time the Sun God set (XI 88, 91). George's introduction says that in the Atram-hasis tradition the ark was built on the pretext of sailing down to Ea's domain, the Ocean Below (pp. xlv-xlvi).

### XI.4 Building the boat (XI 48-80; pp. 87-88)
- **Where and when:** at Atra-hasis's gate (Atra-hasis is Uta-napishti), from the first light of dawn. The hull is in place by the fifth day; on the last day the oiling begins at sunrise and the boat is finished before sunset.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti (narrating as "I"); the land, gathered and named by trade and class: carpenter, reed-worker, shipwright (conjectural), young men, old men, rich man, poor man; his workmen and porters. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* at first light the whole land gathers at Atra-hasis's gate (XI 48-49).
  2. *Retold:* the carpenter brings his hatchet, the reed-worker his stone, and the shipwright (conjectural) his heavy axe; the young men something (lost); the old men bring palm-fiber ropes; the rich man brings the pitch, the poor man some tackle (a word lost) (XI 50-56).
  3. *Retold:* by the fifth day he has the hull in place: an acre in area, with sides ten rods high and the roof's sides ten rods each. He sets up her body and draws her plan (XI 57-59).
  4. *Retold:* he gives her six decks, making seven levels, and nine compartments inside; he drives in the bilge plugs and sees to the punting-pole and the tackle (XI 60-65).
  5. *Retold:* he pours three myriad measures of pitch into a kiln and three myriad of tar (verb restored) inside; porters fetch three myriad of oil; one myriad of oil goes in "libations" (uncertain), and the shipwright stores away two myriad more (XI 66-70).
  6. *Retold:* he slaughters oxen and, every day, lambs for his workers, and gives them beer, ale, oil and wine (the giving verb restored) as freely as river water; they feast as at New Year (XI 71-75).
  7. *Retold:* at sunrise (conjectural) he starts the oiling; before (restored) sunset the boat is done (XI 76-77).
  8. *Retold:* something (lost) was very hard: they shift slipway poles from back to front until (conjectural) two-thirds of the boat is in the water (conjectural) (XI 78-80).
- **Specifics:** the name Atra-hasis ("Surpassing Wise," an epithet of Uta-napishti, Glossary p. 221) (XI 49); hatchet; stone; heavy axe; palm-fiber ropes; pitch; tackle; the fifth day; one acre; sides ten rods; roof sides ten rods; six decks, seven levels; nine compartments; bilge plugs; punting-pole; a kiln; three myriad pitch, three myriad tar, three myriad oil; one myriad oil in libations; two myriad stored by the shipwright; oxen; lambs daily; beer, ale, oil, wine; New Year; sunrise to sunset; slipway poles; two-thirds.
- **Short quotation:** "Sevenfold did I divide her . . . ., divided her inwards / Ninefold" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 51).
- **Gaps:** "his" in XI 50-51 is restored. The shipwright in XI 52 is conjectural, so his appearance in this line is George's filling. XI 53 is broken; XI 56 has a lost word; "libations" (XI 69) is uncertain; the verbs in XI 67 and XI 74 are restored; "rise" (XI 76) is conjectural and "before" (XI 77) restored; XI 78 opens with a loss; in XI 80 "until" and the boat's entering the water are conjectural. Between XI 55 and XI 65 the printed lines do not fall exactly on the margin numbers (XI 60 is printed against the six decks); George's note on translation explains such mismatches (p. lv).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the children, not the rich man, as bringing the pitch; sides "Ten gar each" and a deck "Ten gar to match," with no acre; the hull "six times cross-pinn'd"; six _shar_ of bitumen (variant three) on the hull, three of pitch inside, three of grease brought by porters, one _shar_ used up by "the tackling (?)," and two stored by the boatman (Tablet 11, pp. 50-51).
- **Repetitions:** the first light of dawn opens both this scene (XI 48) and the storm (XI 97). Ten rods is given for both the sides and the roof (XI 58-59). "Three myriad" is said three times (XI 66-68).

### XI.5 Loading the boat; the appointed time; the hatch is sealed (XI 81-96; p. 88)
- **Where and when:** at the boat, after the launch. The moment is the one the Sun God set, signaled by bread-cakes in the morning and wheat in the evening.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti (narrating); his kin; Puzur-Enlil the shipwright. No one speaks except in the quoted signal.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* he loads everything he owns (restored) aboard: all his silver, all his gold, every living creature he has (XI 81-84).
  2. *Retold:* he puts aboard his whole family and kin, the animals of the steppe, the wild creatures, and skilled workers of every craft (XI 85-86).
  3. *Retold:* the moment the Sun God had fixed, given by the bread-cakes-and-wheat signal and the order to get in and seal the hatch, has now arrived (XI 87-91).
  4. *Retold:* he studies the weather. It looks threatening, so he boards and seals the hatch (XI 92-94).
  5. *Retold:* to Puzur-Enlil, the shipwright who sealed the boat, he hands over his palace and everything in it (XI 95-96).
- **Specifics:** silver; gold; living creatures; family and kin; animals of the steppe; wild creatures; craftsmen of every skill; the Sun God's appointed time; the hatch; Puzur-Enlil ("Protected by Enlil," the boatbuilder; the name can also be read Puzur-Amurru, Glossary p. 224); the palace and its goods.
- **Short quotation:** "(Then) do thou enter the vessel, and (straightway) shut down thy hatchway." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 51).
- **Gaps:** XI 81's opening is restored. Otherwise preserved.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the Sun-god's signal as a night of plentiful rain (difficult lines), and has Puzur-Amurri, "the boatman," receive "the poop (of the ship), besides its equipment," not a palace (Tablet 11, p. 51).
- **Repetitions:** four lines run "all the … I had, I loaded aboard" (XI 81-84). The bread-cakes-and-wheat couplet is quoted twice here (XI 88, 91), after Ea's first use of it (XI 46-47). George's introduction notes that the craftsmen and animals taken aboard explain how crafts and animal life survived the Flood (p. li).

### XI.6 The storm breaks; the gods take fright; Belet-ili's lament (XI 97-127; p. 89)
- **Where and when:** over the land, from the first light of dawn (XI 97); a day of gale (XI 110, largely conjectural), then the Deluge. Uta-napishti is shut inside the boat.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti (narrating); the gods Adad, Shullat, Hanish, Errakal, Ninurta, the Anunnaki and Belet-ili; the people. Belet-ili speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* at first light a black cloud rises on the horizon, with Adad the Storm God roaring inside it. Shullat and Hanish march ahead of him, carrying his throne over hill and plain (XI 97-101).
  2. *Retold:* Errakal tears out the mooring-posts; Ninurta comes past and makes the weirs overflow; the Anunnaki hold up blazing torches and scorch the land with their glare (XI 102-105).
  3. *Retold:* the Storm God's deathly stillness passes across the sky and every bright thing goes dark. He (restored) charges the land like a bull (uncertain) on the rampage (conjectural) and breaks it to pieces (it restored) like a clay pot (conjectural) (XI 106-109).
  4. *Retold:* for a day the gale flattens the country (conjectural); the winds blow fast, and then (conjectural) comes the Deluge (conjectural). The catastrophe (restored) sweeps over the people like a battle; no one can make out anyone else, and in the destruction people cannot be recognized (XI 110-113).
  5. *Retold:* even the gods are terrified by the Deluge. They withdraw up to Anu's heaven and lie curled up like dogs out in the open (XI 114-116).
  6. *Retold:* the goddess screams like a woman in labor; sweet-voiced Belet-ili wails (XI 117-118).
  7. *Retold speech,* Belet-ili (lament; no listener named): the days of old have turned to clay because she spoke for evil in the gods' assembly. How could she have done it, and declared war to wipe out her own people? She is the one who gives them birth; they are hers; and now they float in the sea like so many fish (XI 119-124).
  8. *Retold:* the Anunnaki weep with her (restored), faces wet with grief, lips dry and burning with fever (XI 125-127).
- **Specifics:** dawn; a black cloud; Adad, the Storm God, roaring; Shullat and Hanish carrying his throne; Errakal (a form of Nergal as a god of wanton devastation, Glossary p. 222); mooring-posts; Ninurta; weirs; the Anunnaki with fire-torches; flashes of light; darkness; a bull (uncertain); a clay pot (conjectural); one day of gale; a battle; Anu's heaven; dogs; a woman giving birth; Belet-ili ("Lady of the Gods," the Mother Goddess, Glossary p. 221); the gods' assembly; fish; parched, fevered lips. Figure 12 (p. 89): a detail of a seventh-century BC wall relief from the North Palace at Nineveh, British Museum ME 124931 (List of Figures), captioned with XI 124.
- **Short quotation:** "Cow'ring like curs were the gods" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 52).
- **Gaps:** uncertain: the bull (XI 108). Conjectural: the rampage (XI 108), the clay pot (XI 109), the flattening of the country, "then came" and the Deluge itself in XI 110. Restored: "He" (XI 108), "it" (XI 109), the catastrophe (XI 111), "with her" (XI 126). George uses six lines of this passage (XI 102-107) as the third worked example in his appendix, rebuilt from three Nineveh manuscripts, MSS C, J and T (pp. 211, 213-215); he points out the repeated consonants r-k-l in XI 102 and the heavy alliteration on m in XI 105 and 107, which he likens to thunder rumbling on the horizon (p. 220).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "Nabu and Sharru" as the gods who lead the storm, not Shullat and Hanish, and "Irragal" as the one who pulls the bollards; the goddess who cries out like a woman in travail is Ishtar, and her lament wishes "that day" would turn to dust (Tablet 11, pp. 51-52). George has Belet-ili.
- **Repetitions:** "turned to clay" (XI 119) is used of the people again at XI 135, and of Enkidu in Tablet X (X 68, 145, 245). The woman-in-labor simile returns for the ocean (XI 132). George notes that in Tablet XI the poet likens the gods to dogs and to flies (p. xxxv); the flies come at XI 163.

### XI.7 Six days and seven nights; the calm; Mount Nimush; the three birds (XI 128-156; pp. 90-91)
- **Where and when:** the boat on the flood, then aground on Mount Nimush. Durations as stated: the storm blows for six days and seven (restored) nights and stops on the seventh day; the boat is held on Nimush for six days, counted in pairs; the birds go out on the seventh day after that.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti (narrating); a dove, a swallow, a raven. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* wind, downpour, gale and Deluge flatten the land, six days and seven nights long (XI 128-129).
  2. *Retold:* on the seventh day the gale eases and the Deluge ends (uncertain verb). The sea, which had been heaving like a woman in labor, settles; the storm goes quiet (XI 130-133).
  3. *Retold:* he looks out at the weather. All is still, but every human being has turned to clay, and the flooded plain lies as flat as a rooftop (XI 134-136).
  4. *Retold:* he opens a vent and sunlight falls on his cheek. He sinks to his knees and sits weeping, tears streaming down his face (XI 137-139).
  5. *Retold:* scanning the horizon at the sea's edge, he sees land rising in fourteen places (XI 140-141).
  6. *Retold:* the boat grounds on Mount Nimush, which grips it and will not let it move, for one day and a second, a third and a fourth, a fifth and a sixth (XI 142-146).
  7. *Retold:* on the seventh day he sends out a dove. It flies off but returns, finding nowhere to settle (XI 147-150).
  8. *Retold:* he sends out a swallow; it too comes back for the same reason (XI 151-153).
  9. *Retold:* he sends out a raven. It sees the water going down, finds food, keeps "bowing and bobbing" (uncertain), and never returns (XI 154-156).
- **Specifics:** six days and seven nights; the seventh day; wind, rain, gale, Deluge; the sea likened to a woman in labor; the people turned to clay; the plain flat as a roof; a vent; sunlight on his cheek; land in fourteen places; Mount Nimush (a high Zagros peak, probably Pir Omar Gudrun near Suleimaniyah in southern Kurdistan, Glossary p. 223); six days aground; the seventh day; dove, swallow, raven.
- **Short quotation:** "One day, (nay,) two, did Nisir hold fast, nor give lease to her shifting." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 52).
- **Gaps:** "seven" (XI 128) is restored; the verb for the Deluge ending (XI 131) and "bowing and bobbing" (XI 156) are uncertain. Otherwise preserved.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** land rising "at twelve (points)" (George: fourteen) and names the mountain Nisir (George: Nimush); his raven eats "as she waded (and) splash'd" (Tablet 11, p. 52).
- **Repetitions:** the line about Nimush holding the boat fast is said four times (XI 143-146), counting the days in pairs. The bird release follows one pattern three times (dove, swallow, raven: XI 148-156); the first two match word for word. The weeping line (XI 139) is used again of Gilgamesh after the snake (XI 309). George notes that the text explains in passing why doves and swallows stay near people and ravens do not (p. xvi).

### XI.8 The sacrifice; the gods gather; Belet-ili's necklace; Enlil's anger; Ea's rebuke (XI 157-198; pp. 91-92)
- **Where and when:** on the mountain peak (Nimush), after the raven fails to come back.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti (narrating); the gods; Belet-ili, Enlil, Ninurta and Ea speak. The Igigi are the target of Enlil's rage.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* he brings out an offering and sacrifices toward the four winds, setting incense on the summit. He sets out seven flasks and seven more, with reed, cedar and myrtle heaped beneath (XI 157-160).
  2. *Retold:* the gods catch the smell, the sweet smell, and swarm like flies around the one who is sacrificing (XI 161-163).
  3. *Retold:* Belet-ili arrives at once and holds up the lapis lazuli flies, Anu's courtship gift to her (XI 164-165).
  4. *Retold speech,* Belet-ili → the gods: by the big beads of this necklace she will keep these days in mind and never let them slip from memory. Every god may come to the incense except Enlil, because he brought the Deluge without thinking and sent her people to destruction (XI 166-171).
  5. *Retold:* Enlil arrives at once. Seeing the boat, he is furious and rages at the Igigi gods: how did any living being get away? No one was supposed to survive the destruction! (XI 172-176; "From" restored).
  6. *Retold speech,* Ninurta → Enlil (called the hero): only Ea could have arranged this; Ea is the one who knows how everything is done (XI 177-180).
  7. *Retold speech,* Ea → Enlil: he calls Enlil the gods' sage and hero, and asks how he could bring on the Deluge so thoughtlessly. The sinner should bear his own sin, the wrongdoer his own wrong (XI 181-186).
  8. Ea (quoting a saying): ease off before it breaks, pull tight before it goes slack (the last word restored) (XI 187).
  9. Ea → Enlil: rather than a Deluge, he could have sent a lion or a wolf to thin out the people, or a famine, or a rising of the Plague God to strike the land down (XI 188-195).
  10. Ea → Enlil: he did not give away the great gods' secret; he let Atra-hasis see a vision, and that is how he found out. It is now for them to settle his fate (XI 196-198).
- **Specifics:** an offering; the four winds; incense on the peak; seven and seven flasks; reed, cedar and myrtle; the sweet smell; flies; the lapis lazuli flies Anu made when courting her; the big beads of her necklace; the Igigi (the great gods of heaven, Glossary p. 222); a living being; Enlil called hero and, by Ea, the gods' sage; a lion; a wolf; famine; the Plague God; Atra-hasis; a vision.
- **Short quotation:** "the gods did assemble like flies o'er him making the off'ring" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 53).
- **Gaps:** "From" (XI 175) and the last word of the saying (XI 187) are restored. Otherwise preserved.
- **Dreams and omens:** Ea's claim that the warning came as a vision granted to Atra-hasis (XI 197) is the only dream-like element in the flood story; as told in XI.2, the warning is spoken to a reed fence.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "Unto the four winds (of heaven) I freed (all the beasts)" before the offering and libation, so the animals are let go here; the goddess is "the Queen (of the gods)," holding up jewels Anu made "in accord with her wishes" and swearing by a "necklet of sapphires"; Ea also pleads for mercy for the sinner; a jackal stands where George has a wolf; and Ea says he "vouchsafed … a dream," not a vision (Tablet 11, pp. 52-53).
- **Repetitions:** the gods smelling the savor is said twice (XI 161-162). Belet-ili (XI 164) and Enlil (XI 172) each "arrived at once." The charge that Enlil brought the Deluge without counsel is made by Belet-ili (XI 170) and by Ea (XI 184). The speech formula for Ninurta and Ea addressing Enlil is used twice (XI 177-178, 181-182). Ea's "instead of the Deluge" comes four times (XI 188, 190, 192, 194), in two pairs: the first pair ends with the people thinned out, the second with the land struck down. George's account of the Atram-hasis background: the gods, starving because the people who fed them were dead, rushed down at the smell of the offering (p. xlvi).

### XI.9 Enlil blesses Uta-napishti and his wife; they are settled far away (XI 199-206; p. 92)
- **Where and when:** at the boat on the mountain, straight after Ea's speech; then the move to the far-off place. This ends Uta-napishti's story.
- **Who:** Enlil (speaks), Uta-napishti, his wife; an unnamed "they" who take him away.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. *Retold:* Enlil climbs into the boat, takes him by the hand and leads him out. He brings out his wife too and has her kneel beside him, then stands between them and touches their foreheads in blessing (XI 199-202).
  2. *Retold speech,* Enlil (blessing; no listener named): Uta-napishti used to be a mortal man; from now on he and his wife will be like the gods, and his home will be a distant place at the rivers' outflow (XI 203-205).
  3. *Retold:* so they carry him far off and settle him where the rivers flow out (XI 206).
- **Specifics:** Enlil's hand; the wife kneeling; foreheads touched; Enlil standing between them; made like the gods; a far place where the rivers flow out. George's introduction puts it on a remote island where the Euphrates and Tigris well up again from the deep (p. xxxiv).
- **Short quotation:** "'Uta-Napishtim hath hitherto only been mortal, / Now, indeed, Uta-Napishtim and (also) his wife shall be equal / Like to us gods'" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 53).
- **Gaps:** none.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the place as "at the mouth of the rivers" (Tablet 11, pp. 53-54), matching George.
- **Repetitions:** the far place where the rivers flow out is named twice (XI 205-206). George notes that in the Gilgamesh version Ea asks the gods to decide the survivor's fate and Enlil grants immortality, whereas the Atram-hasis poem goes on to Ea's answer to the problem of human noise (p. xlvi).

### XI.10 The test of sleep: six days and seven nights (XI 207-246; pp. 93-94)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's home, straight after the flood story. The test is to stay awake six days and seven nights; Gilgamesh sleeps through the baking of seven daily loaves.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti, his wife and Gilgamesh; all three speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: who will call the gods together in assembly for him now, so that he can find the life he is searching for? Let him try to stay awake six days and seven nights running (XI 207-209).
  2. The moment Gilgamesh squats down, sleep drifts over him like a mist (XI 210-211).
  3. Uta-napishti → his wife: look at this man who wanted life so badly; sleep is already drifting over him like a mist (XI 212-214).
  4. Wife → Uta-napishti (called "the Distant"): wake him up! Let him go home safely the way he came, back through the gate he came out by (XI 215-218).
  5. Uta-napishti → wife: people are liars, and he will lie to her. She should bake him a loaf for each day, set the loaves by his head, and keep count of his days asleep on the wall (XI 219-222).
  6. She does it: a loaf a day by his head, and each day marked on the wall (XI 223-224).
  7. The first loaf has dried out; the second has gone leathery; the third is soggy; the fourth has turned white; the fifth has gray mold; the sixth is freshly baked; the seventh is still on the coals. Then Uta-napishti touches him, and he wakes (XI 225-230).
  8. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: he had hardly dozed off when Uta-napishti touched him awake (XI 231-233). George reads this as a lie that proves Uta-napishti's point about human deceit (p. xli).
  9. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: come (uncertain), count the loaves and see how many days he slept (restored); he runs through the seven loaves again, and says only then did he wake him (about XI 234-242).
  10. Gilgamesh → Uta-napishti: what is he to do, where can he go? A thief has seized his flesh (conjectural); Death lives in his bedroom, and wherever he turns, Death will be waiting (about XI 243-246).
- **Specifics:** who will convene the gods' assembly for him; six days and seven nights; squatting on his haunches; sleep compared to a mist (George: fog); a loaf a day; loaves by his head; days marked on the wall; the loaves' states (dry; leathery; soggy; white; gray mold; fresh; on the coals); a thief; the bedroom.
- **Short quotation:** "Death [in] my bed-chamber broodeth, and Death is wherever I [listen]" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 54).
- **Gaps:** "Come" (XI 235) is uncertain; restored: the days he slept (XI 236), the first loaf and its drying (XI 237), the seventh on the coals (XI 241), and "I" (XI 246). The thief's object, his flesh (about XI 244), is conjectural. Between the markers XI 235, 240 and 245 there are six printed lines for every five numbers, so lines in that stretch are counted only approximately.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** sleep coming "like a breeze"; Uta-napishti's answer to his wife is that the troubles of mortals trouble her too, not that people lie; the seven stages are steps in making bread (the flour gathered, sifted, moistened, kneaded, leavened, baked) rather than loaves going stale, a passage he calls difficult; and the "Robber" takes his courage (Tablet 11, p. 54 and n. 1).
- **Repetitions:** sleep drifting over him like a mist is said twice (XI 211, 214). The list of loaves is given twice (XI 225-229, about 237-241). The order to bake and mark the wall is carried out in the same words (XI 221-222, 223-224). Six days and seven nights matches the Deluge (XI 128) and the weeping for Enkidu (X 58, 135, 235). George calls Sleep the younger brother of Death and says Uta-napishti sets the test knowing Gilgamesh will fail (p. xlvii).

### XI.11 Ur-shanabi is banished; Gilgamesh is washed and given royal robes (XI 247-270; pp. 94-95)
- **Where and when:** Uta-napishti's shore, right after the sleep test; the washtub by the sea.
- **Who:** Uta-napishti speaks to Ur-shanabi; Ur-shanabi and Gilgamesh act.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Uta-napishti → Ur-shanabi (the boatman): may the landing-place turn him away (restored) and the ferry reject him! He who used to come and go on this shore is banished from it now (XI 247-249). No reason is given ⚠️.
  2. Uta-napishti → Ur-shanabi: the man he brought here is covered in matted hair, and animal skins have spoiled the beauty of his body. Ur-shanabi is to take him to the washtub and have him scrub his hair clean (XI 250-254).
  3. Uta-napishti: he should throw off the skins for the sea to carry away and soak his body until it is clean and fair; he should get a new headcloth and put on royal robes worthy of his rank (XI 255-258).
  4. Uta-napishti: until he reaches his city and the end of his journey, the robes must stay spotless and as good as new (XI 259-261).
  5. Ur-shanabi takes him to the washtub. Gilgamesh washes his hair clean and throws off the skins, which the sea carries away; his body is soaked fair; he puts on a new headcloth (restored) and the royal robes (XI 262-267).
  6. The wish for the robes is repeated, in quotation marks with no speaker named (partly restored) (XI 268-270) ⚠️.
- **Specifics:** the landing-place; the ferry; this shore; matted hair; animal skins; the washtub; the sea carrying the skins off; a new headcloth (George: kerchief); royal robes fit for his dignity; robes that stay clean and fresh. George calls them a magic garment that cannot get dirty (p. l).
- **Short quotation:** "No (sign of) age shall the mantle betray, but preserve (all) its freshness." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Gaps:** in XI 248 the opening wish and its verb are restored; in XI 266 the headcloth phrase is restored; XI 268-270 are partly restored.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the washing as making him white "as the snow," a "fillet" renewed on his head and a mantle that betrays no sign of age (Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Repetitions:** the instructions (XI 253-261) are carried out in the same words (XI 262-270), down to the closing wish for the robes.

### XI.12 Departure; the wife asks for a parting gift; the secret of the plant (XI 271-286; p. 95)
- **Where and when:** the shore, as the boat leaves, and then close to shore again.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi in the boat; Uta-napishti's wife and Uta-napishti speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh and Ur-shanabi board the boat, push off, and work it themselves (XI 271-272).
  2. Wife → Uta-napishti (called "the Distant"): Gilgamesh got here through hard labor and hardship; what is Uta-napishti giving him to take home? (XI 273-275).
  3. Gilgamesh takes up a punting-pole and brings the boat back near the shore (XI 276-277). The text does not say whether he heard her ⚠️.
  4. Uta-napishti → Gilgamesh: he came through labor and hardship, so what should he be given for the journey home? Uta-napishti will reveal a hidden matter, a secret of the gods (XI 278-282).
  5. Uta-napishti: a certain plant resembles (verb restored) a box-thorn, with prickles like a "dog rose" (uncertain) that will prick (restored) whoever picks it (conjectural). If he can get hold of it, he will be young again as he once was (all conjectural) (XI 283-286).
- **Specifics:** the boat; a punting-pole; labor and hardship; a gift for the journey home; a hidden matter, a secret of the gods; the plant: like a box-thorn, with prickles like a dog rose (uncertain). George's synopsis calls it a coral-like plant deep under the sea (p. 85), and his introduction says Gilgamesh was remembered as the first to dive for coral (p. xvi).
- **Short quotation:** "There is a plant like a thorn with its root (?) [deep down in the ocean]" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Gaps:** in XI 283 the verb "looks" is restored; in XI 284 "dog rose" is uncertain, the verb of pricking restored, and its object conjectural; XI 286, the plant's promised effect, is conjectural from start to finish. The effect is stated in plain text only by Gilgamesh (XI 296, 299-300) ⚠️.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the wife's words as Gilgamesh arriving "aweary with rowing," and restores the plant's promise as "[thou'lt surely find life (everlasting)]," not youth (Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Repetitions:** XI 271-272 repeat the boarding formula of X 169-170. The wife and then Uta-napishti both speak of his labor and hardship and the gift for the road (XI 274-275, 279-280). XI 281-282 repeat the opening of the flood story (XI 9-10).

### XI.13 The dive for the plant (XI 287-293; p. 95)
- **Where and when:** at the water, as soon as Gilgamesh has heard Uta-napishti. The place is described only through a channel (restored), the Ocean Below and the sea's shore.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The moment he hears this, Gilgamesh opens up a channel (restored); the rest of the line is lost (XI 287-288).
  2. He ties heavy stones to his feet (restored), and they drag him down (a word lost) to the Ocean Below (XI 289-290).
  3. He takes hold of the plant and pulls it up (restored), lifting it (conjectural) (XI 291).
  4. He cuts the heavy stones loose from his feet (restored), and the sea throws him up onto its shore (XI 292-293).
- **Specifics:** a channel (restored); heavy stones; his feet (restored); the Ocean Below (Ea's freshwater ocean beneath the earth, the _Apsû_, Glossary p. 224); the plant; the sea; its shore.
- **Short quotation:** "Bound heavy stones [on his feet], which dragg'd him down to the sea-deeps" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Gaps:** in XI 288 "channel" is restored and the rest of the line lost; "to his feet" (XI 289) and "from his feet" (XI 292) are restored; XI 290 has a lost word; in XI 291 "it up" is restored and the lifting conjectural.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "he loosen'd [his girdle about him]" where George has the channel, and adds that the plant's prickles scratch him as he seizes it (Tablet 11, p. 55).
- **Repetitions:** the heavy stones are mentioned twice (XI 289, 292). The opened channel and the tools left behind come back in his lament (XI 316).

### XI.14 Gilgamesh names the plant and plans to test it (XI 294-300; pp. 95-96)
- **Where and when:** on the shore where the sea threw him up, straight after the dive.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Ur-shanabi.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi (the boatman): this is the "Plant of Heartbeat," and it can give a man back his strength (XI 294-296).
  2. Gilgamesh: he will carry it to Uruk-the-Sheepfold and try it out first by feeding some to an old man (XI 297-298).
  3. Gilgamesh: if the old man becomes young, he will eat it himself and be young again as he used to be (XI 299-300).
- **Specifics:** the name "Plant of Heartbeat"; restored vigor; Uruk-the-Sheepfold; an old man as the test subject; test first, then eat.
- **Short quotation:** "'Greybeard-who-turneth-to-man-in-his-prime' is its name" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Gaps:** none.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the plant as "a plant of great wonder(?)" with the name above, in place of George's "Plant of Heartbeat"; he has no test on an old man: Gilgamesh will take it to Erech, give it to someone to eat (the name is lost), and eat it himself to regain his youth (Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Repetitions:** his hope of being young again (XI 300) is the plain-text version of the effect George conjectures in Uta-napishti's words at XI 286. George calls it the plant of rejuvenation and says Uta-napishti arranges for Gilgamesh to find it knowing he will lose it through his own doing (p. xlvii).

### XI.15 The journey home begins; the pool; the snake (XI 301-307; p. 96)
- **Where and when:** on the road to Uruk. Distances as stated: they stop to eat after twenty leagues and stop for the night after thirty (XI 301-302). The pool comes in the very next line after the night stop; the text does not say outright that it is at the night stop ⚠️.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Ur-shanabi, a snake. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. After twenty leagues they eat; after thirty they halt for the night (XI 301-302).
  2. Gilgamesh comes across a pool of cool water and goes down into it to bathe (XI 303-304).
  3. A snake smells the plant's scent, comes up silently (restored) and carries it off (XI 305-306).
  4. As it turns back, it sheds its skin (XI 307).
- **Specifics:** twenty leagues; thirty leagues; a meal; a night halt; a pool; cool water; bathing; a snake; the plant's scent; a cast-off skin.
- **Short quotation:** "Bathed in the water. (But here was) a serpent who snuff'd the plant's fragrance" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Gaps:** "silence" (XI 306) is restored.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** the stages as hours, not leagues: they break their fast "at the fortieth hour" and rest "at the sixtieth." His serpent snatches the plant "uttering malison / As he drew back," and there is no shedding of skin in his reading (Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Repetitions:** the two travel lines (XI 301-302) come again at XI 319-320, with making camp in place of stopping for the night. George calls the snake that sheds its skin the epic's purest example of myth (p. xxxv) and lists why snakes shed their skins among the things the text explains in passing (p. xvi).

### XI.16 Gilgamesh weeps: the "Lion of the Earth" and the lost landmark (XI 308-318; p. 96)
- **Where and when:** at the pool, right after the theft.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Ur-shanabi.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh sits down and weeps, tears running down his face (XI 308-309).
  2. Gilgamesh → the boatman Ur-shanabi: he asks for whose sake (restored) his arms worked so hard and his heart's blood drained away. He has won nothing for himself; all he has done is a good turn to the "Lion of the Earth" (XI 310-314).
  3. Gilgamesh: the tide is rising far and wide by now. When he opened the channel he left the tools behind; what is there to mark the spot for him? If only he had gone back and left the boat beached! (XI 315-318).
- **Specifics:** tears; his arms; his heart's blood; a reward; the "Lion of the Earth" (by context the snake; the text does not explain the name ⚠️); the tide rising everywhere; the channel; the tools; a landmark; the boat; the shore.
- **Short quotation:** "Would I had turn'd and abandon'd the boat at the marge (of the ocean)!" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Gaps:** XI 310 opens with a lost word, and the verb of speaking is conjectural; "For whom" (XI 311) and "for" (XI 314) are restored.
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "an earth-lion"; he has the thief take the plant "now at the fortieth hour," and Gilgamesh speak of "the sign (?) which to me was vouchsafed as a warning" instead of the rising tide and the lost landmark (Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Repetitions:** his sitting and weeping, tears down his cheeks (XI 308-309), repeat Uta-napishti's words about himself after the Flood (XI 138-139). The landmark echoes his requests to Shiduri and Ur-shanabi for the road's landmark (X 74-75, 151-152). George quotes XI 318 as Gilgamesh's realization that he would have done better never to make the journey (p. xlvii).

### XI.17 Arrival in Uruk: "climb Uruk's wall" (XI 319-328; pp. 96-97)
- **Where and when:** the road, then Uruk-the-Sheepfold, on arrival. Distances: a meal after twenty leagues, camp after thirty (XI 319-320). How many such stages the journey took is not stated ⚠️.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Ur-shanabi.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. After twenty leagues they eat; after thirty they make camp (XI 319-320).
  2. They reach Uruk-the-Sheepfold (XI 321).
  3. Gilgamesh → Ur-shanabi (the boatman): go up on Uruk's wall and walk along it; look over its foundations and inspect the brickwork. Are its bricks not kiln-fired? Was it not the Seven Sages who laid its foundations? (XI 322-326).
  4. Gilgamesh: the city covers one square mile, the date-groves one, the clay-pits one, and Ishtar's temple half, so Uruk spreads over three and a half square miles (XI 327-328). The epic ends here.
- **Specifics:** twenty leagues; thirty leagues; camp; Uruk-the-Sheepfold; the wall; its foundations; the brickwork; kiln-fired bricks; the Seven Sages (sent by Ea at the dawn of history to civilize mankind, Glossary p. 224); 1 square mile city + 1 date-grove + 1 clay-pit + 1/2 temple of Ishtar = 3 1/2 square miles.
- **Short quotation:** "Do thou, Ur-Shanabi, go up and walk on the ramparts of Erech" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Gaps:** none. The last printed marker is XI 325. The square-mile line is printed as one long line; in Tablet I the same line covers two numbers (I 22-23, fixed by the marker I 25 two lines later). If it does here too, the tablet ends at about XI 328 ⚠️, two more than the 326 lines George gives as the longest tablet in his introduction (p. xxx).
- **Thompson 1928 reads** "its ground-work be not bitumen, e'en seven courses" where George has the Seven Sages laying the foundations, and gives the city's parts in _shar_ (city, gardens, a lost item, the temple of Ishtar) with a total of three _shar_ and a lost fraction (Tablet 11, p. 56).
- **Repetitions:** XI 323-328 repeat the prologue's I 18-23 almost word for word. The differences: Tablet XI adds the address to Ur-shanabi; Tablet XI says to survey "its" foundations where Tablet I says "the" foundations; and the square-mile line, partly restored in Tablet I, is fully preserved here. The travel couplet repeats XI 301-302.

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ The plant's promised effect in Uta-napishti's own words (XI 286, being young again) is wholly conjectural; the effect is plain text only in Gilgamesh's speech (XI 296, 299-300). The dog-rose comparison for the prickles is an uncertain rendering (XI 284).
- ⚠️ The poem never calls the plant coral. George's synopsis describes it as a coral-like plant under the sea (p. 85), and his introduction says Gilgamesh was remembered as the first to dive for coral (p. xvi); the text says only that it resembles a box-thorn and is brought up from the Ocean Below.
- ⚠️ The "Lion of the Earth" (XI 314) is not explained in the text; it follows the snake's theft, and George says only the snake is meant to benefit from the plant (p. xlvii).
- ⚠️ No reason is given for Ur-shanabi's banishment (XI 248-249).
- ⚠️ The second statement of the wish for the robes (XI 268-270) is in quotation marks with no speaker named.
- ⚠️ The text does not say whether Gilgamesh hears the wife before he brings the boat back (XI 273-277).
- ⚠️ The shipwright at XI 52 is conjectural; Puzur-Enlil the shipwright at XI 95 is plain text. "Libations" (XI 69) is uncertain.
- ⚠️ The margin numbers and printed lines do not line up exactly in a few stretches (XI 55-65, 105-110, 125-130, 235-245); numbers inside those stretches are approximate.
- ⚠️ Two timings come in pairs that are easy to merge: the storm lasts six days and seven nights and ends on the seventh day; then the boat sits on Nimush for six days and the birds go out on a second seventh day. The text gives no total.
- ⚠️ The tablet's last line number is not printed: after the marker XI 325 come three printed lines (about XI 326-328; see XI.17). George's introduction gives 326 lines as the longest tablet (p. xxx).
- ⚠️ The distance from the pool to Uruk is not given; the twenty-and-thirty-leagues couplet is said twice (XI 301-302, 319-320), with no count of days.
- George reads Gilgamesh's claim that he had barely fallen asleep (XI 232-233) as a lie that bears out Uta-napishti's remark about human deceit (p. xli); the text itself does not call it a lie.

Thompson 1928 has Ishtar for the lament and "the Queen (of the gods)" for the necklace (Tablet 11, pp. 52-53).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the storm lasted six days and nights; George 2020 has six days and seven nights, ending on the seventh day (XI 128-131).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says that when the raven does not return, Utnapishtim opens the ark and frees those aboard; George 2020 has him bring out an offering and sacrifice toward the four winds (XI 157), with no release of the passengers described. Thompson 1928 reads that he "freed (all the beasts)" to the four winds (Tablet 11, p. 52).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), in its Tablet IX section, says Utnapishtim and his wife were the only humans the gods let survive, and that their immortality was a reward in gratitude for food offerings; George 2020 has his whole family and kin and skilled workers aboard (XI 85-86), and Enlil grants immortality after Ea's speech (XI 196-205) with no reason of gratitude stated.
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) calls the god who warns Utnapishtim Enki, his Sumerian name; George 2020's standard text has Ea.
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), in an illustration caption, names Utnapishtim's island Dilmun and calls the plant the herb of eternal life; George 2020's text says only that he lives far off where the rivers flow out (XI 205-206), names no Dilmun, and makes the plant one of renewed youth (XI 296, 299-300). Thompson 1928's restoration of Uta-napishti's first description of the plant does read "life (everlasting)" (Tablet 11, p. 55).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the loss of the plant ends Gilgamesh's chance of immortality; in George 2020 what he loses is the means of becoming young again (XI 299-300), immortality having been ruled out by the sleep test and Uta-napishti's story.

## Leads (observations on the text's own structure only)
- Lead: the flood story (XI 8-206) is told in the first person inside the epic's third-person narration, and it contains speeches within speeches (Ea's words for the people, inside Ea's words to Uta-napishti, XI 38-47).
- Lead: the same opening promise, to reveal a hidden matter and a secret of the gods, introduces both of Uta-napishti's revelations, the Deluge (XI 9-10) and the plant (XI 281-282).
- Lead: six days and seven nights measures the storm (XI 128), the sleep test (XI 209) and, in Tablet X, the weeping for Enkidu (X 58, 135, 235).
- Lead: Uta-napishti's weeping after the Flood (XI 138-139) and Gilgamesh's weeping after the snake (XI 308-309) use the same tear formula.
- Lead: orders followed by their execution in the same words run through the tablet: the wife's baking (XI 221-224), the washing and robes (XI 253-270).
- Lead: counted series recur: the boat's dimensions (XI 57-65), the three myriads (XI 66-68), the six days on Nimush counted in pairs (XI 144-146), the three birds (XI 148-156), the seven loaves (XI 225-229, 237-241), and Ea's four "instead of the Deluge" alternatives (XI 188-195).
- Lead: the last lines (XI 323-328) return to the prologue (I 18-23), so the poem closes on its opening words.

## Tablet XII (George's note, p. 97)
At this point George prints no translation of Tablet XII, only a brief note under the heading "Appendix." In his account, Tablet XII closes the twelve-tablet "Series of Gilgamesh" but does not belong to the epic itself. It renders the second part of the Sumerian poem "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" into Akkadian prose, and he supposes it was attached because its subject fits: it deals with the state of things in the Netherworld, where Gilgamesh, once dead, ruled over the shades. His translation of it comes in chapter 2, after the Sumerian poem.
- Where it is: chapter 2 says the Akkadian version corresponds to the Sumerian poem from line 172 on, and that in it Gilgamesh appeals to the Moon God, as well as to Enlil and Ea (Enki), for help in getting Enkidu back (p. 134). The translation runs from p. 145, where George introduces it as a somewhat different version of lines 172-303, to p. 150.
- The introduction adds that the series has twelve tablets while the epic has eleven, that Tablet XII is a line-by-line prose translation of the second half of one Sumerian Gilgamesh poem, and that most scholars hold it was attached as related material rather than as part of the epic (pp. xxix-xxx).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) adds that in Tablet XII Enkidu is alive again although he died earlier in the epic; George 2020's chapter 2 note is consistent with this, since there Gilgamesh seeks help to rescue Enkidu (p. 134).
- Its play-by-play belongs to the chapter 2 file, `Sumerian 4 - Gilgamesh and the Netherworld (and Tablet XII).md`.
