# Chapter 3. Fragments of Old Versions of the Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 3 (pp. 163-196). A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How this file is organized.** Chapter 3 is not one continuous text. It is a series of separate tablets and fragments, from the Old and Middle Babylonian periods, that George places in the order of the standard version. Each fragment below gets its own heading with George's note on where the tablet is from and how old it is (paraphrased), then its scenes. Scenes are numbered OB.1, OB.2 and so on straight through the chapter. Line numbers belong to each fragment, not to the standard version, except where marked (for example "SB I 30" or "Sealand 40"). Page numbers are the printed pages.

**Notes on the text used.** The margin line numbers come from the printed book (George puts one every five lines, plus the first and last lines of a section after a gap). The prime mark (´) on a line number, such as 20´, means George counts from the first surviving line of a broken column, not from the true start of the tablet. Italics in the book (shown here with ⚠️ or marked "uncertain") mean an insecure decipherment or rendering; italics inside brackets mean an uncertain or conjectural restoration (Note on the Translation, p. lvi). An asterisk before a name (e.g., *Gilgamesh) means George changed the name to its later form for consistency (p. lvii). In older poetry one verse may run over two or three lines of the tablet, so the margin numbers often jump faster than the printed lines (p. lv); line ranges below are therefore given as "about" where they rest on that count. Ellipses (…) mark lost or unreadable words; "* * *" marks a gap of more than one line. George's numbered illustrations (figs. 18-24 in this chapter) have captions that quote lines; they add no text and are not listed.

**Wording and quotations.** Everything below is in our own words, with indirect summary for speeches. Square brackets inside a summary mark content that George restores rather than reads. For the Pennsylvania (P) and Yale (Y) tablets, short quotations come from the public-domain translation of Morris Jastrow Jr. and Albert T. Clay, *An Old Babylonian Version of the Gilgamesh Epic* (1920), cited as "Jastrow and Clay 1920," with the line numbers of their edition (which George's P and Y numbering follows closely). Their 1920 readings often differ from George's; where the difference matters it is noted. Other fragments are paraphrased, with at most a few words of George where the exact wording matters.

**A warning about the comparisons.** Where the standard version's own lines are lost, George fills the gap in chapter 1 with the old tablet's text. In those places the "standard version" parallel is the same Old Babylonian text, not independent evidence. Each comparison below says which case applies: "SB's own text" or "SB gap filled with this same tablet."

## George's synopsis (chapter introduction, p. 163)
The Babylonian Gilgamesh poem is centuries older than its standard version. The pieces in this chapter belong to the Old and Middle Babylonian periods, about the 18th to the 12th centuries BC. They show that much of the poem stayed stable: many lines reappear in the standard version with only small changes of vocabulary, and big departures are rarer. George arranges them in the standard version's order so that, together, they give a skeleton of the poem at a date much closer to when it was composed. (Preface, p. xii: this standard-version arrangement is new in the second edition; early pieces that do not fit it are put in chapter 4.)

## Fragment 1. The Prologue (pp. 163-164)
**The source (George, p. 163):** there is no separate old tablet for it. In George's view the Old Babylonian prologue is preserved inside the standard version from Tablet I 29 onward, so he reprints SB I 29-48 to stand for it. The Pennsylvania tablet shows that the poem's opening words, "Surpassing all other kings," served as its title in the Old Babylonian period.

### OB.1 The paean: "Surpassing all other kings" (SB I 29-48; pp. 163-164)
- **Where and when:** no setting. This is a narrator's hymn of praise, not action.
- **Who:** an unnamed narrator. Gilgamesh is the subject. Named in passing: Lugalbanda, Ninsun, Uta-napishti.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The narrator ranks Gilgamesh above every other king: tall and heroic, Uruk's brave son, a charging wild bull.
  2. In battle he leads from the front and can be relied on at the back; he shields his men like a river embankment and breaks walls like a flood.
  3. His parents: he is Lugalbanda's "wild bull" and was nursed by the goddess Ninsun, the Wild Cow.
  4. His achievements are listed: he cut passes through mountains, dug wells on the upland slopes, crossed the ocean eastward to the sunrise, searched the world for life, forced his way to the faraway Uta-napishti, rebuilt the cult-centers the Flood had destroyed and set up the cosmic rites for mankind.
  5. The narrator asks who could match his kingship and claim the kingly title as he does.
  6. He bore the name Gilgamesh from birth, and he was two parts god to one part man.
- **Specifics:** epithets (towering, heroic, Uruk's brave son, charging wild bull, perfect in strength, tall and fearsome); Ninsun as the Wild Cow; Uta-napishti "the Distant"; the eastern sea; the Flood; the two-thirds/one-third proportion.
- **Short quotations:** "Surpassing all other kings" (I 29), the Old Babylonian title; "two-thirds of him god" (I 48).
- **Gaps:** none.
- **Repetitions:** none within the passage.
- **Compared with the standard version:** this is SB I 29-48 itself, so the wording is the standard version's. No separate Old Babylonian manuscript of the prologue is translated. One small printing difference: the copy on p. 164 begins the well-digging line with "who," and SB I 39 on p. 2 does not. What the old prologue lacked: the standard version puts I 1-28 in front of it (the opening about the one who saw the Deep, the wall of Uruk, the cedar tablet-box), so in George's account that framing came later and the Old Babylonian poem began at "Surpassing all other kings" (p. 163). See also the Ugarit tablet (chapter 4 file, M.1-M.3), which mixes both openings.

## Fragment 2. The Creation of Enkidu: the Nippur school tablet (pp. 164-165)
**The source (George, p. 164):** dug up on Tablet Hill at Nippur in 1949 and kept by the Oriental Institute, University of Chicago. A trainee scribe copied it as a school exercise, probably between the early 14th and late 13th centuries BC, a prosperous time for the city (so it is Middle Babylonian, not Old Babylonian). It is a very short excerpt from the scene in which the gods task the Mother Goddess with making Enkidu as Gilgamesh's match.

### OB.2 The gods call for Aruru (lines about 1-9; pp. 164-165)
- **Where and when:** among the gods; no place or time stated.
- **Who:** an unnamed speaker or speakers (who makes the first speech is not preserved); Aruru; [Anu] (restored, uncertain). Speakers: the unnamed speaker, then [Anu] to Aruru.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. An unnamed speaker proposes sending for Aruru, the great goddess who made the multitude of humans, so that she can make an [*equal*] for him, someone as strong, to compete with him and give Uruk rest.
  2. They send for Aruru, who is called "the sister."
  3. [Anu] (restored) addresses her and repeats the proposal: she made mankind, so let her now make his equal to compete with him and give Uruk rest (the last two lines are entirely restored).
  4. The excerpt stops; what Aruru does is not on the fragment.
- **Specifics:** Aruru, called "the great one" and "the sister"; humanity described as numerous; the goal, rest for Uruk. (Aruru is another name for Belet-ili, the Mother Goddess; Glossary, p. 221.)
- **Short quotations:** "so Uruk may be rested!" (line 4).
- **Gaps:** almost every key word is a restoration. Unrestored: the call to summon her, her title "the great one," the numerous humans, the strength of the one to be made, the competing, the rest for Uruk, the summoning, her title "the sister," the speech formula, and Aruru's name with her making of man (line 7). Restored: Aruru's name in lines 1 and 5; "equal" in line 3 (restored and uncertain); the god's name Anu in line 6 (restored and uncertain); all of lines 8-9.
- **Repetitions:** the proposal (lines 1-4) comes back as the speech to Aruru (lines 7-9), there mostly restored from the first version.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 93-98 (SB's own text). In SB the gods first protest to Anu about Gilgamesh (I 79-92); Anu listens; then the gods summon Aruru with a single speech: since she made mankind, she must now shape the creature Anu has in mind, a match for Gilgamesh's stormy heart, so that the two compete and Uruk gets rest. Differences:
  - Events: Nippur has a proposal, then the summons, then a second speech; SB has the summons and one speech.
  - Names and titles: only Nippur calls Aruru "the sister."
  - Wording of the task: Nippur asks for an equal of equal strength; SB asks her to make what Anu has conceived, a match for his stormy heart.
  - Who speaks: in Nippur [Anu] (uncertain) speaks to Aruru; in SB the gods do.
  - Outcome: Nippur breaks off before she acts; SB goes on to her washing her hands, nipping off clay, throwing it down and making Enkidu in the wild (I 99-104).

## Fragment 3. Enkidu becomes Human: the Cornell tablet (pp. 165-166)
**The source (George, p. 165):** the top part of an Old Babylonian tablet from southern Babylonia, early 18th century BC, held by Cornell University and published in 2018. Only the opening and closing lines survive. It matters because it proves that a version close to both the Pennsylvania tablet (slightly later) and the standard version already existed at this early date. Its opening has the prostitute's invitation after the first week of lovemaking, worded much like SB I 200-214. Its ending, Enkidu's arrival in Uruk, is so close to Pennsylvania lines 174-89 that George does not translate it separately.

### OB.3 Enkidu changed; the harlot's invitation (obverse, lines about 1-10; pp. 165-166)
- **Where and when:** in the wild, right after the first week of lovemaking (George's note). This is the first surviving line of the tablet.
- **Who:** Enkidu; the harlot (not named in these lines). The harlot speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu's legs tire while his herd keeps moving, so he cannot keep up.
  2. He now possesses reason and great understanding.
  3. He kneels and sits before the harlot and, unlike his old self, listens to what she says.
  4. The harlot invites him to come with her to Uruk-the-City: to the holy temple, Anu's home, and to sacred Eanna, Ishtar's home, where Gilgamesh lives in full strength, like a wild bull with [*horns*] (restored, uncertain).
  5. Her words win him over; he was the boldest in all the uplands.
  6. (Not translated by George: the end of the tablet, Enkidu's arrival in Uruk, close to P 174-89; see OB.9.)
- **Specifics:** Uruk-the-City; holy Eanna; Anu's home and Ishtar's home (both restored); the horned wild bull (the horns restored, uncertain).
- **Short quotations:** "stoutest of heart in all the uplands" (line about 10).
- **Gaps:** restored in brackets: the herd's motion, the verb "had," his sitting down, the word "harlot," the temple as Anu's home, the "arise, let me lead you" phrase, Eanna as Ishtar's home, "where Gilgamesh is," the horns (uncertain), the first half of the closing couplet. Unrestored: Enkidu's name (three times), his reason and understanding, Uruk-the-City (twice), holy Eanna, Gilgamesh's strength and the wild-bull simile, her word, and the last line.
- **Repetitions:** the invitation pattern (come, I will take you to Uruk-the-City) recurs in the Pennsylvania tablet's second invitation (OB.6).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 199-214 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Events: Cornell opens with Enkidu already changed; SB first tells how the gazelles ran from him and how he had soiled his pure body (I 197-199). Cornell's harlot begins straight with the invitation; SB's opens by praising his godlike looks and asking why he roams with animals (I 207-208).
  - Wording: his legs tire (Cornell) vs. his legs stand still (SB I 200); "superb" vs. "wide" understanding (I 202); he sits in front of her (Cornell) vs. at her feet, studying her face (SB I 203-204).
  - Places: Cornell has two holy places, Anu's temple and Ishtar's Eanna; SB has one temple shared by Anu and Ishtar (I 210). Cornell "Uruk-the-City"; SB "Uruk-the-Sheepfold" (I 209).
  - Image: a horned wild bull (Cornell) vs. a wild bull lording it over the men (SB I 212).
  - Outcome line: Cornell calls him the boldest in the uplands; SB says he sensed he should look for a friend (I 214). SB then has Enkidu's boast that he will challenge Gilgamesh (I 215-223) and Shamhat's long second speech with the dreams (I 224-298); on Cornell the middle of the tablet is lost, so whether these were there cannot be known ⚠️.

## Fragment 4. The Pennsylvania tablet (P): Gilgamesh dreams of Enkidu; Enkidu in the shepherds' camp; Enkidu arrives in Uruk (pp. 166-172)
**The source (George, p. 166):** known as "Gilgamesh P." It comes from southern Babylonia, probably from the mid-18th century BC, and the University Museum in Philadelphia bought it in 1914. It is the second tablet of an Old Babylonian edition whose ancient title was "Surpassing all other kings." Its text follows SB Tablets I-II, with some differences of wording, and chapter 1 borrows much of it to fill gaps in Tablet II. A fragment in the Suleimaniyah Museum carries nearly the same text and is not given separately. (Jastrow and Clay 1920 published this tablet with the Yale tablet; their title page gives it as the "second tablet," 240 lines.)

**George's synopsis (p. 166), paraphrased:** Gilgamesh tells his mother, the goddess Ninsun, two dreams, and she explains them. Enkidu and the prostitute, called Shamkatum on this tablet, make love a second time, and she urges him to leave the wild for Uruk and a place among people. At a shepherds' camp he is given bread and beer, washed, groomed and clothed, and he becomes the shepherds' night guard. A traveler tells him of Gilgamesh's right to sleep with brides first (*droit de seigneur*), and Enkidu sets off for Uruk. George says that he seems to arrive just as the young men are choosing one of themselves to challenge the king, and that Enkidu presumably takes that part, since he stops the wedding procession and wrestles Gilgamesh. The tablet ends with Enkidu accepting Gilgamesh's kingship as rightful.

### OB.4 Gilgamesh's first dream: the lump of the sky (P about 1-22; p. 167)
- **Where and when:** Gilgamesh with his mother; the dream came during the night. No place is named; the dream itself is set in Uruk.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; his mother (the text calls her only his mother; George's synopsis names her Ninsun). Gilgamesh speaks, then his mother.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh gets up to tell his mother a dream.
  2. Dream (retold by Gilgamesh): that night he was walking, full of vigor, among the young men; the stars came out clearly; a chunk of the sky dropped in front of him; he lifted it but it became too heavy, and pushing would not move it.
  3. Dream, continued: all Uruk crowded around it and the young men kissed its feet; he set his forehead against it while they helped him push, then lifted it and carried it to her.
  4. The mother explains: a man like Gilgamesh has been born in the wild and raised in the uplands; Gilgamesh will see him and be glad, the young men will kiss his feet, and Gilgamesh will hug him and bring him to her.
- **Specifics:** the fallen piece of sky; the stars; the land of Uruk; the young men; bracing with the forehead; the mother's epithet, that she knows everything.
- **Short quotations:** "A meteor(?) of Anu fell upon me" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, l. 7). George reads the same line as a lump "of the sky" (P 7); Anu is the sky god.
- **Gaps:** none in this scene.
- **Repetitions:** the formula introducing the all-knowing mother's reply (P 15 and the line after) recurs at P about 36-38. The kissing of feet in the dream returns in the interpretation, now of the coming man.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 245-272 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Framing: P tells the dream directly, at the start of the tablet; SB places it inside Shamhat's speech to Enkidu, who says Gilgamesh dreamed of him before he left the uplands (I 242-243).
  - The object: a piece of the sky (P) vs. something like a rock from the sky (SB I 248); SB adds an opening line announcing the night's dream (I 246).
  - Events added in P: walking vigorously among the young men; bracing his forehead while they helped him push. Neither is in SB.
  - Events added in SB: the people kiss its feet like an infant's, he loves and embraces it like a wife, sets it at his mother's feet, and she makes it his equal (I 255 to about 258). In P the wife simile belongs only to the second dream, and the "equal" line comes only at the end of the second interpretation (OB.5).
  - The interpretation: P says only that his like was born in the wild and that the young men will kiss his feet. SB's Ninsun retells the dream and promises a powerful comrade who will save his friend, as strong as a rock from the sky, whom he will love like a wife (I about 262-272).
  - Names: P's mother is unnamed; SB calls her Wild-Cow Ninsun, clever and wise (I about 259-260).
  - See also the Sealand tablet's version, with the names Sîn and Ur (chapter 4 file, M.13).

### OB.5 Gilgamesh's second dream: the axe (P about 23-38, Sealand 39-41, P about 42-43; pp. 167-168)
- **Where and when:** the same night or soon after: he lies down again and dreams. He then gets up and tells his mother.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; his mother. Gilgamesh speaks, then his mother.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh sleeps, dreams again, gets up and tells his mother he has had a second dream.
  2. Dream (retold): [something lost] in a street of Uruk-the-City, an axe lay on the ground with people crowding round it.
  3. Dream, continued: the axe had an odd shape; the sight made him happy; he loved it, stroked and hugged it as if it were his wife, then picked it up and kept it at his side.
  4. His all-knowing mother starts to reply (P about 36-38); then P breaks off briefly.
  5. (George fills the break from the Sealand tablet, chapter 4, changing the name to *Gilgamesh.) The mother explains that the axe is a man: someone will come who rescues his friend; Gilgamesh will be glad to see him, will hug him and bring him to her.
  6. (P resumes.) She adds that she will then make him Gilgamesh's equal.
- **Specifics:** the axe of strange shape; the street of Uruk-the-City; the crowd; the wife simile.
- **Short quotations:** "I loved him as a woman, / I embraced him" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, ll. 33-34).
- **George's footnote** to P 35 (chapter 3, note 1, in the book's Footnotes section): the line about keeping the axe at his side can also be read as making it his brother. Jastrow and Clay 1920 (ll. 35-36) take it that way: "I took him and regarded him / As my brother."
- **A different 1920 reading:** Jastrow and Clay 1920 read the start of the dream differently, with Gilgamesh seeing "My likeness" in the streets and an axe that "made him angry" (Pennsylvania tablet, about ll. 27-31). George reads a lost word, an axe lying in the street, and an axe of strange shape ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** the start of P about 27 is lost. P breaks after line 38; George fills lines 39-41 from the Sealand tablet. In those lines the promise of a rescuer, the "see him and rejoice" half-line and the "embrace him and bring" half-line are restored; unrestored are the line naming the axe as a man (with the name *Gilgamesh, which marks George's substitution for Sîn), "his friend," "will rejoice" and "before me."
- **Repetitions:** the dream-telling formula of OB.4 repeats; the interpretation reuses the see-rejoice-embrace-bring formula of the first.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB I 273-298 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - The dream: SB adds the people of Uruk standing and thronging round the axe (I 279-282). P's odd shape and Gilgamesh's gladness are not in SB.
  - Where the axe goes: at his side or made his brother (P) vs. set at his mother's feet (SB I 283).
  - Interpretation: the axe is a man (P, Sealand) vs. a friend (SB I about 288). SB adds the wife simile, the comrade who saves his friend and the rock-from-the-sky strength (I about 289-293). Both have the mother making him Gilgamesh's equal.
  - Who speaks after: SB closes with Gilgamesh's reply, asking that it happen by Enlil's command and that he gain a friend and counselor (I 294-297). P has no reply from Gilgamesh and moves straight to Enkidu and the harlot (OB.6).
  - Names: P's mother is unnamed; SB names her Wild-Cow Ninsun (I about 286-287), and she names herself (I 290).

### OB.6 The second week with Shamkatum; her invitation; the shared garment (P about 44-71; pp. 168-169)
- **Where and when:** in the wild, at the same time as the dream-telling: the text sets Enkidu before the harlot while Gilgamesh is recounting his dream (P about 44-45). Duration: seven days and seven nights.
- **Who:** Enkidu; the harlot Shamkatum (an older form of the name Shamhat; Glossary, p. 224). The harlot speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. While Gilgamesh tells his dream, Enkidu is sitting with the harlot.
  2. They make love, and he forgets the wild where he was born.
  3. For seven days and seven nights he stays aroused and sleeps with Shamkatum.
  4. The harlot tells Enkidu that, looking at him, he seems a god; why does he roam the wild with animals?
  5. She offers to lead him to Uruk-the-City, to the holy temple where Anu lives, to Eanna, Anu's dwelling, where men practice skilled crafts; he too, like a man (uncertain), could make a place for himself (uncertain) there; he is now an exile from the shepherdless lands.
  6. He listens and is persuaded; the woman's advice reaches his heart.
  7. She takes off her garment, dresses him in one part and keeps the other part on herself.
- **Specifics:** seven days and seven nights; Eanna as Anu's dwelling (twice); skilled labor; one garment shared between two.
- **Short quotations:** "Six days and seven nights" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, l. 46). Jastrow and Clay read six days here; George reads seven days and seven nights (P about 48-49) ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** "[men]" (P about 61) is restored. "Like a man" and "find a place for" are uncertain renderings (italic in George).
- **Repetitions:** the invitation follows the pattern of the harlot's first invitation (Cornell, OB.3; SB I 207-212), including the godlike-looks question.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 1 is SB's own (only that Enkidu sat before her); the rest falls in a lacuna of Tablet II that George fills with this same P text (SB II, p. 12), changing the name to *Shamhat. So no independent standard-version wording survives here. What can be compared:
  - Numbers: in George, P's second week lasts seven days and seven nights, while SB's first week lasts six days and seven nights (SB I about 194). George's preface (p. xii) says new sources show the standard version had two weeks of lovemaking, not one.
  - Names: Shamkatum (P) vs. Shamhat (SB).
  - Against SB's first invitation (I 207-212, SB's own): SB says Uruk-the-Sheepfold and one temple of Anu and Ishtar; P says Uruk-the-City and Eanna as Anu's dwelling, and adds the crafts line and the exile line.

### OB.7 The shepherds' camp: bread, ale, grooming, the night watch (P about 72-119; pp. 169-170)
- **Where and when:** the shepherds' camp by the sheep-pen. The scene ends at night, while the shepherds sleep.
- **Who:** the harlot (Shamkatum), Enkidu, the shepherds (speaking as a group), a barber, the chief herdsmen. Speakers: the shepherds, then the harlot.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Holding his hand, she leads him like a god to the shepherds' camp.
  2. The shepherds crowd round him; (broken) they say [he looks like Gilgamesh but is shorter and heavier-boned, surely the one born in the uplands]; he was raised on animals' milk.
  3. They set bread in front of him; he stares at it, baffled. He has never learned to eat bread or been taught to drink ale.
  4. The harlot tells him to eat the bread, which sustains life, and drink the ale, which is the custom of the land.
  5. He eats his fill and drinks seven full jugs of ale. He relaxes, sings, cheers up, and his face brightens.
  6. The barber trims his hairy body; rubbed with oil, he becomes a man. He dresses, looks like a warrior, and takes up a weapon to fight lions.
  7. At night, while the shepherds sleep, he kills wolves and drives off lions. The chief herdsmen sleep, and Enkidu stands guard, fully awake.
- **Specifics:** seven jugs of ale; bread; a barber; oil; a garment; a weapon; wolves and lions; the chief herdsmen; animals' milk.
- **Short quotations:** "Drink wine, the custom of the land!" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, l. 94). Jastrow and Clay translate the drink as "wine" and the amount as "Seven goblets" (l. 98); George has ale and jugs.
- **Gaps:** P about 77 breaks off after an uncertain word and the next lines are lost. The shepherds' remark (P 80 to about 84) is George's restoration from the matching lines, P about 182-187 (OB.9); only the animals'-milk line (P 85) is read. The phrase for "wide awake" (P about 119) is restored.
- **Repetitions:** the shepherds' remark is the same pair of couplets the people of Uruk say in OB.9 (P about 182-187).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 36-62. SB's own text survives for II 36-46 and II 59-62; George fills the lacuna between them with this same P text (P 90-111; SB II pp. 13-14). Differences, where SB has its own text:
  - The shepherds' remark: in SB they say he is built like Gilgamesh, tall and proud as a battlement, that he must be Enkidu from the uplands, and that he is as strong as a rock from the sky (II 40-43). In P he is shorter and heavier-boned and raised on animals' milk. So P makes him shorter, SB tall, and only SB's shepherds name him.
  - The food: SB has bread and ale set before him and Enkidu refusing the bread with a suspicious look (II 44 to about 46); in P he stares at it.
  - The watch: SB calls the sleepers the senior shepherds and Enkidu their shepherd boy (II 61-62); P calls them herdsmen and Enkidu their watchman.
  - Compare also the Hattusa fragment Bo1 (a) and the Sealand tablet in the chapter 4 file (M.4, M.15), which word the bread-and-ale lines differently or lack them.

### OB.8 The passer-by and the wedding custom (P 120-165, with lacunas at about 121-134 and 166-174; pp. 170-171)
- **Where and when:** at or near the shepherds' camp; time not stated.
- **Who:** Enkidu; Shamkatum; a man passing by. Speakers: Enkidu → Shamkatum; Shamkatum → the man; the man → Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. A man has been invited (uncertain rendering) to a wedding; the next words are broken.
  2. (Lacuna, about P 121-134.)
  3. Enkidu is making love with Shamkatum when he looks up and notices the man.
  4. Enkidu asks Shamkatum to call the man over so he can find out why he has come.
  5. The harlot calls to him and asks where he is rushing and why his trip is so hard.
  6. The man tells Enkidu he is going to a wedding feast; marriage is people's lot; he will heap the ritual table with delicacies for the feast.
  7. He goes on: for Uruk's king, Gilgamesh, the *veil* (uncertain) will be opened for the first to choose.
  8. He goes on: the king sleeps with the bride before the groom does; the gods ordained it; she was his by fate from the moment his umbilical cord was cut.
  9. Hearing this, Enkidu goes pale with anger.
  10. (Lacuna, about P 166-174: George says the rest of Enkidu's reaction is lost.)
- **Specifics:** the wedding feast; the ritual table and its delicacies; the veil (uncertain); the umbilical cord; Uruk-the-City.
- **Short quotations:** "From the cutting of his umbilical cord / (Such) is his fate." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, ll. 158-159). Jastrow and Clay read the custom itself differently, as an order to "perform the marriage act" for the king and a rule that "With the legitimate wife one should cohabit" (ll. 150, 154); George reads the king first, the groom after ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** P about 121 is broken; about P 121-134 lost; about P 166-174 lost. Uncertain renderings: the invitation (P 120) and the veil (P 155 and about 157).
- **Repetitions:** the veil couplet is said twice, first for Uruk's king and then naming Gilgamesh (P about 153-157).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 63-64 is SB's own: a man had been invited to a wedding and was on his way [to Uruk-the-Sheepfold] for the banquet (much restored). The rest (P 135-175) fills a lacuna in Tablet II, so the SB parallel is this same P text, with *Shamhat for Shamkatum (SB II pp. 14-15). Differences that can be seen:
  - Place: SB II 64 sends the man toward Uruk-the-Sheepfold; P's matching line (P about 121) is broken.
  - Names: Shamkatum (P) vs. *Shamhat in George's SB fill.
  - George's SB II synopsis says the custom shocks Enkidu; the text of P says only that he paled with anger before the break.

### OB.9 Enkidu enters Uruk; the festival; he blocks Gilgamesh's path (P 175-204; pp. 171-172)
- **Where and when:** in a street of Uruk-the-City, during the city's regular sacrificial festivals; Gilgamesh goes to the bride at night.
- **Who:** Enkidu; Shamkatum (behind him); a crowd and the townspeople (speaking as a group); the young men; Gilgamesh; the bride. Speakers: the townspeople.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu walks in front, with Shamkatum behind.
  2. He enters Uruk-the-City and people crowd round him.
  3. He stops in the street, and the gathered people talk about him: he has Gilgamesh's build but is shorter and heavier-boned; he must be the upland-born one, raised on animals' milk.
  4. The narrator explains that Uruk held sacrificial festivals regularly; the young men celebrated and put forward a *champion* (uncertain): a rival was set up against handsome, godlike Gilgamesh.
  5. A bed had been spread for the goddess of weddings, and Gilgamesh was to join the girl at night.
  6. Enkidu steps out and stands in the street, barring Gilgamesh's way.
  7. (Broken) something is said about him (P 204, uncertain). Then a gap.
- **Specifics:** sacrificial festivals; the goddess of weddings and her bed; the bride; Uruk-the-City.
- **Short quotations:** "For Ishhara a couch / Was stretched" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, ll. 192-193). Jastrow and Clay name the goddess Ishhara where George translates "the goddess of weddings."
- **Gaps:** "[first]" (P 175) and the "must be the one who" phrase (P about 186) are restored; "champion" and "talked about" are uncertain; the start of P 204 is lost; a gap of several lines follows (P about 205-208).
- **Repetitions:** the townspeople's remark repeats the shepherds' remark word for word (OB.7, where George restores it from here).
- **George's reading (p. 166):** George writes that Enkidu seems to arrive just as the young men pick a challenger and presumably takes the role. The text itself says only that a rival was set up for Gilgamesh and that Enkidu then blocked his path; it does not say Enkidu was chosen ⚠️.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II: George fills this lacuna with the same P 175-204 (SB II pp. 15-16), so most of the parallel is this text. SB's own text resumes at II 103-110, which overlaps the end of this scene:
  - Events added in SB: all Uruk stands and throngs round Enkidu and kisses his feet as if he were a baby (II 103-107). P has no feet-kissing in its preserved lines (P 204-208 are broken or lost) ⚠️.
  - Wording: SB calls the one set up against Gilgamesh a "substitute" (II 110); P calls him a "rival" (P 195).
  - SB's own lines also have the bed ready for the goddess of weddings (II 109), where P has it spread (P about 196).

### OB.10 The wrestling match in the Square of the Land; Enkidu hails Gilgamesh as king (P about 205-240; p. 172)
- **Where and when:** at a doorway in the Square of the Land, in Uruk; the same night (continuing from OB.9).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Enkidu speaks at the end.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Broken) Gilgamesh … at the doorway (uncertain); in front of him …; he was getting angry …
  2. Enkidu goes toward him, and they face each other in the Square of the Land.
  3. Enkidu puts his foot across the doorway and will not let Gilgamesh in.
  4. They grapple, bent over like bulls, break the doorpost and make the wall shake. The couplet is said twice, the second time naming both men.
  5. Gilgamesh goes down on one knee, one foot to the ground; his rage cools and he stops fighting.
  6. Once the fight is over, Enkidu tells Gilgamesh that his mother, Ninsun, the wild cow of the fold, bore him as a unique one, that he stands above all warriors, and that Enlil decreed him king over the people.
  7. The tablet ends here (George, p. 166: it ends with Enkidu's acceptance that Gilgamesh's kingship is legitimate).
- **Specifics:** the Square of the Land; the door, doorpost and wall; the bull simile; Ninsun as the wild cow of the fold; Enlil as giver of kingship.
- **Short quotations:** "Kingship over men / Enlil has decreed for thee." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Pennsylvania tablet, ll. 239-240). Jastrow and Clay compare the fighters to "oxen" (l. 218).
- **Gaps:** about P 205-208 lost; P about 209-212 broken (Gilgamesh, the space in front of him, his growing anger). The numbering then runs 225 to 230 to 235 across fewer printed lines; George marks no gap there, so this is the old verse-to-line count (p. lv), not a lacuna ⚠️.
- **Repetitions:** the grappling couplet, P about 217-220, repeated at P about 221-225 with the two names spelled out.
- **Who won:** the text says only that Gilgamesh knelt with one foot on the ground, his anger subsided and he broke off, and that Enkidu then praised him as king. It does not say in so many words who won ⚠️.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 111-117 is SB's own text; after it George fills a lacuna with this same P 229-240 (SB II p. 16). Differences:
  - The door: SB specifies the door of the wedding house (II 111); P says only the door.
  - The fight: SB has them seize each other at the wedding-house door and fight in the street, in the Square of the Land, with doorposts and wall shaking (II 113-115, partly restored). P adds the bull simile and the broken doorpost.
  - After the fight: SB goes on (II 162-190) to Gilgamesh introducing Enkidu to Ninsun and Ninsun's words that make Enkidu weep; P ends at Enkidu's praise of Gilgamesh. The friendship scenes that follow are on the Yale tablet (OB.11-OB.13).

## Fragment 5. The Yale tablet (Y): Enkidu becomes Gilgamesh's brother; preparations for the journey to the Cedar Forest (pp. 172-180)
**The source (George, pp. 172-173):** called "Gilgamesh Y." It is the third tablet of the same Old Babylonian edition as the Pennsylvania tablet and has the same handwriting and make, so it must come from the same place. The Yale Babylonian Collection in New Haven bought it around 1914. It follows SB Tablets II-III, but with many differences, and parts of it fill gaps in chapter 1. (Jastrow and Clay 1920 published it with the Pennsylvania tablet; their Yale line numbers match George's closely.)

**George's synopsis (p. 173), paraphrased:** as Ninsun foretold, the two become close friends. Breaks in the tablet hide why Enkidu then falls into misery. To cheer him, Gilgamesh proposes an expedition to the Cedar Forest to kill the monster Huwawa (the Old Babylonian form of Humbaba's name). Enkidu warns that the gods set Huwawa to guard the cedar. Gilgamesh mocks his fear of dying and praises death in battle for the lasting fame it wins. They have weapons forged, and Gilgamesh calls Uruk's assembly; the elders urge caution and he laughs at them. After a break someone, perhaps Ninsun, gives him a farewell blessing. Gilgamesh prays to the Sun God for protection and to Lugalbanda, his deified father. The heroes take up their gear, and the elders bless and advise Gilgamesh. On leaving, Enkidu tells him to send the young men back into the city, and the two go on alone.

### OB.11 A kiss and a friendship (Y about 14-18; p. 173)
- **Where and when:** not stated (the beginning of the tablet is broken).
- **Who:** an unnamed speaker (not identified in the surviving lines); then Gilgamesh and Enkidu together (implied by "each other"; not named).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Beginning broken.)
  2. An unnamed speaker asks an unnamed listener why he wants to do this, and what it is he wants so badly; then (broken) speaks of something never before achieved in the land.
  3. The two kiss and become friends.
- **Specifics:** a deed never yet done in the land.
- **Short quotations:** "[Why] dost thou desire / [to do this?]" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, ll. 13-14). Jastrow and Clay do not read the kiss: their following lines concern a message carried "among [men]"; George reads that the two kissed and formed a friendship (Y about 18) ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** the beginning of the tablet (about Y 1-13) is lost; Y 15-16 are broken; a lacuna follows (about Y 19-23). Who speaks and what "this" refers to are lost ⚠️.
- **Repetitions:** the why-do-you-want-this question comes back later in the tablet, from Enkidu (Y about 112) and from the elders (Y 199), about the expedition. The never-done-before deed recurs in the Harmal dream (OB.27, Ha1 17).
- **Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. The matching part of SB Tablet II is a lacuna (between II 117 and II 162). George's synopsis of SB Tablet II says the two become firm friends, and SB II 182-183 has them take hold of each other and join hands, but after Enkidu's weeping, not before.

### OB.12 "I have found a friend"; Enkidu thanks the harlot (Y about 24-29; p. 173)
- **Where and when:** in Uruk (Enkidu thanks the harlot for bringing him there); time not stated.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (George says the passage opens with him speaking); Enkidu; the harlot (unnamed here). Speakers: Gilgamesh (listener not stated), then Enkidu → the harlot.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh declares that he has found his friend, the adviser he saw in his dreams, and names him: Enkidu.
  2. Enkidu tells the harlot he will do her a kindness, because she brought him to Uruk-the-City-of-the-Land and introduced him to a fine companion and friend.
  3. (Long lacuna, about Y 30-70. What the kindness was is lost.)
- **Specifics:** Uruk, called the City-of-the-[Land]; the dream adviser; the companion and friend.
- **Short quotations:** "I have found a friend" (Y about 24). Jastrow and Clay 1920 had no text for this passage, which George restores with help from a later-found fragment; their lines here speak only of something done "[To] the woman" (Yale tablet, about ll. 27-28).
- **Gaps:** George says a fragment now in Norway (a Schøyen piece, see OB.22's source note) helps restore these lines. The words "in my dreams" (Y about 24) and "Land" (Y about 28) are restored. A long lacuna follows.
- **Repetitions:** the dream-adviser phrase is said twice, the second time with Enkidu's name.
- **Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version; this falls in the SB II lacuna. Related SB passages: Gilgamesh's wish, at the end of his dreams, for a friend to advise him (SB I 296-297), which these lines answer; and Enkidu's blessing of Shamhat (SB VII 151-163), which in SB comes only on his deathbed after Shamash rebukes him for cursing her. In Y, Enkidu offers the harlot a kindness unprompted and at once, for bringing him to Uruk and to his friend.

### OB.13 Enkidu's tears (Y 71-90; p. 174)
- **Where and when:** not stated.
- **Who:** Enkidu, Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh asks; Enkidu answers; Gilgamesh begins to reply.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu's eyes fill with tears; he grows gloomy and sighs *heavily* (uncertain). The couplet is said twice, the second time naming him.
  2. Gilgamesh *turns* (uncertain) his face to him and asks why his eyes are full of tears and why he is so downcast and sighing.
  3. Enkidu answers that weeping has tied knots in his neck muscles, his arms hang limp and his strength is draining away.
  4. Gilgamesh starts to answer (Y about 87-90). Lacuna (about Y 91-96).
- **Specifics:** tears and sighs; the knotted neck sinews; limp arms; failing strength.
- **Short quotations:** "My arms are lame, / my strength has become weak." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, about l. 86).
- **Gaps:** the cause of Enkidu's misery is lost in the long lacuna before Y 71 (George, p. 173, says the reasons are unclear because the tablet is broken). Y 71 and Y 81 are mostly restored.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's tears are described twice (Y 71-75) and again in Gilgamesh's question (Y 80 and about 81).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 162-190 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Cause: SB gives one. Gilgamesh presents Enkidu to Ninsun, who remarks that Enkidu has no [*family*], was born in the wild and has no [*brother*]; hearing this, Enkidu sits down in tears (II 162-179). In Y the cause is lost.
  - Wording: in SB his eyes fill, his arms drop and his strength [*fails*] (II 180-181), and he says his heart is troubled, his [*legs*] shake from sobbing and terror has entered his heart (II 189 to about 191). Y has the knotted neck, the limp arms and the failing strength, and no terror.
  - Events: SB has the two take hold of each other and join hands before Gilgamesh's question (II 182-183); Y does not in its preserved lines.

### OB.14 Gilgamesh proposes to kill Huwawa; Enkidu's first warning (Y about 91-126; pp. 174-175)
- **Where and when:** continuing from OB.13; not stated.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Lacuna, about Y 91-96: the start of Gilgamesh's answer is lost.)
  2. Gilgamesh proposes that they kill the fierce Huwawa and end his power, catching him off guard in his lair in the Cedar Forest.
  3. Enkidu replies that he knew Huwawa in the uplands when he wandered with the herd; the forest is sixty leagues of wilderness; who would dare go in?
  4. Enkidu goes on: Huwawa's voice is the Flood, his speech is fire, his breath is death; why does Gilgamesh want this? An ambush by Huwawa is a fight no one can win.
  5. Gilgamesh answers that he will climb the [*forest's*] slopes.
  6. (Lacuna, about Y 120-126.)
- **Specifics:** Huwawa "ferocious"; the Cedar Forest; his lair; sixty leagues; voice, speech and breath as Flood, fire and death.
- **Short quotations:** "Huwawa, whose roar is a flood, / whose mouth is fire, / whose breath is death." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, ll. 109-111). Jastrow and Clay read the forest's extent as "one double hour" (about l. 108); George reads sixty leagues ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** Y 91-96 lost; Y 97-101 partly restored (the "let us" verbs, his power, the forest, Huwawa's dwelling); "the forest's" (Y 119) is an uncertain restoration; Y 120-126 lost.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's warning (Y 106-115) is repeated almost word for word by the elders (OB.17, Y 195-200). The why-question echoes Y 14 (OB.11).
- **Compared with the standard version:** George fills the SB lacuna after II 190 with this same Y 90-119, changing Huwawa to *Humbaba (SB II pp. 17-18). So there is no independent SB wording for this exchange. Points of comparison with SB's own lines elsewhere in Tablet II:
  - Names: Huwawa (Y) vs. Humbaba (SB).
  - Numbers: Y says the forest is a wilderness for sixty leagues; SB's own warning (II 223) says Humbaba hears the forest rustle from sixty leagues away.

### OB.15 Wer and the Seven Terrors; Gilgamesh on death and an eternal name (Y 127-162; pp. 175-176)
- **Where and when:** continuing; not stated.
- **Who:** Enkidu, Gilgamesh. Enkidu → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Enkidu (a long speech).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu asks how they could possibly go to the Cedar Forest. Its guardian is the god Wer, mighty and never sleeping; Huwawa [*was appointed*] by Wer; Adad comes first and Huwawa [second]; and to protect the cedars [*Enlil*] gave Huwawa the Seven Terrors.
  2. Gilgamesh answers: no one can climb up to heaven; only the gods live forever in the sun; a man's days are counted and all he does is wind.
  3. He goes on: Enkidu is afraid of death; where has his great courage gone? Gilgamesh will walk ahead, and Enkidu can shout encouragement after him.
  4. He goes on: if he falls, he will have made his name, as the man who fought fierce Huwawa.
  5. He goes on: Enkidu was born and raised in the wild, was attacked by a lion and has been through everything; grown men ran from him; … (broken) in the evening.
  6. He goes on: why does Enkidu now talk like a coward? His feeble words hurt Gilgamesh's heart.
  7. He goes on: he will set out and cut down the cedar, and win a name that lasts forever.
  8. He ends: let them hurry to the forge and have hatchets cast while they watch.
- **Specifics:** Wer, mighty and unsleeping (Wer, "Violent One," is a name of Adad, especially in the west; Glossary, p. 224); Adad first, Huwawa second; the Seven Terrors; a lion; the forge; hatchets.
- **Short quotations:** "If I fall, I will establish my name." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, about l. 148). Jastrow and Clay give the guardian's terror as "seven-fold terror" (l. 137) and do not read the name Wer; their lines 140-141 speak of a man who overcomes terror being well with Shamash, where George reads that only the gods live forever in sunlight ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** restorations: the cedars, Enlil (uncertain), "was appointed by" (uncertain), "is second," "dwell," "why now," the feeble words, "a name that," "come," the hatchets; Y 155 is broken (only "you" and "evening" survive).
- **Repetitions:** the cut-the-cedar, eternal-name couplet comes back in Gilgamesh's speech to the elders (OB.17, Y about 186-187).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB II 216-241 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Who guards the forest: Y names the god Wer as guardian and appointer of Huwawa; SB says Enlil gave Humbaba the task of terrifying men (II about 217-219, and again II 227-228), with no Wer.
  - The Seven Terrors: Y has Enlil (restored) give him the Seven Terrors; SB has the terrifying and a seizure of trembling that grips anyone who enters his forest (II 229). (In SB V 99-100 Humbaba has seven cloaks.)
  - Added in SB: the journey that must not be made and the man who must not be looked at (II about 218-219); Humbaba hearing the forest rustle from sixty leagues (II 223); the question who among the Igigi could oppose him (II 226). Both have Adad first and Humbaba second.
  - Gilgamesh's speech, added in Y with no counterpart in SB's preserved lines: climbing to heaven and the gods' endless sunlight; the taunt that Enkidu fears death; the offer to walk in front; making his name if he falls. SB II 236 is broken.
  - Wording: Y says a lion attacked Enkidu; SB says even lions feared him (II 238); SB adds that his heart is tested in combat (II 240).
  - Order: SB opens Gilgamesh's speech with the charge of talking like a coward (II 232); Y has it near the end (Y about 156).
  - Outcome: the same; both end with the call to hurry to the forge.

### OB.16 At the forge: the weapons are cast (Y 163-171; p. 176)
- **Where and when:** the forge in Uruk, where the smiths are sitting in council; directly after OB.15.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu, the smiths. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Hand in hand, the two hurry to the forge, where the smiths sit deliberating.
  2. The smiths cast large hatchets and axes of three talents each.
  3. They cast large daggers: two-talent blades, handle crests of half a talent, and gold fittings of half a talent each.
  4. Each hero carries ten talents of weapons.
- **Specifics:** axes 3 talents each; dagger blades 2 talents each; handle crests 1/2 talent; gold fittings 1/2 talent each; a load of 10 talents per man.
- **Short quotations:** "Gish and [Enki]du were equipped with 10 talents each" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, l. 171). Jastrow and Clay read the half-talent items as "30 mina," which is the same weight (ll. 169-170).
- **Gaps:** none in this passage.
- **Repetitions:** the casting of hatchets and the casting of daggers form a parallel pair.
- **Compared with the standard version:** George fills the short lacuna after SB II 241 with this same Y 162-171 (SB II p. 19), so the parallel is this text and no independent SB wording survives. For weights elsewhere: in SB V about 309 the hatchets used against Humbaba's seven sons weigh two talents each; in the Ishchali tablet (OB.30) Gilgamesh takes up a war-net of two talents, a sword of eight and a load of ten.

### OB.17 The assembly of Uruk; the elders' warning; Gilgamesh laughs (Y 172-204; pp. 176-177)
- **Where and when:** in a street of Uruk-the-City, with the city gates bolted; directly after the forge.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; the assembly (the crowd); the elders of Uruk, his advisers; Enkidu (addressed at the end). Speakers: Gilgamesh → the elders; the elders → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh bars Uruk's seven gates and calls [the assembly]; a crowd collects.
  2. In a street of the city he [*takes his seat on*] his throne, with the crowd seated before him.
  3. He tells the elders that he means [*to go to*] fierce Huwawa, to see this god everyone talks about, whose name is on every land's lips.
  4. He goes on: he will defeat him in the Cedar Forest so the land knows how strong Uruk's offspring is; he will set out, fell the cedar and win a name that lasts forever.
  5. The elders reply that he is young and carried away by feeling and does not understand what he is doing; they hear Huwawa looks uncanny; who can withstand his weapons? The forest is wilderness for sixty [leagues]; who would go in?
  6. They go on: Huwawa's voice is the Flood, his speech fire, his breath death; why does he want this? An ambush by Huwawa is a fight no one can win.
  7. Gilgamesh hears his advisers, turns laughing to his friend and asks, in mock alarm, whether fear of Huwawa should make him [*change his mind*] (uncertain).
  8. (Lacuna, about Y 205-212.)
- **Specifics:** the seven gates of Uruk; the throne; the assembly; the elders; Huwawa called a god (Y 182); Uruk's offspring; sixty leagues.
- **Short quotations:** "Thou art young, O Gish, and thy heart carries thee away." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, l. 191). Jastrow and Clay read Gilgamesh's reply as an admission, "I fear him," followed by a resolve to go anyway (about ll. 203-204); George reads it as mockery, with the key words uncertain ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** Y 173-181 partly restored (the assembly, his seating on the throne, the street and crowd, the speech formula, the address to the elders, and all of Y 181, his intent to go to Huwawa). "Leagues" (Y 195) is restored. Gilgamesh's last two lines (Y 203-204) are uncertain restorations.
- **Repetitions:** the elders' warning repeats Enkidu's warning (Y 106-115) almost word for word. Gilgamesh's cut-the-cedar, eternal-name couplet repeats Y about 158-160.
- **Compared with the standard version:** Y 172-187 fills a lacuna in SB Tablet II (SB II pp. 19-20), so that part's parallel is this text. SB's own text resumes at II 258-302. Differences:
  - Events added in SB: Gilgamesh also speaks to Uruk's young men, asks their blessing and promises to hold the New Year festival twice, with drums before Ninsun (II 258-271); Enkidu then advises the elders and young men to stop him going (II 272-286). Neither is in Y's preserved lines.
  - Who warns: in Y only the elders answer; in SB both Enkidu (II 272-286) and the senior advisers (II 287-299) speak.
  - Wording of the elders: Y says he does not understand what he does; SB says what he talks of (II 290). Only Y has the line about Huwawa's uncanny look and unmatched weapons (Y 193-194). Where Y has the sixty leagues of wilderness, the why-question and the unwinnable ambush, SB has Humbaba hearing the forest at sixty leagues, the Igigi line and Enlil's assignment (II about 293-299).
  - Names: Huwawa (Y) vs. Humbaba (SB).
  - Outcome: the same; Gilgamesh laughs (SB II 300-302, restored from Y).
  - Compare also the Hattusa fragment Bo1 (d), chapter 4 file, M.5.

### OB.18 A blessing; Gilgamesh kneels and prays to Shamash (Y 213-223; pp. 177-178)
- **Where and when:** presumably still in Uruk, before departure; not stated.
- **Who:** an unnamed speaker of the blessing (George, p. 173, suggests it may be Ninsun); Gilgamesh; Shamash (addressed, silent); someone Gilgamesh summons (the word is lost).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (The text picks up in the middle of a blessing.) The unnamed speaker wishes that Gilgamesh's god will [set] his feet [on the straightest road] and let his *steps* (uncertain) finish the journey and bring him home safe to Uruk's quay.
  2. Gilgamesh kneels [before] the Sun God and prays: he is going to [Huwawa's home]; may Shamash let him leave it safely, [keep] him alive, bring him back to Uruk's quay and [shelter him].
  3. Gilgamesh calls for someone (lost) and gives instructions (lost).
  4. (Lacuna, about Y 224-228.)
- **Specifics:** the quay of Uruk (twice); the kneeling prayer; Shamash the Sun God.
- **Short quotations:** "Bring me back to the rampart [in Erech]." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, l. 220). Jastrow and Clay read "rampart" where George reads "quay," and they read the kneeling as taking place "At the rampart" (about l. 215) ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** about Y 205-212 lost before; Y 213 and much of Y 217-221 restored; Y 222-223 broken (whom he summons and what he instructs are lost) ⚠️.
- **Repetitions:** Uruk's quay appears in the blessing and again in the prayer.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB Tablet III (SB's own text).
  - The blessing: in SB the wish for a safe return to Uruk's quay belongs to the elders (III 1, III 215). In Y the speaker is not shown to be the elders (George suggests Ninsun) ⚠️.
  - Gilgamesh's prayer: no counterpart in SB III. In SB it is Ninsun who prays to Shamash, from the palace roof with incense, after bathing seven times and dressing (III 35-115), and she then adopts Enkidu (III 116-128). None of that is in Y's preserved lines. SB's nearest prayer by Gilgamesh himself to Shamash comes later, at the forest (V 90 to about 94).
  - The instructions: SB III 202-211 has Gilgamesh instructing the regents on ruling Uruk in his absence. Whether Y 222-223 is a version of this cannot be told from the broken lines ⚠️.

### OB.19 The prayer to Lugalbanda; the heroes take up their weapons (Y about 229-244; p. 178)
- **Where and when:** not stated; before departure.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (praying, in tears); his god (George's note on p. 178 identifies the prayer as addressed to Lugalbanda); unnamed people who hand over the weapons.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Tears run down [his] cheeks.
  2. Gilgamesh prays to his god: he is about to take a road he has never traveled and does not know its … (broken).
  3. He goes on: may he come home safe and see his god's face again with a glad heart; he promises to build the god a pleasing house and seat him on many thrones.
  4. (Broken) his gear …, the great daggers …, bow and quiver … are put into their hands.
  5. [Gilgamesh] takes up the hatchets and [receives] his quiver and the bow from Anshan.
  6. He sticks his dagger in his belt, [*ready*] to start the journey.
- **Specifics:** the bow of Anshan (Anshan: an area of south-western Iran; Glossary, p. 221); hatchets; great daggers; bow and quiver; dagger in the belt; the promised house and thrones for the god.
- **Short quotations:** "[I will seat thee] on thrones." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, l. 235). Jastrow and Clay took lines 229-235 as a second prayer to the sun god; George reads them as a prayer to Lugalbanda ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** about Y 224-228 lost before; Y 229-235 partly restored (the cheeks, the opening verbs of each request and promise); Y 236-239 broken at the start; Gilgamesh's name, the receiving of the quiver, the placing of the weapons and the "ready" phrase (uncertain) are restored.
- **Repetitions:** "my god" twice in the opening couplet.
- **Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. The end of SB Tablet III is lost after III 227, and no prayer by Gilgamesh to Lugalbanda survives anywhere in SB. In SB, Lugalbanda receives the Bull of Heaven's horns as oil vessels (VI 165), and Enkidu reminds Gilgamesh to keep his god Lugalbanda in mind (V 141). The arming scene: SB's matching lines are lost.

### OB.20 The young men come up; the elders' blessing and advice for the road (Y 245-271; pp. 178-179)
- **Where and when:** at the start of the journey; not otherwise stated.
- **Who:** [*the young men*] (uncertain); Gilgamesh; the elders. Speakers: the young men (one broken line), then the elders → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. [*The young men*] approach Gilgamesh and say something (broken) about sending back to the city.
  2. The elders bless Gilgamesh and advise him not to trust his own strength alone, to keep a sharp lookout and to protect himself.
  3. They go on: Enkidu should walk ahead, since he knows the path, has traveled the road, [knows] the routes into the forest and every one of Huwawa's tricks.
  4. They cite a saying: whoever walks in front protects his companion, and whoever keeps a keen eye [guards] himself.
  5. They go on: may Shamash grant him victory and let him see the deeds he boasted of; may he open closed paths, clear the road and the mountain for his feet; may the night bring him good news.
  6. They go on: may Lugalbanda stand by him in his victory, and may he win it like a small child.
  7. They go on: he should wash his feet in Huwawa's river, his goal.
  8. They go on: at each night's camp he should dig a well and keep his flask full of fresh water, pour out cool water for Shamash and remember his [god] Lugalbanda.
- **Specifics:** Huwawa's tricks; Huwawa's river; a well at each night's camp; a flask of fresh water; cool water offered to Shamash; Lugalbanda; "like a little child."
- **Short quotations:** "In the river of Huwawa as thou plannest, / wash thy feet!" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, ll. 266-267). For George's "like a little child" (Y 265), Jastrow and Clay have "Like a youth" ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** "the young men" (Y 245) is uncertain; Y about 246 is broken at the start; the "do not," "let," "he knows," "protect" and "god" are restored.
- **Repetitions:** the wish that Shamash grant victory recurs in the young men's prayer (OB.21, Y 286). Victory is wished three times in a row (Y 257, about 263, 265).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB III 1-13 and again III 215-227 (SB's own text; in SB the elders give this advice twice, at the start of Tablet III and again at the departure). In SB they wish him a safe return to Uruk's quay, tell him not to trust his strength alone and to aim his blows with care, quote the saying, tell him to let Enkidu lead because he knows the way and is tested in battle, say Enkidu will bring him home safe to *the wives*, and then turn to Enkidu and put the king in his keeping until he brings him back. Differences:
  - Events missing from SB: the blessings by Shamash and Lugalbanda; victory "like a little child"; washing the feet in Huwawa's river; digging a well each night; cool water for Shamash. (SB IV does have the heroes dig a well facing the sun at every camp, IV 5 and repeats.)
  - Events added in SB: the address to Enkidu entrusting the king to him; his testing in battle; bringing the king home to the wives.
  - The saying: Y pairs the one in front with the sharp-eyed one who protects himself; SB pairs the one in front with the one who knows the road and protects his friend (III 4-5).
  - Names: Huwawa (Y) vs. Humbaba (SB). SB has an infant simile too, but in Gilgamesh's own instructions to the regents (III 210), not in the elders' speech.

### OB.21 Enkidu's assurance; the crowd sent home; the young men's prayer; departure (Y about 272-287; pp. 179-180)
- **Where and when:** at the departure from Uruk.
- **Who:** Enkidu, Gilgamesh, the crowd, the young men. Speakers: Enkidu → Gilgamesh; an unnamed speaker (broken, probably Gilgamesh to the crowd ⚠️); the young men → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu tells Gilgamesh to start out wherever he has *resolved* (uncertain), to fear nothing and to watch him.
  2. Enkidu goes on: he knew Huwawa's lair in the forest and the paths he roams; Gilgamesh should speak [*to the crowd*] and send them home.
  3. (One line lost.) An unnamed speaker says (broken) that some people [should not] come with him.
  4. The crowd, glad at heart, hears what he said.
  5. The young men *pray earnestly* (uncertain) that Gilgamesh go, that his god go [before him] and that [Shamash] grant him [victory].
  6. Gilgamesh and Enkidu *set out* (uncertain) …
  7. A few more broken lines follow; George says the tablet would have ended soon after (p. 180).
- **Specifics:** the crowd sent home; the young men's prayer; Huwawa's lair.
- **Short quotations:** "Thy heart [be not afraid,] trust to me!" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, about l. 274).
- **Gaps:** Y 278 lost; Y 279-284 broken at the start or end; "to the crowd" (Y 277), "before you" (Y 285), Shamash's name and "your victory" (Y about 286) are restored. The last lines of the tablet are not translated.
- **Repetitions:** the young men's wish for Shamash's victory repeats the elders' blessing (Y 257, OB.20).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB III. George fills the lost end of Tablet III with this same Y 272-277 (SB III pp. 27-28), so the parallel for Enkidu's words is this text. SB's own last preserved lines before that have the young men running behind in a crowd and the regents kissing Gilgamesh's feet (III 213-214), then the elders' repeated advice (III 215-227) and the start of a speech by Enkidu (III about 228). Differences: the sending home of the crowd, the young men's prayer and the departure have no surviving SB text. George's Y synopsis (p. 173) says the two leave alone; the text itself says only that the crowd heard and that the two set out ⚠️.

## Fragment 6. The Schøyen tablet (Schøyen2): the first and second dreams on the journey to the Cedar Forest (pp. 180-183)
**The source (George, p. 180):** the Schøyen Collection in Norway owns three Old Babylonian Gilgamesh pieces, two of them published in 2003 and again in 2009. One is a set of a dozen scraps of little use. The second is a small piece from southern Babylonia, probably early 18th century BC, much like the Cornell fragment: its front helps restore Yale lines 24ff. (OB.12) and its back repeats Yale lines 151-53, so it is not reprinted. The third, a whole tablet perhaps a century younger, covers the march to the Cedar Forest with Gilgamesh's first two dreams and Enkidu's readings of them; chapter 1 uses some of its lines at SB IV 54 and 109. The scenes below come from this complete tablet. (It was found long after 1920, so there is no public-domain translation; the scenes are paraphrased.)

### OB.22 The first dream: the mountain and the man like a lion (Schøyen2 about 1-24; pp. 180-181)
- **Where and when:** on the journey, at night; Gilgamesh wakes in the middle watch. This is the start of the tablet.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh lies down to rest and dreams; in the middle watch he jolts awake and gets up to speak to his friend.
  2. He tells Enkidu he has dreamed, asks why Enkidu did not wake him, and says it was terrifying.
  3. Dream (retold): he was holding up a mountain with his shoulder when it collapsed on him and crushed him; his legs went weak and a dreadful aura overcame his arms.
  4. Dream, continued: a lion-like man came to him, radiant and very handsome, took him by the upper arm and dragged him out from under the mountain.
  5. Enkidu reads the dream: the strange one they are heading for, Huwawa, is the mountain (he says it twice, the second time naming him).
  6. Enkidu goes on: when they meet, Gilgamesh has a single duty, the fighter's task that falls to a man; Huwawa will turn his rage on him and fear will weaken his legs; but the man in the dream was Shamash, the Sun God, called king, who will take his hand when danger comes.
  7. Gilgamesh is pleased that the dream is a good sign; his heart lifts and his face shines.
- **Specifics:** the collapsing mountain; the aura; the lion-like man; the middle watch of the night; Shamash given the title king.
- **Short quotations:** "the warrior's rite" (Schøyen2 about 19).
- **Gaps:** only the word for his beauty (Schøyen2 10) is restored.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's is-he-not-the-mountain couplet is said twice, the second time naming Huwawa. The weakness of the legs in the dream returns as the fear that will weaken them.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IV. George fills the place of SB's lost second dream (after IV 54) with this same Schøyen2 text (SB IV pp. 30-31), citing a Hattusa paraphrase that shows SB's second dream told of a rescue from beneath an avalanche by the Sun God. So no independent SB wording survives for this dream. Differences that can be seen:
  - Order: this is the first dream here; in SB it stands as the second dream. SB's own first dream (IV 22-34) is different: the two stood on a slope, the mountain fell between them and they flew over it like flies (restored), and Enkidu says they will capture and kill Humbaba.
  - The ritual: SB frames every dream with a ritual (a well dug facing the sun; flour poured on the mountain with a request for a dream; a House of the Dream God built by Enkidu, a circle drawn, Enkidu lying across the doorway; Gilgamesh sleeping chin on knees) and with waking questions about whether someone called him, touched him or a god passed by (IV 5-21 and repeats). Schøyen2 has none of this before the first dream; the tablet is complete, so this is its true beginning. Its waking words are only the complaint that Enkidu did not wake him.
  - Names: Huwawa (Schøyen2) vs. Humbaba (SB).
  - Compare the Hattusa tablet Bo2 (chapter 4 file, M.6), which tells this same dream as the second.

### OB.23 Three days' march toward Ebla; the second dream: the Storm God's fire (Schøyen2 25-52; pp. 181-182)
- **Where and when:** on the road, three days' march, nearing the land of Ebla; then on a hilltop; the dream comes and he wakes in the middle watch of the night.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, [Enkidu] (name restored at Schøyen2 about 44). Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. They march for one day, then a second and a third, getting closer to Ebla.
  2. Gilgamesh climbs a hilltop and looks out over all the mountains; he puts his chin on his knees and the sleep that comes over all people takes him.
  3. At the middle watch he wakes with a jolt and tells his friend he has had a second dream, worse than the first.
  4. Dream (retold): the Storm God thundered and the earth shook; daylight was veiled and darkness came; lightning struck, fire flared and death fell like rain; Adad's thunder left him lost in the dark, not knowing where he was going.
  5. Dream, continued: *in the end* (uncertain) the blaze died down to embers; the darkness lightened and a god appeared in radiance; … (broken).
  6. [Enkidu] reads it: Adad's thunder … will turn against Gilgamesh … his eyes will shine; the radiance that appeared will [*put out*] (uncertain) the flames, and he will reduce the god's weapons to ash.
  7. Enkidu goes on: the dreams are good; a god is with him; he will soon reach his goal.
- **Specifics:** three days' march; Ebla (a town in north Syria, now Tell Mardikh; Glossary, p. 222); the hilltop; chin on knees; the Storm God, Adad; thunder, lightning, fire, embers; a shining god.
- **Short quotations:** "a god is with you" (Schøyen2 about 51).
- **Gaps:** Schøyen2 about 43 and 45-48 are broken at the start; Enkidu's name, the word "dream," a relative "that" and the dousing verb are restorations.
- **Repetitions:** the waking couplet of OB.22 repeats. The sleep "that spills over people" (Schøyen2 30) is a formula SB uses before every dream.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IV (SB's own text for the travel, the ritual and the dream; George fills the lost end of Enkidu's explanation, after IV 109, with this same Schøyen2 50-52).
  - The march: SB's refrain measures each stage, bread at twenty leagues, camp at thirty, fifty leagues a day, a month and a half's journey in three days, toward Mount Lebanon (IV 1-4 and repeats). Schøyen2 just counts three days, toward Ebla, with no leagues.
  - The ritual: in SB Gilgamesh climbs the mountain and offers flour asking for a dream, and Enkidu builds the House of the Dream God (IV about 82-91 for this dream); Schøyen2 has him climb a hill and look at the mountains, with no offering or dream house. Both have chin on knees and the all-conquering sleep.
  - Order: this is the second dream here; in SB the same storm dream is the third (IV about 99-107).
  - The dream: SB has heaven roaring and earth rumbling, stillness, darkness, lightning, fire and raining death, then the fire dying to cinders (IV 100 to about 106). Only Schøyen2 names the Storm God and Adad, and only Schøyen2 has the darkness brightening and a god appearing. SB adds Gilgamesh asking Enkidu, born in the wild, for advice (IV about 107).
  - The explanation: SB's own text keeps only Enkidu's opening, that the dream is good and lucky (IV 109).

### OB.24 At Hamran: Huwawa sees them; Enkidu's terror at the sight of the cedar; the third dream announced (Schøyen2 about 53-84; pp. 182-183)
- **Where and when:** after traveling day and night, at Hamran, the mountain where the Amorites live; they sit on its summit. At the end they make camp for the night.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu, Huwawa (who sees them; he does not speak). Speakers: Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Traveling day and night, they approach Hamran and *settle* (uncertain) on the top of the [mountain] of the Amorites, where they hear Huwawa's voice every day.
  2. Huwawa, the cedar's guardian who turns back every intruder, sees them (the couplet is said twice, the second time naming him).
  3. [Enkidu] looks up and sees the cedar, its [glory] spread over the uplands; he goes as pale as a cut-off head and fear sinks deep into his heart.
  4. Gilgamesh turns to him and asks why he has gone pale and why fear has gripped him.
  5. Enkidu answers that he saw the cedar in its glory over the uplands; who can stand against that god, whose weapon is the strongest among the gods? Can they stand against Huwawa, whose weapon is the strongest on earth? That is why he went pale.
  6. Gilgamesh replies: was he not swept along by eagerness in *his strength* (uncertain)? The *Sun God* (uncertain) told him [he would] come with [him]. Enkidu should not be afraid but watch him; Gilgamesh will fight a battle such as Enkidu has never seen.
  7. They make camp and lie down; woken by his sleep, Gilgamesh tells Enkidu he has had a third dream. (The tablet ends here; the third dream is not on it.)
- **Specifics:** Hamran (a mountain of uncertain location on the way to the Cedar Forest; Glossary, p. 222); the Amorites; Huwawa's voice heard daily; Huwawa as the cedar's guardian who turns everyone back; the cedar's glory; the face like a severed head; Huwawa's weapon the strongest on earth.
- **Short quotations:** "like a severed head" (Schøyen2 about 64).
- **Gaps:** "mountain," Huwawa's name with its title, Enkidu's raising of his eyes, "splendour," and the Sun God's "I shall ... you" are restored; "sat down," "my power" and "Sun God" are uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's sight of the cedar and his paling are told three times: by the narrator, in Gilgamesh's question, in Enkidu's answer. The two "who can withstand" questions form a parallel pair.
- **Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version for the stop at Hamran, the Amorites, or Huwawa seeing them on the road; SB's own Tablet IV ends with lost lines that George says must have told of the final approach (p. 35). Related SB passages (SB's own text):
  - Fear at the cedar: in SB V 1-30 the heroes stand gazing at the forest, and as the cedar's shadow falls terror seizes Gilgamesh, stiffening his arms and shaking his legs, and Enkidu urges him on (V 27-44). In Schøyen2 it is Enkidu who turns pale and Gilgamesh who reassures him: the roles are reversed.
  - Huwawa's awareness: in SB V about 61-72 Humbaba talks to himself and guesses that Enkidu is behind the intrusion; in Schøyen2 Huwawa simply sees them.
  - The Sun God's promise: in SB, Shamash's voice tells Gilgamesh not to fear and to stand against Humbaba only at the forest (V 95-100); here Gilgamesh reports an earlier promise to go with him.
  - Places: Hamran (Schøyen2) vs. Mount Lebanon (SB IV).

## Fragment 7. The Nippur tablet (OB Ni): the third and fourth dreams on the journey to the Cedar Forest (pp. 183-185)
**The source (George, pp. 183-184):** found on Tablet Hill at Nippur in the 1951-52 season, in the back room of a house that doubled as a scribal school. It dates to the mid-18th century BC, shortly before the economic collapse in southern Babylonia that shut the school and emptied the house. It is now in the Iraq Museum, Baghdad. A pupil's exercise, it holds part of the dreams on the approach to the forest; chapter 1 uses it to restore SB IV 142, where the fourth dream is told and read.

### OB.25 The end of the third dream's explanation: the old man is Lugalbanda (OB Ni about 1-8; p. 184)
- **Where and when:** on the journey, close to the forest (the speaker says they are getting nearer to it); time not stated.
- **Who:** the speaker is not named in the surviving lines; he is explaining a dream and calls the listener his friend, so it is Enkidu speaking to Gilgamesh by context ⚠️.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (The tablet starts partway through an explanation; the dream itself is not on it.)
  2. The speaker says they are closing in on the forest, the dreams are near and so is the battle; Gilgamesh will see the shining auras of the god Huwawa, whom he dreads so much in his mind.
  3. He goes on: Gilgamesh will lock horns with him like a bull, beat him and force his head down by strength.
  4. He goes on: the old man in the dream was Gilgamesh's mighty god, his father, the divine Lugalbanda.
- **Specifics:** Huwawa's radiant auras; Huwawa called a god; the bull simile; the old man; Lugalbanda as Gilgamesh's begetter.
- **Short quotations:** "the one who begot you" (OB Ni 8).
- **Gaps:** the dream being explained is lost (not on this tablet). No brackets in these lines.
- **Repetitions:** none within the passage.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IV (SB's own text). SB's third dream is the storm dream (IV 99-107), whose explanation is lost. No old man appears in SB's own dream text (SB calls Gilgamesh Lugalbanda's wild bull only in the prologue, I 35). So the old-man dream and this explanation have no counterpart in the standard version. ⚠️ Whether this Nippur "third dream" is the same dream that the Schøyen2 tablet announces at its end (OB.24) is not stated by George; they are separate tablets.

### OB.26 The fourth dream: the Lion-Bird (OB Ni 9-19, gap, 20´-26´; pp. 184-185)
- **Where and when:** on the journey; not stated.
- **Who:** the dreamer (Gilgamesh by context) and the explainer (Enkidu by context); neither is named in the surviving lines ⚠️.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The dreamer tells his friend of a fourth dream, better than the other three.
  2. Dream (retold): a Lion-Bird appeared in the sky, climbing like a cloud and hovering above them; it was a … (lost) with a twisted face, fire in its mouth and death in its breath.
  3. Dream, continued: a man of strange shape was there too, who … (lost) and stood in the dream; he [*tied*] (uncertain) the bird's wings, took the dreamer's arm and threw it down in front of him; … (broken).
  4. (Short lacuna.)
  5. The explainer repeats the dream's opening lines (the Lion-Bird rising like a cloud, its twisted face, its fiery mouth and deadly breath), then says the dreamer will be afraid of its dreadful aura, and that he, the explainer, will … its foot and let the dreamer get up.
  6. He ends: the man in the dream was mighty Shamash.
- **Specifics:** the Lion-Bird (the Babylonian Anzû, imagined as a lion-headed eagle or a flying stallion; Glossary, p. 223); its fiery mouth and deadly breath; the strangely formed man; Shamash, "mighty."
- **Short quotations:** "I saw a Lion-Bird in the sky" (OB Ni 11).
- **Gaps:** what the Lion-Bird was (Ni 13, 22) and what the man did (Ni 16) are lost; Ni 18-19 broken; the man's introduction, the binding verb and "before" are restored; a short lacuna after Ni 19; Ni 20´-21´ mostly restored from the dream lines.
- **Repetitions:** the explanation repeats the dream's first four lines word for word.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IV. George fills SB's poorly preserved fourth dream (around IV 142) with this same Nippur text (SB IV pp. 33-34), so the parallel is this text. SB's own explanation then resumes (IV 155-162), badly broken: the dream is good; something about Humbaba and something kindled upon him; "we" will bring about his … and bind his wings and stand on him; and next morning the Sun God will give a good sign. Differences: in SB's own lines the binding of wings is something "we" will do to Humbaba; in the Nippur explanation the man who bound the wings is Shamash. The fire-and-death description is also used of Huwawa (Y 110-111, OB.14).

## Fragment 8. The Tell Harmal tablet (Ha1): another dream on the journey to the Cedar Forest (pp. 185-186)
**The source (George, p. 185):** two Old Babylonian Gilgamesh tablets came out of a private house at Tell Harmal (ancient Shaduppûm, on Baghdad's eastern edge) in the third season of digging, August 1947. Both date to the early 18th century BC, look like school exercises and are now in the Iraq Museum. One records a talk between Gilgamesh and Enkidu as they reach the Cedar Forest, but it is too damaged to translate as connected text (not given). The other, given here, has one of Gilgamesh's dreams on the road and Enkidu's reading of it; chapter 1 uses most of it to fill the gap in SB IV at line 183.

### OB.27 The dream of the wild bull; Shamash and Lugalbanda as helpers (Ha1 1-17; pp. 185-186)
- **Where and when:** on the journey, by a mountain crag; time not stated.
- **Who:** the dreamer and the explainer are not named in the surviving lines (Gilgamesh and Enkidu by context ⚠️). Dreamer → friend; explainer → dreamer.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The dreamer tells his friend to climb the crag and look at … (broken), and complains that he has been deprived of the sleep of the gods.
  2. He says he has dreamed, and that the dream was *ominous*, *bleak* (both uncertain) and obscure.
  3. Dream (retold): he had grabbed hold of a wild bull; its bellowing split the ground and the dust it stirred up rose far into the sky; he *bent forward* (uncertain) before it.
  4. Dream, continued: someone gripped … and held his arms; … pulled him free … *by force* (uncertain); his cheek …; [gave] him water [to drink] from a waterskin.
  5. The explainer says that the [god] they are going against is not the wild bull, strange as he is; the *wild bull* (uncertain) was radiant Shamash, who will take their hands in danger.
  6. He goes on: the one who gave him water from the waterskin was his own god, who honors him, the divine Lugalbanda.
  7. He ends: if they join forces, they have a single task, a deed never done before in the land.
- **Specifics:** the mountain crag; the sleep of the gods; a wild bull; its bellowing; clouds of dust; a waterskin; Shamash "shining"; Lugalbanda as the god who honors him.
- **Short quotations:** "the sleep of the gods" (Ha1 about 2).
- **Gaps:** Ha1 1 broken at the end; Ha1 about 7-9 broken in several places; "me," "he gave," "to drink" and "god" are restored.
- **Repetitions:** "strange as he is" repeats the Schøyen2 explanation formula (OB.22). The never-done-before deed repeats Y about 17 (OB.11).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IV. After the announcement of a fifth dream (IV about 182, restored) SB's text is lost (George: a lacuna at IV 183); George fills it with this same Ha1 text from line 3 on (SB IV p. 35), so the parallel is this text. Differences that can be seen:
  - Lines not used in SB: Ha1 1-2 (the mountain crag; the lost sleep of the gods) appear only here.
  - The helpers: here the wild bull is Shamash and the water-giver is Lugalbanda; in Schøyen2 the lion-like man is Shamash (OB.22); in the Nippur tablet the old man is Lugalbanda and the man who binds the Lion-Bird is Shamash (OB.25-26).

## Fragment 9. The Ishchali tablet: the slaying of the guardian of the Cedar Forest (pp. 186-188)
**The source (George, p. 186):** a school tablet dug up in 1935 at Ishchali (ancient Nerebtum), a town on the Diyala river just east of Baghdad, from the early 18th century BC; it is now in the Oriental Institute, University of Chicago. Its text corresponds loosely to the end of SB Tablet V, with substantial differences.

**George's synopsis (p. 186), paraphrased:** Gilgamesh has just beaten Huwawa. Enkidu presses him not to spare the enemy, but Gilgamesh would rather chase down Huwawa's auras, the shining supernatural powers that protect him. Enkidu says the auras can easily be caught afterward. The heroes kill Huwawa, apparently his servant as well, and probably the seven auras after that; then they violate the sacred groves and cut the cedar.

### OB.28 Mercy or the auras? The bird and its chicks (Ishchali 6´-18´; p. 187)
- **Where and when:** in the Forest of Cedar, in the thicket, right after Huwawa has been overcome (George's note). The beginning of the tablet is broken.
- **Who:** Enkidu, Gilgamesh; Huwawa (present, defeated, silent in the surviving lines); Huwawa's servant (mentioned). Enkidu → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu urges Gilgamesh to strike down Huwawa, the [*ogre*] (uncertain) [*hated*] (uncertain) by Gilgamesh's gods, and asks [*why*] he is showing him mercy.
  2. Gilgamesh replies that they must press their victory home: the auras are escaping into the thicket, slipping away and their light *fading* (uncertain).
  3. Enkidu answers with a saying: once the bird is caught, where can its chicks go? They can hunt the auras later, when they scatter through the thicket like chicks.
  4. He goes on: strike Huwawa again, and kill his servant *with him* (uncertain).
- **Specifics:** Huwawa as an ogre (restored); the auras escaping and dimming; the thicket; the bird and its chicks; Huwawa's servant.
- **Short quotations:** "catch a bird and where go its chicks?" (Ishchali 15´).
- **Gaps:** the beginning of the tablet is lost (before 6´); Ishchali 8´ is lost; the words "ogre," "abhor" and "why, my friend" are uncertain restorations.
- **Repetitions:** the escaping of the auras is said twice; the speech formula naming Enkidu and Gilgamesh.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB V about 177-282 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Events: in SB Humbaba begs for his life three or more times, offering to serve Gilgamesh and keep timber for him, while Enkidu urges killing him before Enlil learns of it (V about 196-204 and repeats), and Humbaba finally curses them. Ishchali, in its preserved lines, has no pleading and no curse; Enkidu's argument there is about mercy versus chasing the auras.
  - Who hesitates and why: in both, Gilgamesh holds back and Enkidu presses. In SB Gilgamesh is swayed by Humbaba's pleas (Enkidu tells him not to listen, V about 179). In Ishchali Gilgamesh wants to catch the escaping auras first.
  - The auras: SB V 99-100 has Humbaba's seven cloaks (one worn, six taken off), V 148-149 his dreadful auras seized by the heroes, and V 183 Enkidu's warning that Humbaba will put on his auras if he gets home. The bird-and-chicks saying has no SB counterpart.
  - Added in Ishchali: Huwawa's servant.

### OB.29 Gilgamesh kills Huwawa (Ishchali 19´-35´; p. 187)
- **Where and when:** the same place and moment, continuing.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (acts), Enkidu (encourages), Huwawa (killed); someone killed along with him (name lost). No speeches.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh takes his companion's advice: axe in hand, he pulls the sword from his belt.
  2. He strikes Huwawa on the neck while Enkidu *urges him on* (uncertain).
  3. (Broken) Huwawa falls, and the gullies run with his blood.
  4. He fells Huwawa the guardian; for two leagues around … (broken).
  5. He kills … (name lost) along with him; something is done to the woods (broken).
  6. He kills the ogre who guarded the forest, whose shout had *split* (uncertain) the peaks of Sirion and Lebanon, made the mountains *shake* (uncertain) and the hillsides tremble.
  7. He kills the ogre who guarded the cedar; … (broken).
- **Specifics:** axe and sword; the blow to the neck; gullies running with blood; two leagues; Sirion (the Anti-Lebanon range, including Mount Hermon; Glossary, p. 224) and Lebanon; Huwawa as ogre, guardian of the forest and guardian of the cedar.
- **Short quotations:** "the ravines did run with his blood" (Ishchali 25´).
- **Gaps:** Ishchali 24´, 27´, 28´, 29´, 32´, 33´ and 35´ are partly broken. Whom he killed with Huwawa is lost; George's synopsis says apparently the servant ⚠️.
- **Repetitions:** the line "He slew the ogre," first as the forest's guardian, then as the cedar's (30´, 34´).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB V 285 to about 292 (SB's own text). In SB Gilgamesh, heeding his friend, draws the sword at his side and stabs Humbaba in the neck; Enkidu tears out his heart and lungs; tusks are taken from the head as booty; blood pours down on the mountain. Differences:
  - Weapons: Ishchali axe and sword; SB sword.
  - Enkidu's part: Ishchali has him urging Gilgamesh on; SB has him cutting out the heart and lungs.
  - Added in SB: the tusks as booty. Added in Ishchali: a second victim, the two leagues, the gullies of blood.
  - The mountains: Ishchali says Huwawa's shout had split Sirion and Lebanon (an epithet, given at his death); in SB Sirion and Lebanon are shattered during the fight, when the ground splits under the heroes' heels (V 155-156).
  - Before the killing: SB has Humbaba's curse that the pair should not grow old and that only Gilgamesh would bury Enkidu (V about 279-280); Ishchali has none in its preserved lines.

### OB.30 The seven auras slain; the forest trampled; the secret abode of the gods (Ishchali about 36´-41´; p. 188)
- **Where and when:** the Forest of Cedar, after the killing.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Enkidu → Gilgamesh at the end.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Once he has killed all seven [auras] (restored),
  2. he picks up a war-net *weighing two talents* (uncertain), an eight-talent sword and a ten-talent load,
  3. and goes into the forest to tread it down.
  4. He finds the hidden dwelling of the gods; Gilgamesh cuts trees while Enkidu picks the *timber* (uncertain).
  5. Enkidu urges Gilgamesh to strike the cedar (the start of the line is lost).
  6. A few badly damaged lines follow and then the tablet breaks off (George, p. 188).
- **Specifics:** "all seven" (the word "auras" restored); a war-net of 2 talents; a sword of 8 talents; a load of 10 talents; the gods' hidden dwelling.
- **Short quotations:** "the secret abode of the gods" (Ishchali about 39´).
- **Gaps:** "of the auras" is George's restoration; the tablet itself says only "all seven" ⚠️. Ishchali 41´ is broken at the start. The last lines are not translated.
- **Repetitions:** the finding of the gods' hidden dwelling recurs in the next tablet (OB.31, line about 18).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB V 300 to about 312 (SB's own text). In SB Gilgamesh tramples the forest to gather cedar resin for Enlil's table; Enkidu worries about Enlil (V about 302-306); then, after the killing of Humbaba's seven sons (six names survive: Cricket, Screecher, Typhoon, Screamer, Crafty, Storm), with two-talent hatchets that cut chips three and a half cubits long, Gilgamesh fells trees while Enkidu chooses the *best timber* (V about 306-312). Differences:
  - The seven: Ishchali has seven [auras]; SB has seven sons with names.
  - Numbers: Ishchali war-net 2 talents, sword 8, load 10; SB hatchets of 2 talents each and chips 3 1/2 cubits long.
  - Places: Ishchali's hidden dwelling of the gods; SB earlier calls the Cedar Mountain the gods' seat and the goddesses' throne (V about 6).
  - Added in SB: resin for Enlil's table; Enkidu's fear of Enlil's anger (see OB.31).
  - The same in both: Gilgamesh fells, Enkidu chooses the timber.

## Fragment 10. The Iraq Museum tablet of unknown provenance: felling trees in the Cedar Forest (pp. 188-189)
**The source (George, pp. 188-189):** an Old Babylonian tablet that reached the Iraq Museum by gift or seizure; nothing is known of where it came from beyond its being from somewhere in Iraq. It is badly damaged. What can be read concerns the cutting of the cedar and overlaps a little with the Ishchali tablet.

### OB.31 Enkidu's counsel: no dishonor; the great door for Enlil (lines 17 to about 29; pp. 188-189)
- **Where and when:** the Forest of Cedar, after Huwawa's death.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (implied: he is the one trampling the forest), Enkidu (called "the Wild-Born"). Enkidu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. … (broken) he tramples through the Cedar Forest and finds the gods' hidden dwelling.
  2. Enkidu, the one born in the wild, who knew how to advise, tells his friend that he killed the guardian by his own strength alone, so nothing can bring him shame; he should flatten the Cedar [Forest].
  3. Enkidu goes on: Gilgamesh should find him a towering cedar whose top reaches the sky.
  4. Enkidu goes on: he will make from it a door a reed wide that *needs no* (uncertain) pivots but swings in its jamb, a cubit on its side and a reed across; no outsider is to come near it, and a god is to love it.
  5. Enkidu goes on: the Euphrates will carry it to Enlil's house; the people of Nippur will rejoice over it, and Enlil will take delight in it.
- **Specifics:** the door, a reed wide and a cubit on its side, without pivots, turning in its jamb; a cedar reaching the sky; the Euphrates; Nippur; Enlil's house; Enkidu's epithet "the Wild-Born."
- **Short quotations:** "what can bring you dishonour?" (line about 21).
- **Gaps:** the tablet before line 17 is too damaged to translate; "Cedar" and the final "it" are restored; "let it not need" is uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** the finding of the gods' hidden dwelling repeats Ishchali (OB.30). The phrase calling Enkidu the wild-born adviser is the formula SB uses before Enkidu explains a dream (SB IV 26).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB V about 300-319 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Enkidu's stance, opposite: in SB Enkidu says they have turned the forest into a wasteland and asks how they will answer Enlil in Nippur, who will ask why, after killing the guardian by his might, Gilgamesh went on to trample the forest in rage (V about 302-306). On the Iraq Museum tablet Enkidu says that since Gilgamesh killed the guardian by his strength alone nothing can shame him, and urges him to flatten the forest. The same half-line about killing the guardian leads in SB to fear of Enlil and here to an urging to fell more.
  - The door's size: SB six rods high, two rods wide, one cubit thick (V about 316); here a reed wide and a cubit on its side.
  - The pivots: SB has pole and pivots carved in one piece with the door (V about 317); here it needs no pivots and turns in its jamb.
  - Added here: no outsider is to approach it, a god is to love it, Enlil is to delight in it. Both have the Euphrates carrying it to Enlil's house and Nippur rejoicing.
  - Added in SB: the raft of cedar and cypress logs and Gilgamesh carrying Humbaba's head (V 320 to about 323); and, later, Enkidu's curse on this door on his deathbed (SB VII 37-63), which has no counterpart in any fragment of chapter 3.

## Fragment 11. The Ur tablet: Enkidu curses the hunter and the prostitute (pp. 189-192)
**The source (George, p. 189):** recovered during Sir Leonard Woolley's digs at Ur (1922-1934), in southern Babylonia west of modern Nasiriyah, and now in the British Museum. It probably dates to the 12th century BC (Middle Babylonian). It is an extract from an edition close to the standard version but earlier. On his deathbed Enkidu curses the trapper and the prostitute, then blesses the prostitute, and begins to describe his dream of the Netherworld. The text matches SB VII 90-171 and helps to restore it. (Introduction, p. xxvii: this tablet and the Nippur creation fragment are the only two tablets from Babylonia of Sîn-leqi-unninni's era; they closely resemble the standard version but differ, and it is hard to say whether they come from just before or just after his edition.)

### OB.32 Dawn: Enkidu's curse on the hunter (Ur 1-8; pp. 189-190)
- **Where and when:** Enkidu's sickbed (George's note: deathbed); at the first light of dawn. This is the start of the excerpt.
- **Who:** Enkidu; Shamash (addressed, silent here); the hunter (cursed, absent).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. As dawn begins to break, Enkidu lifts his head and weeps to Shamash, his tears falling in the sunlight.
  2. He calls on Shamash to act against the hunter, the trapper.
  3. He curses him: since the "shackler" kept Enkidu from getting as much as his friend, may the hunter not get as much as his friend; may his gains be cut and his income shrink, his share reduced before Shamash.
- **Specifics:** dawn; the sun's rays; the hunter as trapper and "shackler"; profit, income, share.
- **Short quotations:** "the 'shackler'" (Ur 5).
- **Gaps:** none in these lines.
- **Repetitions:** the curse mirrors the grievance (not as much as my friend / not as much as his friend).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII 90-99 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - The appeal: in SB Enkidu laments to Shamash and appeals for his precious life before naming the hunter (VII 91-94); in Ur he weeps and appeals on the hunter's account.
  - Epithet added in Ur: the "shackler."
  - Added in SB: a curse that the god of any house the hunter enters should leave by the window (VII 99). Ur has no such line.
  - What comes before: SB VII 1-89 (the dream of the gods' assembly, the curse of the door, Gilgamesh's reply) is not on the Ur excerpt, which begins here.

### OB.33 The curse on Shamhat; Shamash's voice (Ur 9-42, with a gap at about 34-38 and after 42; pp. 190-191)
- **Where and when:** the same dawn, continuing.
- **Who:** Enkidu; Shamhat (addressed by name, absent or silent); Shamash (whose voice cries out; his words are lost). Enkidu → Shamhat.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. When he has cursed the hunter to his satisfaction, Enkidu resolves to curse the harlot as well.
  2. He tells Shamhat he will decree her fate, with a great curse that will strike her at once.
  3. The curses: she will never have a home to enjoy; she will not sit in the young women's *quarters* (uncertain); the dirt will soil her best dress, and drunks will muddy her party clothes.
  4. More curses: she will never own a house furnished with pots and dishes; [her home will be a potter's] *clay pit* (uncertain); she will have no [bedroom, household shrine or] sacred [*hearth*]; no fine bed, chair or table will furnish her room.
  5. More curses: a hard bench will be her couch; she will sit in the dust where the potters' paths cross, sleep in a ruined field and stand in the shadow of the city wall.
  6. More curses: briars and thorns will cut her feet; drunk and sober alike will slap her face; [*the riffraff*] of the street will crowd into her brothel, and fights will break out [*in her tavern*].
  7. More curses: [*wives*] will sue her; [no builder will plaster her roof]; [*wild*] *dogs* (uncertain) will lie in her room; [no feast will ever be held in her house].
  8. (Gap of several lines, about Ur 34-38.)
  9. He gives his reason: [because] she weakened him in the wild when he was pure.
  10. [Shamash hears] him, and [at once] a [voice] calls from the sky.
  11. (Short lacuna, about Ur 43-47: Shamash's words are lost.)
- **Specifics:** home, young women's quarters, best dress, party clothes, pots and dishes, the potter's clay pit, bedroom, household shrine, hearth, bed, chair, table, hard bench, the dust of the potters' crossing, a ruined field, the shade of the wall, briars and thorns, a brothel, a tavern, wives suing, an unplastered roof, wild dogs, banquets.
- **Short quotations:** "I will fix your destiny" (Ur 11).
- **Gaps:** the restorations marked above; about Ur 34-38 lost; about Ur 43-47 lost.
- **Repetitions:** the reason (her weakening him when he was pure) is said twice (Ur 39-40); the fate-fixing opening returns at the start of the blessing (OB.34, Ur 48).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII 100-147 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Added in SB: that the doom will last forever (VII 103); that she will never live within a family (VII 107); that she will never have a household full of beautiful things (VII 111).
  - Added in Ur: the house furnished with pots and dishes; the riffraff crowding her brothel; the tavern fights.
  - Wording: Ur's potters' crossing vs. SB's crossroads (VII about 116); Ur's bedding down vs. SB's sleeping (VII about 117); Ur's wild dogs in her room vs. SB's owl nesting there (VII 122).
  - Shamash's reply: SB gives it in full (VII 134-147). He asks why Enkidu curses Shamhat, who fed him bread fit for a god and ale fit for a king, dressed him finely and gave him Gilgamesh as companion; he foretells that Gilgamesh will lay him on a bed of honor, have Uruk mourn him and roam the wild in a lion skin. In Ur these words are lost in the lacuna.

### OB.34 The blessing of Shamhat (Ur 48-58; pp. 191-192)
- **Where and when:** the same deathbed, after Shamash has spoken (his words are lost).
- **Who:** Enkidu → Shamhat.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Enkidu tells Shamhat he will [decree her fate] again: the mouth that cursed her will now bless her.
  2. The blessing: governors and nobles will love her; men a league away will slap their thighs at her, and two leagues away will shake their hair loose.
  3. More blessing: no soldier will hesitate to undo his belt for her; he will give her a "nail" ornament and a necklace, and gold earrings for her ears.
  4. More blessing: Ishtar, most capable of the gods, will get her into the house of a man whose home is secure and whose riches are [piled] high; his wife, though she has borne seven children, will be abandoned for her.
- **Specifics:** governors, nobles, soldiers; one league and two leagues; nail and necklace; gold earrings; Ishtar as the most capable of the gods; the mother of seven.
- **Short quotations:** "nail and necklace" (Ur 54).
- **Gaps:** "I will fix," "destiny" and "heaped" are restored.
- **Repetitions:** the fate-fixing opening repeats the start of the curse (Ur 11).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII 148-161 (SB's own text, much of it restored in brackets). Differences:
  - Before the blessing: SB says Enkidu's angry heart calmed when he heard Shamash (VII 148-150); in Ur those lines fall in the lacuna.
  - The gifts: Ur has the nail ornament and necklace; SB has obsidian, lapis lazuli and gold (VII 157). Both then have gold earrings.
  - Otherwise the blessing is the same in substance and order: governors and nobles, one league and two leagues, the soldier's belt, Ishtar's help, the abandoned wife with seven children.

### OB.35 Enkidu begins to tell his dream of the Netherworld (Ur 59-69; p. 192)
- **Where and when:** the same deathbed; the dream came the night before.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh (sitting before Enkidu), Enkidu. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (dream retold).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh sits facing him.
  2. While he turns over what is on his mind (⚠️ the English does not make clear whether "he" is Gilgamesh or Enkidu), Enkidu speaks.
  3. Dream (retold by Enkidu): last night's dream was a remarkable one; the sky roared, the earth answered, and he stood between them.
  4. Dream, continued: a man appeared with a dark face, his look as fierce as a roaring Lion-Bird; [his] hands were lion's paws and [his] claws eagle's talons.
  5. Dream, continued: the man grabbed him by the hair and *overcame* (uncertain) him.
  6. The tablet ends here.
- **Specifics:** the roaring sky and echoing earth; the dark-faced man; the roaring Lion-Bird; lion's paws; eagle's talons; seized by the hair.
- **Short quotations:** "standing between them" (Ur about 64).
- **Gaps:** "his" is restored twice; "overpowered" is uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** none within the passage.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB VII 162-171 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Who is present: Ur has Gilgamesh sitting before him; SB has Enkidu troubled, lying alone and brooding, then telling his friend what was on his mind (VII 162-164).
  - Wording: Ur's "dream of last night" vs. SB's dream "in the course of the night" (VII 165); Ur's "roaring" Lion-Bird vs. SB's plain Lion-Bird (VII 169).
  - Outcome: SB continues (VII 172-208) with the struggle, Enkidu turned into a dove, the journey to the House of Dust and what he saw there; Ur breaks off as the man overpowers him. See the Megiddo tablet (chapter 4 file, M.11-M.12) for another early version of this dream.

## Fragment 12. The Sippar tablet (Si): Gilgamesh at the end of the world (pp. 192-196)
**The source (George, p. 192):** two pieces that join, one now in the British Museum and the other in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin. Both were bought in Baghdad in 1902 and said to come from Sippar, on the Euphrates above Babylon. They date to the 18th or 17th century BC. This is a library tablet, two columns to a side, not a school exercise, so it probably belonged to a full edition of the epic. Its text runs alongside parts of SB Tablets IX-X with a number of differences, and chapter 1 borrows some lines (column i is placed at SB IX 18). (Introduction, p. xxii: one of three finely written Old Babylonian library tablets, with the Pennsylvania and Yale tablets.) The Berlin piece, published by Bruno Meissner in 1902, is quoted in two public-domain works used below: Jastrow and Clay 1920 (introduction) and R. Campbell Thompson, *The Epic of Gilgamish* (1928), cited as "Thompson 1928."

**George's synopsis (pp. 192-193), paraphrased:** grief-stricken at Enkidu's death, Gilgamesh roams the wild looking for the secret of everlasting life. The Sun God speaks to him from the sky and tells him the search is hopeless. Gilgamesh refuses a life of quiet rest: death will bring rest forever soon enough, so he must lengthen his life if he can. After a break, he is telling Shiduri how his friend died and how he came to fear death. The tavern-keeper answers that only the gods live forever; a man should enjoy his life while it lasts and care for the family that will give him descendants. Gilgamesh will not be convinced. After a second break he is breaking the stone crew of the ferryman Sursunabu (the later Ur-shanabi). Sursunabu gives his name; Gilgamesh describes his journey and asks for help reaching the immortal Uta-napishti, here called Uta-naishtim. Sursunabu explains that the Stone Ones were how he crossed the ocean, but tells him to make punting-poles.

### OB.36 In the wild: Shamash speaks to Gilgamesh (Si i 2´-15´; pp. 192-193)
- **Where and when:** the wild; time not stated. The beginning of the column is lost.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; Shamash (called a hero). Shamash → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Shamash.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Beginning lost.) [He] dresses in their hides and eats their meat (whose, the tablet does not preserve ⚠️).
  2. Gilgamesh [*digs*] (uncertain) wells where there had never been any and *drinks* (uncertain) as he pursues the winds.
  3. Shamash becomes troubled, *bends down* (uncertain) and asks Gilgamesh where he is wandering; the life he is looking for he will never find.
  4. Gilgamesh answers the hero Shamash: after all his *roaming* (uncertain) in the wild, will there be too little rest once he is in the Netherworld? He will sleep there through all the years.
  5. He goes on: let his eyes look at the sun and have their fill of light. The darkness is *far off* (uncertain); how much light remains? When do the dead ever see sunlight?
- **Specifics:** hides and meat; wells dug where none had been; chasing the winds; the Netherworld; light, darkness, sunlight.
- **Short quotations:** "the life which thou seekest / Thou shalt not find" (Thompson 1928, Tablet IX, p. 43). Thompson reads the lines before Shamash's speech differently, as a crossing "where none hath been ever" while the gale drives the water; George reads wells that never existed and the chasing of winds ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** the start of column i is lost; "he," "dug" and a second "he" are restored.
- **Repetitions:** Shamash's two lines (i 7´-8´) are repeated word for word by the tavern-keeper (OB.38, iii 1-2).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB IX. SB's own text breaks off after IX 18 (Gilgamesh kills the lions at a mountain pass); George fills the lacuna with this same Sippar column (SB IX p. 69), so there is no independent SB wording for Shamash's speech. In George's SB arrangement, the hides therefore follow the killing of the lions (IX 15-18). Related SB lines (SB's own text): the prologue's well-digging on the upland slopes (I 39); Gilgamesh telling Uta-napishti that he killed bear, hyena, lion, panther, cheetah, deer and ibex and ate their meat and skinned them (X 259-261). No speech by Shamash to the wandering Gilgamesh survives in SB's own text.

### OB.37 Gilgamesh tells the tavern-keeper of Enkidu's death and his fear (Si ii 1´ to about 13´, with one line before ii 1´; p. 194)
- **Where and when:** at the tavern-keeper's (she lives on the ocean shore, Si iii 21, restored); after a long lacuna (George, p. 193).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; the tavern-keeper (listening). Gilgamesh → tavern-keeper.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Long lacuna before this column; how Gilgamesh reached her is lost.)
  2. Gilgamesh tells the tavern-keeper about [his dearly loved friend] who shared every danger with him: Enkidu, his dearly loved friend who shared every danger.
  3. He goes on: Enkidu met the fate of all mortals. Gilgamesh wept for him day and night and would not let him be buried, hoping his friend might rise at his cry, for seven days and seven nights, until a maggot fell out of his nose.
  4. He goes on: since Enkidu died he has found no life, roaming the wild like a trapper.
  5. He goes on: now that he has seen the tavern-keeper's face, may he never see death, which he dreads so much.
- **Specifics:** seven days and seven nights; the maggot from Enkidu's nostril; the fate of mortals; roaming like a trapper; the tavern-keeper's face.
- **Short quotations:** "unto the tomb I have not consign’d him" (Thompson 1928, Tablet X, p. 45). Thompson reads the time as "six days, (yea), a se’nnight" and the hope as a lament that his friend does not come at his call; George reads seven days and seven nights and the hope that his friend might rise at his cry ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** the first line, before ii 1´, is wholly restored from ii 2´.
- **Repetitions:** the beloved-friend couplet is said twice.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB X 46 to about 71 (SB's own text, though much of X 46-71 is restored in brackets from the repeats at X 118-148 and X 219-248). Differences:
  - Numbers: Sippar (George) has seven days and seven nights; SB has [six days and seven nights] (X about 58, restored; read at X 135 and X 235).
  - Added in Sippar: the hope that Enkidu might rise at his cry; the weeping day and night; roaming like a trapper; the wish, on seeing her face, never to see death. None is in SB.
  - Added in SB: the tavern-keeper bars her gate on seeing him and Gilgamesh threatens to break it down (X 10-22); she asks why his cheeks are sunken and he answers point by point (X 35 to about 52); he calls Enkidu a fleeing wild ass, an upland donkey, a panther of the wild; he lists their deeds; he says he became afraid that he too would die; he says his friend has turned to clay and asks whether he will not lie down like him forever (X about 53-71). The Sippar tablet's text for the arrival is lost in the lacuna, so whether it had the barred gate cannot be known ⚠️.
  - Repetition across the poem: in SB Gilgamesh tells this story three times (to Shiduri, Ur-shanabi and Uta-napishti); in the Sippar tablet's preserved lines he tells it once, and his words to the ferryman are short (OB.40).
  - Names: Sippar never names the tavern-keeper in its surviving lines; SB names her Shiduri (X 1).

### OB.38 The tavern-keeper's advice: life the gods kept for themselves (Si about ii 14´ to iii 15; pp. 194-195)
- **Where and when:** the tavern-keeper's, directly after OB.37.
- **Who:** the tavern-keeper → Gilgamesh. In the surviving lines she is called only the tavern-keeper, and Gilgamesh addresses her as [the ale-wife] (restored, iii 16). George's synopsis (p. 193) and Introduction (p. xlv) call her Shiduri. (Jastrow and Clay 1920, introduction pp. 11-12 and their note on "Sabitum," record Paul Haupt's 1920 suggestion that her title means "innkeeper.")
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The tavern-keeper asks Gilgamesh where he is wandering and tells him he will never find the life he is looking for.
  2. She explains why: when the gods made mankind, they gave death to humans and held on to life themselves.
  3. She tells him instead to keep his stomach full, to enjoy himself day and night, to celebrate every day and to dance and play day and night.
  4. She tells him to wear clean clothes, wash his hair and bathe.
  5. She tells him to look fondly at the child holding his hand and to let his wife delight in his embrace again and again.
  6. She ends: this is the lot [*of mortals*] (restored, uncertain), that whoever lives … (the line breaks off).
- **What the text says, checked line by line:** the speech has four parts. (a) Her first two lines repeat Shamash's words to Gilgamesh exactly (i 7´-8´, OB.36). (b) The reason: the gods allotted death to people and kept life (iii 3-5). (c) Eight lines of instructions for living: food, enjoyment day and night, celebration, dancing and play, clean clothes, washed hair, bathing, the child holding his hand, a wife's embrace (iii 6-13). (d) A closing statement about the lot of [*mortals*], which is broken: the word for mortals is George's uncertain restoration and the end of iii 15 is lost, so how she sums up is unknown ⚠️. The text says nothing about descendants or about building a family line; George's synopsis speaks of nurturing the family that will give him descendants (p. 193), which is his paraphrase of the child-and-wife lines, not words of the text.
- **Specifics:** the gods' making of mankind; death and life; stomach, clothes, hair, water, child, wife; day and night (three times).
- **Short quotations:** "Death they imposed on mankind; / Life they kept in their power." (Jastrow and Clay 1920, introduction, p. 12, quoting the Berlin fragment). Thompson 1928 renders the same lines and ends the speech "for this is the dower [of man]" (Tablet X, p. 46).
- **Gaps:** "of mortal men" (iii 14) is uncertain; iii 15 breaks off after its first words. The speech is introduced at about ii 14´.
- **Repetitions:** iii 1-2 repeat Shamash at i 7´-8´ word for word. The day-and-night phrase recurs at iii 7 and iii 9 (with "each day" at iii 8).
- **Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. In SB X Shiduri says nothing like this: she tells Gilgamesh that no one has ever crossed the ocean except Shamash, that the Waters of Death lie in the middle, and that he should look for Ur-shanabi and the Stone Ones (X 79-91). George, Introduction p. xlv, states that these lines and the advice after them are absent from the late epic's Shiduri episode, and suggests the late poet kept that wisdom back for Uta-napishti. The nearest SB statement on the same subject is Uta-napishti's at the end of Tablet X: the Anunnaki met with Mammitum, maker of fate, and set up both Death and Life, but keep the day of death secret (X 319 to about 322). That passage has none of the instructions about food, clothes, washing, child or wife.

### OB.39 Gilgamesh refuses the advice and asks the way across the ocean (Si iii 16-27; p. 195)
- **Where and when:** the tavern-keeper's, continuing.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh → the tavern-keeper; the tavern-keeper → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh asks [the ale-wife] why she speaks [*like this*], since his heart still aches for his friend; he says it twice, the second time naming Enkidu.
  2. He goes on: she lives on the [*ocean*] shore and knows every [*route across it*]; let her show him the way; if it can be done, he [will cross] the ocean.
  3. The tavern-keeper replies that there has never [*before*] been anyone like him; who [*except Shamash*] could make [*that journey*]?
  4. (Further lacuna: her directions to the ferryman, if any, are lost.)
- **Specifics:** the ocean shore (restored); the crossing; Shamash (restored).
- **Short quotations:** "sick for Enkidu" (Si iii 20). (No public-domain translation of these lines is on hand; Thompson 1928 notes that the Old Babylonian text breaks off after the advice, Tablet X, p. 46.)
- **Gaps:** almost every key phrase is an uncertain restoration: the name "ale-wife," "this way," "still very," "of the ocean," "the ways across it," "show me," "I will cross," the second "to Gilgamesh," "before," "but Shamash," "that journey." Read are the question why she talks so, his heart's sickness for his friend and for Enkidu, the shore, the plea to be shown the way, the "if it may be done," the ocean, the "never ... one like you" and the verb "travel."
- **Repetitions:** the why-do-you-talk couplet is said twice.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB X 72-91 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - Events added in Sippar: Gilgamesh's rejection of her advice because his heart still aches for his friend. SB has no advice to reject.
  - The request: Sippar asks her, as one who knows the ways across, to show him; SB asks for the road to Uta-napishti and its landmark, vowing to cross the ocean if possible and otherwise to roam the wild (X 73-77). Sippar does not name Uta-naishtim here (in the surviving lines) and lacks the roam-the-wild alternative.
  - The reply: Sippar has the tavern-keeper marvel that there has never been anyone like him and ask who [but Shamash] could go; SB has Shiduri say there has never been a crossing, that only Shamash crosses, that the way is dangerous with the Waters of Death midway, and that Ur-shanabi and the Stone Ones are to be found (X 79-91). The Shamash idea is shared (restored in Sippar); the Waters of Death warning and the directions to Ur-shanabi are not in Sippar's preserved lines.

### OB.40 The Stone Ones smashed; Gilgamesh and Sursunabu exchange names (Si iv 1 to about 17; pp. 195-196)
- **Where and when:** by the ocean, where the ferryman is; after a further lacuna (George notes that the story has moved on to the Stone Ones and the ferryman).
- **Who:** Gilgamesh; Sursunabu (the ferryman; a variant form of the name Ur-shanabi, Glossary, p. 224); the Stone Ones (smashed). Sursunabu → Gilgamesh; Gilgamesh → Sursunabu; Sursunabu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. (Lacuna.) … he breaks [the Stone Ones] in his rage.
  2. [Gilgamesh] returns and stands over Sursunabu, who looks him straight in the eye.
  3. Sursunabu asks Gilgamesh his name and introduces himself as Sursunabu, servant of Uta-naishtim the Faraway.
  4. Gilgamesh gives his name and says he has come from Uruk-Eanna, having made his way around the mountains by the hidden road of the sunrise.
  5. He goes on: now that he has found Sursunabu, let him be shown Uta-naishtim the Faraway.
  6. Sursunabu replies (broken) that [*to reach*] Uta-naishtim he must go by boat, and that he will take him there.
- **Specifics:** the Stone Ones; Uta-naishtim "the Distant" (a variant form of Uta-napishti; Glossary, p. 224); Uruk-Eanna; the hidden road of the sunrise; a boat.
- **Short quotations:** "Tell to me what is thy name" (Thompson 1928, Tablet X, p. 47). Thompson's 1928 reading of the Berlin column calls the ferryman Ur-Shanabi, not Sursunabu, and places the smashing of the "sails(?)" in the Assyrian text; George reads Sursunabu and the Stone Ones ⚠️.
- **Gaps:** iv 1 is broken at the start; "the Stone Ones" and "Gilgamesh" are restored; iv 14-17 are broken at the start of each line (the "to reach" phrase is uncertain).
- **Repetitions:** the epithet "the Distant" with Uta-naishtim's name, three times; the speech formula naming each speaker.
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB X 92-154 (SB's own text). Differences:
  - The fight: SB tells it in full. Gilgamesh creeps up and falls on them like an arrow; Ur-shanabi puts on his aura and grabs an axe, trembling; Gilgamesh hits his head, seizes his hair and pins his arm; the Stone Ones, who did not fear the Waters of Death, had sealed the boat; Gilgamesh wades into the ocean, stops the boat, smashes the Stone Ones, throws them into the river, ties up the boat and sits on the bank (X 92-108). Sippar keeps only the smashing in rage and Gilgamesh standing over Sursunabu.
  - The words after the fight: in SB Gilgamesh mocks Ur-shanabi for picking up an axe while shaking and says he will not fight him (X 109-111). Not in Sippar.
  - Names exchanged: in Sippar Sursunabu asks Gilgamesh's name and gives his own; no counterpart in SB, where Ur-shanabi instead asks why his cheeks are hollow and Gilgamesh repeats his whole lament (X 112-148).
  - Gilgamesh's self-description: the way around the mountains and the hidden road of the sunrise have no counterpart in SB X.
  - The offer: in Sippar Sursunabu says at once that he must go by boat and that he will take him (iv 15-17). In SB, Ur-shanabi first tells him that his own hands have blocked his crossing (X 156).
  - Names: Sursunabu vs. Ur-shanabi; Uta-naishtim vs. Uta-napishti.

### OB.41 Why the Stone Ones mattered; three hundred punting-poles (Si about iv 18 to iv 28; p. 196)
- **Where and when:** by the ocean; they sit down together.
- **Who:** Sursunabu, Gilgamesh. Sursunabu → Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. The two [sit down] and talk things over; then *again* (uncertain) Sursunabu speaks.
  2. He explains that the Stone Ones were what let him cross, because he himself may not touch the Waters of Death; Gilgamesh smashed them in his rage, and they were his means of getting across.
  3. He tells Gilgamesh to take the axe and cut him three hundred punting-poles of sixty cubits each, and to fit them with knobs … (broken).
  4. The rest of the tablet is lost.
- **Specifics:** the Waters of Death; the Stone Ones; an axe; 300 punting-poles; 60 cubits each; knobs (bosses).
- **Short quotations:** "each sixty cubits" (Si iv 27). (No public-domain translation of these lines is on hand.)
- **Gaps:** "they sat" (iv 18) is restored; iv 28 is broken; the remainder is lost.
- **Repetitions:** the Stone Ones as his means of crossing, said twice (iv 22, 25).
- **Compared with the standard version:** SB X 155-168 (SB's own text). In SB Ur-shanabi tells Gilgamesh that his own hands have blocked his crossing: he smashed the Stone Ones and threw them in the river, and the *pine* (uncertain) has not been stripped; he must take his axe, go into the forest, cut three hundred punting-poles five rods long, trim them, fit each with a knob and bring them back. Differences:
  - Who must not touch the water: in Sippar it is Sursunabu himself. In SB the Stone Ones are the ones without fear of the Waters of Death (X 103), and it is Gilgamesh who is later warned not to let his hand touch them (X 175).
  - Numbers: both have 300 poles. Length: Sippar sixty cubits; SB five rods. (The book gives no conversion between these units in this chapter.)
  - Added in SB: the unstripped pine, the trip into the forest, and the voyage itself (three days for a month and a half's journey, the poles called for one by one up to the twelfth, 120 double-furlongs, the garment used as a sail; X 169-183). Sippar's text ends before the voyage.

## Chapter close: George's note on the Deluge (p. 196)
Not a scene. George writes that nothing yet shows the Old Babylonian poem contained a Flood story like the one in SB Tablet XI. Old Babylonian Flood stories did exist, told at least two ways: in the Poem of Atram-hasis, narrated in the third person, and in another poem narrated in the first person, as in Gilgamesh. For the newest evidence he points to Irving Finkel, *The Ark before Noah* (London, 2014).

## Open questions and uncertainties
1. **Line numbers in the old tablets.** One verse can run over two or three tablet lines (p. lv), so between George's printed markers the count of printed lines often falls short. Line numbers in this file that are not printed markers are marked "about." Jastrow and Clay 1920 number the Pennsylvania tablet a few lines differently from George in places (for example, their l. 46 is George's P about 48-49); their Yale numbering matches George's closely.
2. **The Nippur creation fragment (OB.2):** who makes the first proposal is lost; the speaker to Aruru, the god Anu, is an uncertain restoration; "equal" is uncertain.
3. **The Cornell tablet (OB.3):** its middle is lost, so it cannot be known whether it had Enkidu's boast or the harlot's long second speech.
4. **The axe dream (OB.5):** George's footnote offers "made it into my brother" as an alternative to keeping the axe at his side, and Jastrow and Clay 1920 read it that way. The interpretation lines come from the Sealand tablet, not from P.
5. **The wedding custom (OB.8):** the veil and the invitation are uncertain renderings; Enkidu's reaction after he paled is lost (about P 166-174). Jastrow and Clay 1920 read the custom quite differently (see OB.8).
6. **Enkidu's role at the festival (OB.9):** "champion" is uncertain, and George introduces his reading that Enkidu becomes the young men's chosen rival with "as it seems" and "presumably" (p. 166).
7. **Who won the wrestling (OB.10):** the text says Gilgamesh went down on one knee, his anger cooled and he stopped; it does not say who won. George's summary says the tablet ends with Enkidu accepting that Gilgamesh's kingship is rightful.
8. **The Yale tablet's lost passages:** the speaker and subject of Y about 14-17 (OB.11); the kindness Enkidu promises the harlot (OB.12); the cause of Enkidu's tears (OB.13); the speaker of the blessing at Y 213-215, perhaps Ninsun in George's view (OB.18); whom Gilgamesh summons and what he instructs (Y 222-223, OB.18); the speaker of Y 279-280 (OB.21).
9. **Who appointed Huwawa (OB.15):** Wer is read in the text, but "was appointed by" and Enlil's name are uncertain restorations; Jastrow and Clay 1920 did not read Wer at all.
10. **The two leaving alone (OB.21):** this is George's summary (p. 173); the surviving text says only that the crowd was sent home and that the two set out (uncertain).
11. **Dream numbering across tablets (OB.22-OB.27):** Schøyen2 counts the mountain dream first, the Hattusa Bo2 tablet counts it second (chapter 4, M.6), and SB uses it as the second. Schøyen2 ends by announcing a third dream that it does not contain; the Nippur tablet begins with the explanation of a third dream (an old man). George does not say whether these are the same dream. The Harmal dream is unnumbered. In the Nippur and Harmal tablets neither the dreamer nor the explainer is named.
12. **The killing (OB.28-OB.30):** Huwawa's pleas, if any, are lost before Ishchali 6´; whom Gilgamesh killed along with him is lost (George: apparently the servant); "of the auras" after "all seven" is a restoration (George: probably the seven auras).
13. **The Ur tablet (OB.33-OB.35):** Shamash's words are lost (about Ur 43-47); in Ur 60 it is unclear whether the one pondering is Gilgamesh or Enkidu.
14. **The Sippar tablet (OB.36-OB.41):** whose hides and meat Gilgamesh takes in col. i is lost on this tablet; the tavern-keeper is never named in the surviving lines; the last line of her advice is broken and "of mortal men" is uncertain; most of Gilgamesh's request and her reply in OB.39 are uncertain restorations; how Gilgamesh found and fought the ferryman, and how the Stone Ones were smashed, is lost. Thompson 1928 reads the Berlin piece with Ur-Shanabi, not Sursunabu, and with six days rather than seven for the mourning.
15. **No Flood story (chapter close):** George says nothing yet shows the Old Babylonian poem included the Deluge narrative (p. 196).
16. **Older public-domain readings.** Jastrow and Clay 1920 and Thompson 1928 often read the P, Y and Sippar lines differently from George 2020; the main differences are noted in OB.4-OB.6, OB.8-OB.9, OB.11-OB.12, OB.14-OB.15, OB.17-OB.20, OB.36-OB.37 and OB.40. George's readings rest on later collation and newly found pieces; the older readings are recorded only so that the quotations are understood.
17. **Wikipedia cross-check** (Wikipedia, "Epic of Gilgamesh," revision 1377304124, section "Old Babylonian versions"; paraphrased):
    - Wikipedia's synopsis calls Shamkatum a priestess; George 2020 calls her the harlot or prostitute (OB.6-OB.9).
    - Wikipedia's synopsis says Enkidu and Shamkatum travel to Uruk to confront Gilgamesh and stop his abuses; George 2020's text breaks off after Enkidu's angry reaction, and George's own reading is that Enkidu arrives as the young men pick a rival for the king (OB.8-OB.9).
    - Wikipedia's synopsis sends the heroes to a "Pine Forest"; George 2020 has the Forest of Cedar throughout (OB.14-OB.17).
    - Wikipedia's synopsis says the elders protest but agree after Gilgamesh talks to them; George 2020 has Gilgamesh laugh at them, a lacuna, and then the elders' blessing and advice, with no agreement scene preserved (OB.17-OB.20).

    - Wikipedia describes the Nippur school tablet as Enkidu interpreting one dream; George 2020's Nippur tablet has the end of one explanation and the whole fourth dream with its explanation (OB.25-OB.26).
    - Wikipedia says the auras are not mentioned in the standard version and appear in a Sumerian poem as "sons"; George 2020's standard version does mention Humbaba's auras (SB V 148-149, V 183) and his seven cloaks (V 99-100), and names seven sons (V about 306-307) (OB.28-OB.30).

## Leads (observations about the text's own structure only)
- **Two dream interpreters.** In Uruk, Gilgamesh's mother reads his dreams (OB.4-OB.5); on the road, Enkidu does (OB.22-OB.27). In every road dream a frightening image (mountain, storm, Lion-Bird, wild bull) is read as Huwawa or as a helper in disguise.
- **Shamash and Lugalbanda as a pair.** They appear together three times: Gilgamesh's two prayers (OB.18-OB.19), the elders' blessings (OB.20), and the dream helpers (Shamash in OB.22, OB.26, OB.27; Lugalbanda in OB.25, OB.27).
- **Repeated formulas across tablets:** the why-do-you-want-this question (OB.11, OB.14, OB.17); the never-done-before deed (OB.11, OB.27); fire in the mouth and death in the breath, for Huwawa (OB.14, OB.17) and for the Lion-Bird (OB.26); the "strange as he is" tag, for the mountain (OB.22) and the wild bull (OB.27); the shepherds' and the townspeople's identical remark about Enkidu (OB.7, OB.9); the warning that the life he seeks will never be found, from Shamash and then from the tavern-keeper (OB.36, OB.38).
- **Seven:** seven days and nights of lovemaking (OB.6) and of mourning (OB.37); seven jugs (OB.7); the Seven Terrors (OB.15); the seven gates (OB.17); "all seven" (OB.30); the mother of seven (OB.34).
- **Godlike:** the harlot calls Enkidu godlike (OB.6) and leads him like a god (OB.7); Gilgamesh is godlike when a rival is set up against him (OB.9).
- **Fear shifts between the heroes:** Enkidu is the one who warns (OB.14-OB.15) and who goes pale at the cedar (OB.24); Gilgamesh comforts him. In the killing scene, Gilgamesh hesitates and Enkidu presses (OB.28).
- **Dream prediction and fulfillment:** the mother says the young men will kiss the newcomer's feet (OB.4); P's preserved lines do not show this happening (OB.9).
