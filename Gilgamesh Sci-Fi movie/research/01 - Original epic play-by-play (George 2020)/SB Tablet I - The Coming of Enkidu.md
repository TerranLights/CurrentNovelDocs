# Tablet I. The Coming of Enkidu: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How to read this file.** Line numbers are George's margin numbers, which are printed every five lines. Every printed marker used here (I 5, I 10 … I 300) has been checked against the corrected extraction. Every other number is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about"; an "about" in front of a range covers both ends. The exception is I 1–4, which are counted from the first line of the tablet. Pages are the printed pages of the 2020 Penguin edition; Tablet I runs pp. 1–11. Words in quotation marks are George's wording. In quotations, [square brackets] are George's restorations of broken text, *italics* are George's italics (an insecure decipherment or uncertain rendering; inside brackets, an uncertain or conjectural restoration; George, p. lvi), and "……" marks lost text. George's own spelling (British) is kept inside quotations. Everything else is paraphrase, kept short because the translation is under copyright.

## 1. George's synopsis (paraphrased)
The tablet opens with a prologue and a hymn of praise. King Gilgamesh oppresses the people of Uruk, and they complain to the gods. The gods make a counterpart for him to absorb his excess energy: the wild man Enkidu, who is raised by wild animals. A trapper spots Enkidu and uses a prostitute to draw him away from his herd. After a week of sex, the prostitute offers to take him to Uruk, where Gilgamesh has been having dreams about him. (George, p. 1. George says the plot summaries, line numbers, linking notes and other small-type material are his modern additions, p. lvi.)

## 2. Scenes

### I.1 Prologue: "He who saw the Deep" (I 1–about 28; pp. 1–2)
- **Where and when:** Outside the story's time. The poet speaks directly to the listener or reader, with commands to look at Uruk and to read the tablet. The deeds mentioned (the long journey, the story of the Flood) lie in Gilgamesh's past.
- **Who:** The poet (narrator). Gilgamesh is the subject; Ishtar and the Seven Sages are named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: praises the man who saw the Deep, knew the right ways and was wise in everything. The couplet is then said again with his name, Gilgamesh (I 1–4).
  2. Narrator: he explored all the seats of power and knew all wisdom; he saw secret and hidden things; he brought back a story from before the Flood (I 5–about 8).
  3. Narrator: he traveled far, grew weary and found peace; all his labors were written on a stone tablet (about I 9–10).
  4. Narrator: he built the rampart of Uruk and of the temple Eanna (about I 11–12).
  5. Narrator → listener (commands): look at the wall and the unmatched parapet; climb the ancient stairway; approach Eanna, Ishtar's seat, which no later king could equal (about I 13–17).
  6. Narrator → listener: go up on the wall and walk along it; inspect the foundations and the brickwork. He asks whether the bricks were not kiln-fired and whether the Seven Sages did not lay the foundations (about I 18–21).
  7. Narrator: gives Uruk's area in four parts (about I 22–23).
  8. Narrator → listener: find the cedar tablet-box, open its bronze clasps and its lid, take out the lapis lazuli tablet, and read aloud everything Gilgamesh suffered (about I 24–28).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "the Deep, the country's foundation" (I 1). George: the Deep is Ea's cosmic domain, especially as the source of wisdom (p. lii).
  - "a tale of before the Deluge" (about I 8); "a tablet of stone" (I 10).
  - "Uruk-the-Sheepfold" (about I 11); "holy Eanna, the sacred storehouse" (about I 12); "seat of Ishtar the goddess" (about I 16).
  - The wall is "like a strand of *wool*" (about I 13; *wool* is in George's italics, so uncertain); "the stairway of a bygone era" (I 15).
  - Bricks "fired in an oven"; foundations laid by "the Seven Sages" (about I 20–21).
  - Area: one square mile of city, one of date-grove and one of clay-pit, plus half a square mile for the temple of Ishtar. The total is three and a half square miles (about I 22–23). The first two items and the total are restored in brackets.
  - "the tablet-box of cedar," "its clasps of bronze," "the tablet of lapis lazuli" (about I 24–27).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "He who saw the Deep, the country's foundation," (I 1). This first line is the ancient title of the standard version, *Sha naqba īmuru* (George, pp. xviii, xxvii).
  - "He came a far road, was weary, found peace," (about I 9).
- **Gaps:** None lost outright. Restorations: words in about I 2, 4, 5–6 and 10; the opening verbs of about I 24–27 (the first, "*Find*," is conjectural); parts of the area line. The area line survives complete when it is repeated in Tablet XI (about XI 327–328, p. 97). ⚠️ The extra-long area line seems to count as two lines (about I 22–23), since the marker I 25 falls on the second line after it. George says over-long lines and doubled-up verses explain cases where the margin count seems not to match (p. lv).
- **Repetitions:** I 3–4 repeat I 1–2 with Gilgamesh's name added. Gilgamesh repeats the wall passage (about I 18–23) almost word for word to Ur-shanabi at the end of Tablet XI (about XI 323–328, pp. 96–97).
- **George's note:** In the prologue, the poet asks us to believe that Gilgamesh himself set the poem down on stone, so the epic presents itself as his autobiography written in the third person. George compares it to the "Cuthean Legend of Naram-Sîn," which also mentions a tablet-box and a stone tablet (pp. xxxvii–xxxviii).

### I.2 Paean: "Surpassing all other kings" (about I 29–62; pp. 2–3)
- **Where and when:** Still the poet's voice, outside story time. It surveys Gilgamesh's deeds from his whole life, including the late journey to Uta-napishti, and then describes his body.
- **Who:** The poet. Gilgamesh is praised; Lugalbanda, Ninsun, Uta-napishti, the Lady of the Gods and Nudimmud are named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Gilgamesh is greater than all other kings, heroic in build, a raging wild bull (about I 29–30).
  2. Narrator: he leads from the front and can be trusted at the rear; he shields his men like a riverbank and smashes walls like a flood-wave (about I 31–34).
  3. Narrator: his parentage: the wild bull of Lugalbanda, nursed by the Wild Cow Ninsun (I 35–about 36).
  4. Narrator: his deeds: he opened mountain passes, dug wells in the uplands, crossed the ocean to the sunrise, searched the world for life, forced his way to Uta-napishti, restored the cult centers the Flood destroyed, and set up rites for the people (about I 37–44).
  5. Narrator: no one can rival his kingship or claim it as he does (I 45–about 46).
  6. Narrator: he bore the name Gilgamesh from birth and is two-thirds god, one-third human (about I 47–48).
  7. Narrator: the Lady of the Gods designed his figure, and Nudimmud perfected his build (about I 49–50).
  8. Narrator: gives his size and looks (about I 51–62); see Specifics.
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - Epithets: "heroic of stature"; "wild bull on the rampage"; "the vanguard"; "A mighty bank"; "a violent flood-wave"; "Wild bull of Lugalbanda"; "the perfect in strength"; "the august Wild Cow, the goddess Ninsun"; "so tall, magnificent and terrible" (about I 29–37).
  - Deeds: "passes in the mountains"; "wells on the slopes of the uplands"; "the wide sea to the sunrise"; "Uta-napishti the Distant"; "the cult-centres destroyed by the Deluge"; "the rites of the cosmos" (about I 38–44).
  - The king's claim: "It is I am the king" (about I 46).
  - Makers: "the Lady of the Gods"; "divine Nudimmud" (about I 49–50). George's glossary: the Lady of the Gods is Belet-ili, the Mother Goddess, also called Aruru (p. 221); Nudimmud, "Man-fashioner," is a name of Ea (p. 224).
  - Measurements: eleven cubits tall (about I 52; "tall" restored); chest four cubits wide (about I 53; "Four" restored); foot a triple cubit; leg half a rod (about I 54); stride six cubits (I 55=57); the "*curls at* his cheeks" three cubits long (about I 58; italic, so uncertain).
  - Beard dark as lapis lazuli ("[gleaming]" restored) (about I 59); hair as thick as barley ("[as barley]" restored) (I 60).
  - His beauty was "consummate" (about I 61); he was the handsomest of men (about I 62).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "Surpassing all other kings, heroic of stature," (about I 29). This is the opening of the Old Babylonian prologue, embedded here. The Pennsylvania tablet shows it was the Old Babylonian title of the poem (George, pp. xxiii, 163).
  - "two-thirds of him god but one third human." (about I 48).
- **Gaps:** None lost. Restorations in about I 51–53 and 59–60. Italic (uncertain) renderings: "*beauty*" (about I 51), "*curls at*" (about I 58), "*When he grew tall*" (about I 61).
- **Repetitions:** The "wild bull" epithet recurs throughout Tablet I (I 30, 35, about 64, about 81, about 212, about 219). The barley simile for hair (I 60) is used again for Enkidu (about I 107).
- ⚠️ **Line-number note:** The margin marker at the stride line reads "55=57." The numbering jumps by two here; the next marker, I 60, comes three lines later. George does not explain this jump on the page. His general note says the margin count sometimes seems to disagree with the lines shown (p. lv).
- **Book figure (not text):** Fig. 2 (p. 3) is George's line drawing of a cuneiform tablet fragment from Babylon (British Museum, ME Rm 785+…), captioned with about I 64 (List of Figures).

### I.3 Gilgamesh's tyranny in Uruk; the people's complaint (about I 63–78; pp. 3–4)
- **Where and when:** Uruk. The verbs switch to the present tense. The oppression is ongoing and worsens "By day and by night" (about I 69). No starting point is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and his companions; the young men, sons, daughters and brides of Uruk; the people, whose complaint is quoted; the goddess Ishtar, who hears it.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Gilgamesh struts about Uruk like a dominant wild bull, head held high (about I 63–64).
  2. Narrator: no one equals him with weapons, and his *contests* keep his companions constantly on their feet (I 65–about 66).
  3. Narrator: he harasses Uruk's young men without cause and lets no son go to his father (about I 67–68).
  4. Narrator: his oppression grows worse day and night, though he is supposed to guide and shepherd the people (about I 69–71).
  5. Narrator: he lets no daughter go to her mother (about I 72).
  6. Narrator: he is their herdsman and they are his cows; their complaint rises to the far heaven (about I 73–74).
  7. The people (a two-line quoted complaint): the mighty Gilgamesh lets no girl go to her bridegroom (I 75–about 76).
  8. Narrator: Ishtar hears the complaint of the warriors' daughters and the young men's brides (about I 77–78).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "his *contests*" (about I 66; italic, so uncertain); "without warrant" (about I 67).
  - Titles: "[*the guide of the teeming people*!]" (I 70; restored and conjectural); "shepherd of Uruk-the-Sheepfold" (about I 71); "[He is their herdsman,] they are [the] cows" (about I 73).
  - The complainants: "The warrior's daughter, the young man's bride" (about I 77).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "Gilgamesh lets no son go free to his father." (about I 68). This formula recurs.
- **Gaps:** None lost. Restorations in I 70 (the whole epithet, conjectural) and in about I 72–76. Uncertain renderings: "*walks*," "*back and forth*," "*contests*," "*mighty*."
- **Repetitions:** The gods repeat almost all of this passage (about I 64–76) to Anu in I.4. Son/father, daughter/mother and girl/bridegroom form a triple formula.
- **George's note (what the tyranny is):** The poet does not explain the nature of the tyranny. All that is certain is that it displaces family and marital duties. George reviews three suggestions: (1) sexual abuse, linked to the "right of first night" reported in Tablet II; (2) forced labor on the city wall; (3) an exhausting game the young men must play with him, as in the Sumerian poem Gilgamesh and the Netherworld. He thinks the third "probably nearest the mark." He takes the line about Gilgamesh's weapons (I 65) to mean that the Akkadian version's game is more martial (pp. xlviii–xlix).

### I.4 The gods of heaven appeal to Anu (about I 79–93; p. 4)
- **Where and when:** Among the gods (heaven is implied but not named), right after the complaint reaches heaven.
- **Who:** The gods of heaven, who speak; Anu, who is addressed and who listens.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: the gods of heaven call out to Anu (about I 79–80).
  2. Gods → Anu: you have raised a brutal wild bull in Uruk. They list his deeds: no one equals him with weapons, his contests wear out his companions, he harasses the young men, he keeps sons from fathers, and his tyranny grows worse day and night (about I 81–86).
  3. Gods → Anu: yet he is the people's shepherd, guide and herdsman, and he keeps girls from their bridegrooms (about I 87–91).
  4. Narrator: Anu listens to the complaint of the daughters and brides (about I 92–93).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "the lords of *initiative*" (about I 79; italic, so uncertain).
  - "A brutal wild bull" (about I 81). The gods address Anu as the one who "bred" him there.
  - Anu "paid heed" (about I 93), as Ishtar did (about I 78).
- **Gaps:** The speech-introduction is restored and conjectural ("[*called out to the god Anu*]"), followed by a small unrestored gap (I 80). Anu's name in about I 93 is restored, and further words in about I 84, 86 and 88–91.
- **Repetitions:** The gods' speech repeats the narrator's description in I.3 (about I 64–76) almost word for word, in a slightly different order. The closing couplet (about I 92–93) repeats about I 77–78, with Anu in place of Ishtar.

### I.5 Aruru creates Enkidu in the wild (about I 94–112; pp. 4–5)
- **Where and when:** It begins among the gods, right after Anu listens, and moves to the wild, where Aruru throws down the clay. Enkidu's life with the herd is described in the present tense. No time span is given.
- **Who:** "They" summon Aruru. ⚠️ The subject is not named; it follows the gods' appeal and Anu's heeding. Aruru listens and acts without speaking. Enkidu is created; Ninurta strengthens him. The gazelles and the herd are present.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: they summon Aruru (about I 94).
  2. They → Aruru: you created mankind, so now create what Anu has in mind (I 95–about 96).
  3. They → Aruru: make him a match for the storm in Gilgamesh's heart, so the two can compete and Uruk can rest (about I 97–98).
  4. Narrator: Aruru hears and forms Anu's idea within herself (about I 99–100).
  5. Narrator: she washes her hands, pinches off clay and throws it down in the wild (about I 101–102).
  6. Narrator: in the wild she creates Enkidu, whom Ninurta makes strong (about I 103–104).
  7. Narrator: his whole body is covered in hair, which is long like a woman's and thick as barley; he knows no people and no land (I 105–about 108).
  8. Narrator: hairy like the god of the animals, he grazes with the gazelles, joins the herd at the water-hole and enjoys the water with the animals (about I 109–112).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "Aruru, the great one" (about I 94). George's glossary: Aruru is another name of Belet-ili, the Mother Goddess (p. 221).
  - "what Anu has thought of" (about I 96).
  - "a *match* for the *storm* of his heart" (about I 97; both words italic, so uncertain); "so Uruk may be rested" (about I 98).
  - She "washed her hands" (about I 101).
  - "Enkidu, the hero" (about I 103); "offspring of silence, knit strong by Ninurta" (about I 104).
  - "matted with hair" (I 105); "long tresses like those of a woman" (about I 106); "like the god of the animals" (about I 109).
  - "the gazelles," "the herd," "the water-hole" (I 110–about 111).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "took a pinch of clay, threw it down in the wild." (about I 102).
- **Gaps:** "[mankind:]" (I 95) is restored. "*joining the throng*" and "*delighting*" (about I 111–112) are uncertain. Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** The barley simile (about I 107) repeats the one used for Gilgamesh (I 60). About I 110–112 are repeated when Enkidu comes to the water-hole in I.9 (I 175–about 177).
- **George's notes:** The creation follows the myth of humanity's first creation from clay. "Enkidu is thus a replica of the first man, born without a mother's cries of pain" (p. xxxix). The hair and grazing lines (about I 108–110) allude to a tradition that the first humans roamed like beasts, naked but hairy, eating grass (pp. xlii–xliii). A Middle Babylonian school fragment from Nippur with the summoning of Aruru is translated in George's chapter 3 (pp. 164–165).

### I.6 The hunter comes upon Enkidu at the water-hole (about I 113–121; p. 5)
- **Where and when:** The water-hole in the wild, on three days running. The time of day is not stated.
- **Who:** A hunter, who is never named, and Enkidu with his herds. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: a hunter meets Enkidu at the water-hole (about I 113–114).
  2. Narrator: this happens on a first, a second and a third day (I 115–about 116).
  3. Narrator: the hunter's face freezes at the sight; Enkidu goes back to his lair with his herds (about I 117–118).
  4. Narrator: the hunter is troubled, silent and gloomy, with sorrow in his heart and the look of a man who has traveled far (about I 119–121).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "A hunter, a trapper-man" (about I 113).
  - "One day, a second and then a third" (I 115).
  - "his expression froze" (about I 117); Enkidu's "lair" and "his herds" (plural) (about I 118).
  - He looks like "[one come from] afar" (about I 121).
- **Gaps:** Some words are restored in about I 119–121, one of them conjectural ("[*was vexed*,]"). Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** "by the water-hole" ends both about I 114 and about I 116.
- ⚠️ **Line-number note:** The marker I 120 falls on "In his heart there was sorrow," one line later than a straight count from I 115 would put it. The count here is approximate.

### I.7 The hunter tells his father; the father's advice (about I 122–145; pp. 5–6)
- **Where and when:** Not stated. The hunter has evidently gone home from the water-hole. It follows directly on I.6.
- **Who:** The hunter speaks to his father, and the father replies. Gilgamesh and Shamhat are named but not present.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Hunter → father: a man has come to the water-hole, the strongest in the land, with strength like a rock fallen from the sky (about I 122–125).
  2. Hunter → father: the man roams the hills all day and grazes with the herd, and his tracks are always at the water-hole; the hunter is too afraid to go near him (about I 126–129).
  3. Hunter → father: the man fills in the hunter's pits, tears up his snares and frees the animals, so the hunter cannot work (I 130–about 133).
  4. Father → hunter: go to Gilgamesh in Uruk and stand before him, whose strength is also like a rock from the sky (about I 134–137).
  5. Father → hunter: set out for Uruk; human strength alone will not do this (about I 138–139).
  6. Father → hunter: bring Shamhat the harlot, whose allure can overcome even the mighty (I 140–about 141).
  7. Father → hunter (the plan): when the herd comes to drink, she should undress; the man will see her and come to her, and then his herd will reject him (about I 142–145).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "Mightiest in the land" (about I 124).
  - The hunter's work: "pits," "snares," "the beasts of the field," "the work of the wild" (I 130–about 133).
  - "[Shamhat the harlot,]" (I 140; restored). This is the first mention of her name.
- **Short quotations:**
  - "[his strength] is as mighty [as a rock] from the sky." (I 125). This formula recurs.
- **Gaps:** Nothing is wholly lost, but the father's speech (about I 134–145) is almost entirely restored in brackets, and several pieces are conjectural (italic in brackets). Among them: Gilgamesh as "[*the offshoot sprung from*] Uruk's [*midst*]," the warning about relying on "the strength of a man," and Shamhat's "[*allure*]." The plan itself survives intact when Gilgamesh repeats it (about I 163–166), which supports the restoration (⚠️ my inference from George's general note on restoring from parallels, p. lvi). Several phrases in the hunter's speech are also restored and conjectural.
- **Repetitions:** The hunter repeats his speech (about I 123–133) word for word to Gilgamesh in I.8 (I 150–160). Gilgamesh repeats the father's plan (about I 142–145) in I.8 (about I 163–166).
- ⚠️ **Ambiguity:** In about I 136–137, the person whose strength is "as mighty [as a rock from the sky]" is most naturally Gilgamesh, the last person named. That would mean the father applies the hunter's phrase for Enkidu to Gilgamesh. The line is restored, and the text does not make the referent explicit.

### I.8 The hunter goes to Uruk and reports to Gilgamesh (about I 146–166; pp. 6–7)
- **Where and when:** The journey to Uruk, then the king's presence. The length of this outbound journey is not stated (the return journey in I.9 takes until "the third day").
- **Who:** The hunter speaks to Gilgamesh, and Gilgamesh replies. Shamhat is named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: the hunter follows his father's advice and sets out for Uruk (about I 146–148).
  2. Narrator: he speaks before the king (about I 149).
  3. Hunter → Gilgamesh: he repeats his whole report word for word (I 150–160).
  4. Gilgamesh → hunter: go, and take Shamhat the harlot with you (about I 161–162).
  5. Gilgamesh → hunter: he repeats the father's plan word for word (about I 163–166).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "*before* Gilgamesh *the king*" (about I 149; italic, so uncertain); "Shamhat the harlot" (about I 162; not restored here).
- **Gaps:** Some phrases of the narrative and speech-introduction are restored and conjectural. Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** I 150–160 repeat about I 123–133, and about I 163–166 repeat about I 142–145. The instructions pass from father to hunter, from hunter to Gilgamesh, and back to the hunter.

### I.9 Hunter and Shamhat travel to the water-hole and wait (about I 167–179; p. 7)
- **Where and when:** From Uruk to the water-hole. They arrive on the third day and wait two days before the herd comes down to drink.
- **Who:** The hunter and Shamhat; then the herd, the game and Enkidu. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: the hunter sets out with Shamhat (about I 167–168).
  2. Narrator: they reach the place on the third day and sit down to wait (about I 169–170).
  3. Narrator: after two days of waiting, the herd comes to drink (about I 171–172).
  4. Narrator: the animals arrive, and so does Enkidu (about I 173–174).
  5. Narrator: Enkidu grazes with the gazelles and enjoys the water with the herd (I 175–about 177).
  6. Narrator: Shamhat sees him (about I 178–179).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "On the third day" (about I 169); "One day and a second" (about I 171).
  - Enkidu's epithets: "born in the uplands" (about I 174); "the child of nature"; "the savage man from the midst of the wild" (about I 178–179).
- **Gaps:** None. "*to wait*," "*delighting in*," "*joining the throng*" and "*delighting*" are uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** I 175–about 177 repeat about I 110–112.

### I.10 Shamhat seduces Enkidu: six days and seven nights (I 180–about 194; pp. 7–8)
- **Where and when:** At the water-hole, right after the herd arrives. The coupling lasts six days and seven nights.
- **Who:** An unnamed speaker instructs Shamhat. ⚠️ The text has no speech-introduction; by context it is the hunter, the only other person present. Shamhat and Enkidu are present, and Shamhat does not speak.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Speaker (the hunter, by context) → Shamhat: this is the man; uncover yourself so he can see you (I 180–about 181).
  2. Speaker → Shamhat: do not pull back; breathe in his scent; he will see you and come to you (about I 182–183).
  3. Speaker → Shamhat: spread your clothes for him to lie on and give him a woman's lovemaking; once he has had his fill, his herd will reject him (about I 184–187).
  4. Narrator: Shamhat undresses and he looks at her (about I 188–189).
  5. Narrator: she does not pull back; she spreads her clothes and he lies with her (I 190–about 191).
  6. Narrator: she makes love to him, and he embraces her (about I 192–193).
  7. Narrator: Enkidu couples with Shamhat for six days and seven nights (about I 194).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "Uncradle your bosom" (I 180); "take in his scent" (about I 182, I 190); "the cloth of her loins" (about I 188); "the work of a woman" (I 185, about I 192).
  - Duration: "six days and seven nights" (about I 194). The two printed lines "For six days and seven nights / Enkidu was erect …" count as one numbered line, since the marker I 195 falls on the next line.
- **Gaps:** None.
- **Repetitions:** This is an instruction-and-execution pair: the narrative (about I 188–193) repeats the instruction (I 180–about 187) almost word for word, in the past tense. The line that his herd will reject him "though he grew up among it" occurs here for the fourth time (compare I 145 and about I 166).
- ⚠️ **Number variant:** The Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet, which George uses to fill the start of Tablet II, gives "seven days and seven nights" for the second coupling (about P 48, p. 12); see the Tablet II file. The six-day figure here is for the first coupling.

### I.11 The herd flees from Enkidu; he gains reason (I 195–about 204; p. 8)
- **Where and when:** At the water-hole, right after the six days and seven nights, once he is sated.
- **Who:** Enkidu; the gazelles and the beasts of the field; Shamhat. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: sated, Enkidu looks toward his herd (I 195–about 196).
  2. Narrator: the gazelles run off and the other animals shy away from him (about I 197–198).
  3. Narrator: he has made his pure body unclean; his legs stand still while his herd moves away (about I 199–200).
  4. Narrator: he is weaker and cannot run as before, but now he has reason and understanding (about I 201–202).
  5. Narrator: he goes back and sits at Shamhat's feet, studying her face (about I 203–204).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "defiled his body so pure" (about I 199); his legs "stood still" (I 200); "could not run as before" (about I 201); "at the feet of the harlot" (about I 203).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "but now he had reason and wide understanding." (about I 202).
- **Gaps:** None.
- **Repetitions:** None within the scene. An Old Babylonian fragment (Cornell) has a close parallel to this passage and the invitation that follows; George says it is "in much the same language as the standard version, Tablet I 200–214" (chapter 3, pp. 165–166).

### I.12 Shamhat invites Enkidu to Uruk; Enkidu vows to challenge Gilgamesh (I 205–about 223; p. 8)
- **Where and when:** At the water-hole, continuous with I.11, with Enkidu sitting at Shamhat's feet.
- **Who:** Shamhat speaks to Enkidu, and Enkidu replies. This is Enkidu's first speech in the epic.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Enkidu now listens to what she says (I 205–about 206).
  2. Shamhat → Enkidu: you are handsome, like a god; why roam the wild with animals? (about I 207–208).
  3. Shamhat → Enkidu: let me lead you to Uruk and the sacred temple of Anu and Ishtar, where Gilgamesh lords it over the men like a wild bull (about I 209–212).
  4. Narrator: her words please him; by instinct he knows he should look for a friend (about I 213–214).
  5. Enkidu → Shamhat: take me there, to the temple and to Gilgamesh, the wild bull among men (I 215–about 219).
  6. Enkidu → Shamhat: I will challenge him, since I am strong; I will boast in Uruk that I am the mightiest and change the way things are run, because the one born in the wild is strong (I 220–about 223).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "just like a god" (about I 207).
  - "home of Anu and Ishtar" (I 210); in Enkidu's mouth, "holy home of Anu and Ishtar" (about I 217).
  - "perfect in strength" (about I 211).
  - Enkidu's boast: "I am the mightiest!" (about I 221); he will change "the way things are ordered" (about I 222).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "he knew by instinct, he should seek a friend." (about I 214).
- **Gaps:** A few words are restored, several of them conjectural, especially in Enkidu's boast (I 220–about 222). Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's reply (about I 216–219) repeats Shamhat's invitation (I 210–about 212) almost word for word, adding "holy." His closing phrase echoes the hunter's description of him (about I 124).

### I.13 Shamhat describes Uruk and Gilgamesh (about I 224–244; p. 9)
- **Where and when:** At the water-hole, continuous with I.12. Shamhat answers Enkidu's boast.
- **Who:** Shamhat speaks to Enkidu. The speech has no speech-introduction in the poem; George prints a small-type label "Shamhat:" (p. 9), which by his own convention is editorial (p. lvi).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Shamhat → Enkidu: let the people see you; a broken line follows (about I 224–225).
  2. Shamhat → Enkidu: go to Uruk, where the young men wear waistbands; there is a festival every day, with drums (about I 226–229).
  3. Shamhat → Enkidu: there are beautiful, charming harlots there, who can rouse even old men from bed (I 230–about 232).
  4. Shamhat → Enkidu: you know nothing of life yet; I will show you Gilgamesh, a joyful man; look at his face (about I 233–235).
  5. Shamhat → Enkidu: he is handsome and dignified, stronger than you, and never sleeps (about I 236–239).
  6. Shamhat → Enkidu: give up your sinful thoughts; Shamash loves Gilgamesh, and Anu, Enlil and Ea have made him wise (I 240–about 242).
  7. Shamhat → Enkidu: Gilgamesh was already dreaming of you in Uruk before you came from the uplands (about I 243–244). The retold dreams follow (I.14–I.15).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "girt with waistbands" (about I 227); every day "a festival" (about I 228); the drums (about I 229).
  - "harlots, most comely of figure" (I 230).
  - "ignorant of life" (about I 233); Gilgamesh "happy and carefree" (about I 234); "unsleeping" (about I 239).
  - "whom divine Shamash loves" (about I 241); Anu, Enlil and Ea "have broadened his wisdom" (about I 242).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "O Enkidu, cast aside your sinful thoughts!" (I 240).
- **Gaps:** The line marked I 225 begins with lost words, shown as ellipsis dots and not restored. Several words are restored conjecturally, including "[*the people*]" (about I 224) and "[*in Uruk*]" (about I 228).
- **Repetitions:** "by day and by night" (about I 239) echoes the tyranny passage (about I 69).
- ⚠️ The text does not say what the "sinful thoughts" are. The line comes right after Enkidu's plan to challenge Gilgamesh (I 220–about 223).

### I.14 Retold: Gilgamesh's first dream (the rock from the sky) and Ninsun's interpretation (I 245–about 272; pp. 9–10)
- **Where and when:** Two time layers. The frame is Shamhat speaking to Enkidu at the water-hole. Inside it is an earlier scene in Uruk: Gilgamesh dreamed "in the night" and got up to tell his mother, all before Enkidu came from the uplands (about I 243). The whole passage is **retold by Shamhat**; George's double quotation marks show Gilgamesh's and Ninsun's words quoted inside her speech.
- **Who:** Retold by Shamhat. In the embedded scene, Gilgamesh speaks to his mother, and Ninsun answers.
- **Play-by-play (all retold by Shamhat):**
  1. Gilgamesh gets up and tells his mother his dream from the night (I 245–about 246).
  2. **Dream (Gilgamesh → Ninsun):** the stars appeared above him, and one fell in front of him like a rock from the sky (about I 247–248).
  3. **Dream:** he tried to lift it and to roll it, but it was too heavy to move (about I 249–250).
  4. **Dream:** all Uruk gathered and crowded around it (about I 251–254).
  5. **Dream:** the people kissed its feet as if it were an infant; he loved and embraced it as he would a wife (I 255–about 256).
  6. **Dream:** he carried it to his mother and laid it at her feet, and she made it his equal (about I 257–258).
  7. (Within Shamhat's speech) The narration introduces Ninsun as clever, wise and learned (about I 259–260).
  8. **Interpretation (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** she repeats the dream back to him, adding that it was she, Ninsun, who made the thing his equal (about I 261–267).
  9. **Prophecy (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** a mighty comrade will come and save his friend. He will be the strongest in the land, Gilgamesh will love him like a wife, and he will often save Gilgamesh (about I 268–272).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "The stars of the heavens" (about I 247); "like a rock from the sky" (about I 248).
  - "could not dislodge it" (I 250).
  - "[Like a babe-in]-arms" (I 255); "like a wife" (about I 256).
  - Ninsun: "clever and wise," "well versed in everything," "[Wild-Cow] Ninsun" (I 260 and neighboring lines).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "a mighty comrade will come to you, and be his friend's saviour." (about I 268). This formula recurs.
- **Gaps:** Many small restorations in the dream report and the speech-introduction (about I 252–264), one of them conjectural ("[*was milling about*]"). Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** In her interpretation, Ninsun repeats the dream almost word for word, changing "me" to "you." She leaves out the crowd and the kissing of feet (about I 251–255), and she puts the "equal" line in her own voice. The rock-from-the-sky formula repeats the hunter's description of Enkidu (I 125, about 152). The crowd lines (about I 251–255) come back almost word for word in Tablet II, applied to Enkidu in Uruk's street (II 103–about 107).
- ⚠️ **Line-number note:** Each two-line speech-introduction ("The mother of Gilgamesh was clever and wise, / well versed in everything …" and the Wild-Cow Ninsun couplet) counts as a single numbered line. That is the only count that fits the markers I 260 (on the Wild-Cow Ninsun couplet) and I 265. The same is true in I.15 (about I 286–287).

### I.15 Retold: Gilgamesh's second dream (the axe), Ninsun's interpretation, and Gilgamesh's wish (about I 273–298; pp. 10–11)
- **Where and when:** Still retold by Shamhat at the water-hole. In the embedded scene, Gilgamesh has a second dream and goes in to his mother. It comes later than the first dream, but no interval is stated. The dream is set in a street of Uruk.
- **Who:** Retold by Shamhat. In the embedded scene, Gilgamesh speaks to Ninsun, Ninsun answers, and Gilgamesh replies. Enlil is invoked.
- **Play-by-play (all retold by Shamhat):**
  1. After a second dream, Gilgamesh goes in to his mother and says he has dreamed again (about I 273–276).
  2. **Dream (Gilgamesh → Ninsun):** an axe lay in a street of Uruk, and all the people crowded around it (about I 277–282).
  3. **Dream:** he laid it at his mother's feet and loved it like a wife, and she made it his equal (about I 283–285).
  4. (Within Shamhat's speech) The narration introduces Ninsun as before (about I 286–287).
  5. **Interpretation (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** the axe is a friend; you will love him like a wife, and I will make him your equal (about I 288–290).
  6. **Prophecy (Ninsun → Gilgamesh):** she repeats that a mighty comrade will come and save his friend, the strongest in the land (about I 291–293).
  7. Gilgamesh → Ninsun: may this happen, by Enlil's command (about I 294–295).
  8. Gilgamesh → Ninsun: may I find a friend to advise me; I will have one (about I 296–297).
  9. Shamhat (closing her speech): that is how Gilgamesh saw his dreams (about I 298).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "[In a street] of Uruk-the-City" (about I 277); "an axe" (about I 278).
  - "the goddess, his mother" (about I 274).
  - "Counsellor Enlil" (I 295).
  - Ninsun's tense changes: "I shall make him your equal" (I 290), where in the first interpretation it was "I made it your equal" (about I 266).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "My son, the axe you saw is a friend," (about I 288).
  - "Let me find a friend to counsel me," (about I 296).
- **Gaps:** Small restorations in the dream report and in Shamhat's closing line (about I 277–285, 298). Nothing is lost. "*was milling about*" (about I 281) is uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** The dream report repeats the crowd lines and the ending of the first dream (about I 251–258 → 279–285). Ninsun's prophecy repeats her first one (about I 268–270 → 291–293), and her speech-introduction repeats about I 259–260. ⚠️ As in I.14, each two-line speech-introduction counts as one numbered line (about I 286 and 287). That is the only count that fits the marker I 290, which falls on "I shall make him your equal."
- **George's note:** These lines show Gilgamesh's "instinctive longing for trustworthy counsel," which matches the Babylonian ideal of a king guided by counselors (pp. xliii–xliv).

### I.16 Shamhat and Enkidu make love again (about I 299–300; p. 11)
- **Where and when:** Back in the frame, presumably still at the water-hole (the place is not restated), right after Shamhat finishes telling the dreams.
- **Who:** Shamhat and Enkidu. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: once Shamhat has told Enkidu the dreams, the two begin making love (about I 299–300). Tablet I ends here, at the marker I 300.
- **Gaps:** "[After]" and "[began making]" are restored.
- **Repetitions:** Tablet II opens with the Pennsylvania tablet's version of this moment (P 46, p. 12), followed by a second week of lovemaking; see the Tablet II file.

## 3. Elements list (Tablet I)

### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Gilgamesh** (I.1–I.4, I.7–I.8, I.12–I.15). "He who saw the Deep"; "Surpassing all other kings"; "wild bull on the rampage"; "the vanguard"; "A mighty bank"; "a violent flood-wave"; "Wild bull of Lugalbanda"; "the perfect in strength"; "so tall, magnificent and terrible"; "two-thirds of him god"; "[*the guide of the teeming people*]"; "shepherd of Uruk-the-Sheepfold"; "their herdsman"; "Powerful, pre-eminent, expert"; "A brutal wild bull" (the gods' words); "[*the offshoot sprung from*] Uruk's [*midst*]"; "Gilgamesh *the king*"; "happy and carefree"; "fair in manhood"; "unsleeping"; "whom divine Shamash loves."
- **Enkidu** (I.5–I.16). "Enkidu, the hero"; "offspring of silence, knit strong by Ninurta"; "born in the uplands"; "the child of nature"; "the savage man from the midst of the wild"; "Mightiest in the land" (the hunter's words); "just like a god" and "ignorant of life" (Shamhat's words). In the dreams he is foretold as "a mighty comrade" and "his friend's saviour."
- **Shamhat**, "the harlot" (I.7–I.16): "Shamhat the harlot." George's glossary: a cultic prostitute from Uruk, whose name means something between "Good-looking" and "Well-endowed" (p. 224).
- **The hunter**, "a trapper-man" (I.6–I.10). Never named.
- **The hunter's father** (I.7). Never named.
- **Ninsun** (I.2, I.14–I.15): "the august Wild Cow, the goddess Ninsun"; "clever and wise, well versed in everything"; "Wild-Cow Ninsun"; "the goddess, his mother."
- **Lugalbanda** (I.2), only in "Wild bull of Lugalbanda." George's glossary: a mortal king of Uruk, later deified, in one tradition Gilgamesh's father (p. 223).
- **Uta-napishti "the Distant"** (I.2), reached "through sheer force."
- **The people of Uruk** (I.3–I.4, I.14–I.15): the young men, sons, daughters, "The warrior's daughter, the young man's bride," "the teeming people," "the cows" (metaphor); in the dreams, "The land of Uruk," "the menfolk."
- **Gilgamesh's companions** (I.3–I.4), worn out by his "*contests*."

### Places
- **The Deep**, "the country's foundation" (I.1).
- **Uruk**: "Uruk-the-Sheepfold" (I.1, I.3–I.4, I.12–I.13); "Uruk-the-City" (I.15). Its rampart, wall, parapet, ancient stairway, foundations and brickwork (I.1). Its area: city, date-grove, clay-pit, temple of Ishtar (I.1).
- **Eanna**, "the sacred storehouse," "seat of Ishtar the goddess" (I.1); "the sacred temple, home of Anu and Ishtar" (I.12).
- **The seats of power** (I.1).
- **Mountains, uplands, the ocean** ("the wide sea to the sunrise") (I.2).
- **Heaven** (I.3).
- **The wild** (I.5, I.7); **the water-hole** (I.5–I.11); **Enkidu's lair** (I.6); the hills (I.7).
- **A street of Uruk** (I.15, dream).

### Objects and materials
- Tablet of stone (I.1); tablet-box of cedar with bronze clasps and a lid; tablet of lapis lazuli (I.1).
- Bricks fired in an oven (I.1).
- Weapons (I.3–I.4).
- A pinch of clay (I.5).
- The hunter's pits and snares (I.7–I.8).
- Shamhat's clothing; "the cloth of her loins" (I.7–I.10).
- Waistbands; drums (I.13).
- In the dreams: a "rock from the sky" that falls with the stars (I.14); an axe lying in a street (I.15).
- In similes: lapis lazuli (his beard), barley (hair), wool (the wall) (I.1–I.2, I.5).

### Creatures
- Wild bull (simile for Gilgamesh) (I.2–I.4, I.12); Wild Cow (Ninsun's epithet) (I.2, I.14–I.15); cows (simile for the people) (I.3–I.4).
- Gazelles; Enkidu's herd or herds; the beasts of the field; the game; the animals in the water (I.5–I.11).

### Gods and what they do
- **Ishtar**: Eanna is her seat; her temple takes half a square mile (I.1); she hears the complaint (I.3); she shares a temple with Anu (I.12).
- **The gods of heaven, "the lords of *initiative*"**: they appeal to Anu, repeating the complaint (I.4).
- **Anu**: the gods say he "bred" Gilgamesh; he listens (I.4); the plan is his idea (I.5); he has a temple in Uruk (I.12); he made Gilgamesh wise (I.13).
- **Aruru, "the great one"**: creator of mankind; she washes her hands, throws clay down in the wild and creates Enkidu (I.5).
- **The Lady of the Gods**: designed Gilgamesh's figure (I.2). George's glossary equates her with Aruru (p. 221).
- **Nudimmud**: perfected Gilgamesh's build (I.2). He is Ea under another name (glossary, p. 224).
- **Ninurta**: made Enkidu strong (I.5).
- **"The god of the animals"**: Enkidu is as hairy as he is (I.5). He is unnamed in the text.
- **Shamash**: loves Gilgamesh (I.13).
- **Anu, Enlil and Ea**: made Gilgamesh wise (I.13).
- **Enlil, "Counsellor Enlil"**: Gilgamesh asks that the prophecy come true by his command (I.15).
- **The Seven Sages**: laid Uruk's foundations (I.1). Glossary: sent by Ea to civilize mankind (p. 224).

### Numbers and time
- Uruk's area: 1 square mile of city, 1 of date-grove, 1 of clay-pit, ½ for the temple of Ishtar; 3½ in total (I.1).
- Seven Sages (I.1).
- Two-thirds god, one-third human (I.2).
- Body: 11 cubits tall; chest 4 cubits wide; foot a triple cubit; leg half a rod; stride 6 cubits; curls 3 cubits long (I.2).
- "By day and by night" (I.3, I.4, I.13).
- The hunter meets Enkidu on 3 successive days (I.6).
- Uruk to the water-hole: arrival on the third day; 2 days of waiting (I.9).
- Coupling: six days and seven nights (I.10).
- A festival every day in Uruk (I.13).
- Two dreams, at night (I.14–I.15).
- Tablet I ends at the marker I 300 (I.16).

### Dreams and omens
- Dream 1: the stars appear and one falls like a rock from the sky; it is too heavy to lift or roll; Uruk crowds around it and kisses its feet; Gilgamesh loves it like a wife and lays it at his mother's feet. Ninsun reads it as a mighty comrade who will come and often save him (I.14).
- Dream 2: an axe lies in a street of Uruk with a crowd around it. Ninsun reads it as a friend, whom she will make his equal (I.15).
- Gilgamesh's response: may it happen by Enlil's command (I.15).
- Both dreams reach the reader only through Shamhat's retelling to Enkidu (I.13–I.15).

### Customs and rituals
- Writing one's labors on a stone tablet, kept in a cedar tablet-box with bronze clasps, the lapis lazuli tablet to be read aloud (I.1).
- Restoring the cult centers after the Flood; "the rites of the cosmos" (I.2).
- The king's claim, "It is I am the king" (I.2).
- Appeals to the gods by complaint (I.3–I.4).
- Aruru washes her hands before shaping the clay (I.5).
- Trapping with pits and snares (I.6–I.8).
- Using a harlot to lure a wild man away from his herd (I.7–I.10).
- Uruk's daily festival with drums; young men in waistbands; harlots (I.13).
- Telling a dream to one's mother for interpretation (I.14–I.15).

## 4. Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **What the tyranny is** (I.3). The poet does not say. George weighs three readings (sexual abuse, labor on the wall, an exhausting physical game), prefers the game, and notes that the Akkadian version's game seems more martial (pp. xlviii–xlix).
- ⚠️ **Who summons Aruru** (I.5). The text says only "They." The gods of heaven and Anu are the last actors named.
- ⚠️ **Who instructs Shamhat at the water-hole** (I.10). There is no speech-introduction; by context it is the hunter.
- ⚠️ **Whose strength is "as mighty as a rock from the sky" in the father's speech** (I.7, about I 137). Most naturally Gilgamesh's, but the line is restored and the referent is not explicit.
- ⚠️ **The father's speech is mostly restored** (I.7, about I 134–145), with several conjectural pieces. The plan survives in Gilgamesh's repetition (I.8).
- ⚠️ **"The god of the animals"** (I.5, about I 109) is not named. George's glossary has an entry for Shakkan, "The god of gazelles, wild asses and other animals" (p. 224), but the text itself does not name him.
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings (italic in George) that carry meaning**: the wall "like a strand of *wool*" (about I 13); "the lords of *initiative*" (about I 79); "a *match* for the *storm* of his heart" (about I 97); "his *contests*" (about I 66, 83); "the *curls at* his cheeks" (about I 58); "*When he grew tall*" (about I 61).
- ⚠️ **Lost words in Shamhat's speech** (I.13, I 225). They are unrestored.
- ⚠️ **What the "sinful thoughts" are** (I.13, I 240). Not stated.
- ⚠️ **Line numbering**: the margin marker "55=57" (I.2) and the counts around I 120, 260–265 and about 286–290 (I.6, I.14–I.15) show that the numbering does not always match the printed lines. George's general explanation is on p. lv. All "about" numbers in this file are counts from the nearest marker.
- ⚠️ **Number of days**: the first coupling here lasts six days and seven nights (about I 194). The Pennsylvania tablet's account of the second coupling, used for Tablet II, has seven days and seven nights (about P 48, p. 12). George's synopsis calls the first one "a week" (p. 1).
- ⚠️ **When the dreams were told**: Shamhat says Gilgamesh dreamed before Enkidu came from the uplands (about I 243). The Pennsylvania tablet line that opens Tablet II, "As Gilgamesh related the dream," places the telling at the same time as the lovemaking (p. 12). George says only that the overlapping P lines are "slightly different" (p. 11).

## 5. Leads (structure of the text only)
- **Lead:** The tablet is built from **instruction → repetition → execution** chains. The hunter's report is told twice word for word (I.7 → I.8). The lure plan is given by the father, repeated by Gilgamesh and given again at the water-hole (I.7 → I.8 → I.10). The instruction to Shamhat is then carried out in nearly the same words (I.10).
- **Lead:** The **complaint** is told twice, by the narrator (I.3) and by the gods to Anu (I.4). Each telling ends with a deity who "paid heed": first Ishtar, then Anu.
- **Lead:** The **dreams come in a pair**, each told and then interpreted with near-verbatim repetition (I.14, I.15). Between the two interpretations, Ninsun's "I made it your equal" becomes "I shall make him your equal."
- **Lead:** **Formulas shared between characters.** The "mightiest in the land … rock from the sky" pair is used by the hunter (I.7, I.8), probably by the father (I.7) and by Ninsun (I.14, I.15), and the rock from the sky appears literally in Dream 1. The barley simile for hair is used for both Gilgamesh (I 60) and Enkidu (about I 107). "Caress and embrace" is used both in the seduction (I.10) and in the dreams (I.14–I.15).
- **Lead:** **Frame.** The prologue's wall passage (I.1) returns, spoken by Gilgamesh to Ur-shanabi, at the very end of Tablet XI (about XI 323–328, pp. 96–97). George links the prologue's "found peace" (about I 9) to the end of the hero's journey (p. xlvii).
- **Lead:** The crowd scene in the dreams (I.14–I.15: Uruk standing around the object and kissing its feet like an infant's) recurs almost word for word, with Enkidu as its subject, when he enters Uruk (Tablet II, II 103–about 107).
