# Tablet II. The Taming of Enkidu: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How to read this file.** Much of Tablet II is broken, and George fills the gaps with two older (Old Babylonian) tablets that keep their own line numbers. **P** = the Pennsylvania tablet and **Y** = the Yale tablet; both are translated in full in George's chapter 3. **II** = the standard-version Tablet II itself. Each scene says which source it comes from. Line numbers are George's margin numbers, printed every five lines, and every printed marker used here has been checked against the corrected extraction. Every other number is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about"; an "about" in front of a range covers both ends. Counts are least exact in P and Y, where one verse can take up two or three lines of the tablet, so the numbers run ahead of the printed verses (George, p. lv). Pages are the printed pages of the 2020 Penguin edition; Tablet II runs pp. 11–21.

Words in quotation marks are George's wording. In quotations, [square brackets] are George's restorations of broken text, *italics* are George's italics (an insecure decipherment or uncertain rendering; inside brackets, an uncertain or conjectural restoration; George, p. lvi), and "……" marks lost text. An asterisk before a name (\*Shamhat, \*Humbaba) means George changed an older name form in Old Babylonian material to the later form for consistency (p. lvii): P has Shamkatum and Y has Huwawa (chapter 3). George's own spelling (British) is kept inside quotations. Everything else is paraphrase, kept short because the translation is under copyright.

## 1. George's synopsis (paraphrased)
After a second week of sex, the prostitute takes Enkidu to a shepherds' camp. There he learns human ways and becomes the shepherds' watchman. A passing stranger tells him that in Uruk, Gilgamesh exercises *droit de seigneur* at weddings. Shocked, Enkidu goes into Uruk and interrupts the ceremony. Gilgamesh and Enkidu fight until Enkidu accepts that Gilgamesh is supreme, and then the two become firm friends. Seeking fame and glory, Gilgamesh proposes an expedition to the Forest of Cedar and ignores Enkidu's warning about its dangers. The two equip themselves with weapons. Gilgamesh announces his plan to the assembly of Uruk, and the elders try to talk him out of it. (George, p. 11. The synopsis is George's modern addition, p. lvi.)

## 2. Scenes

### II.1 The second week; Shamhat invites Enkidu into human society and clothes him (II 1, then about P 44–71; pp. 11–12)
- **Source:** Only the first line is from the standard version (II 1). After it comes a lacuna, which George fills with P, warning that "the overlapping lines are slightly different" (p. 11). The standard-version lines about II 2–35 are lost.
- **Where and when:** In the wild, continuing from the end of Tablet I. The lovemaking lasts seven days and seven nights. P ties the moment to Gilgamesh's telling of his dream (about P 44).
- **Who:** Enkidu and Shamhat ("the harlot," "\*Shamhat"). Shamhat speaks to Enkidu, who answers only by agreeing.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator (SB): Enkidu is sitting before her; the line breaks off (II 1).
  2. Narrator (P): while Gilgamesh was telling his dream, Enkidu sat before the harlot (about P 44–45).
  3. Narrator (P): they make love, and Enkidu forgets the wild where he was born (P 46–about 47).
  4. Narrator (P): they couple for seven days and seven nights (about P 48–50).
  5. Shamhat → Enkidu (P): you look like a god to me; why live with the animals? (about P 51–55).
  6. Shamhat → Enkidu (P): come, I will lead you to Uruk, to Eanna, Anu's temple (about P 56–60).
  7. Shamhat → Enkidu (P): there, men practice skilled crafts, and you too will find your place among them; you are cut off from the lands that have no shepherd (about P 61–65).
  8. Narrator (P): her advice wins him over (about P 66–67).
  9. Narrator (P): she takes off her garment, dresses him in part of it and wears the other part herself (P 70–about 71).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "seven days and seven nights" (about P 48).
  - "Uruk-the-City"; "the dwelling of Anu"; "the temple Eanna" (about P 56–60). Only Anu is named here; Tablet I's invitation said "home of Anu and Ishtar" (I 210).
  - "labours of skill" (about P 61); "*like a man*" and "*find a place for*" (about P 62; both italic, so uncertain).
  - "the regions that have no shepherd" (P 65).
  - "part of her garment" (P 70).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "a woman's counsel struck home in his heart." (about P 67).
- **Gaps:** The standard-version text about II 2–35 is lost (George's linking note, p. 11). In II 1, "[Enkidu]" is restored and the end of the line is lost. In P, "[men]" (about P 61) is restored. The skips in P numbering (for example, the line after about P 62 is numbered P 65) are not gaps: chapter 3 prints the same lines (pp. 168–169), and George explains such skips by verses that take up more than one tablet line (p. lv).
- **Repetitions:** Shamhat's opening (about P 53–56) parallels Tablet I's invitation (about I 207–210): Enkidu is godlike, so why live among animals? Come to Uruk.

### II.2 Shamhat leads Enkidu to the shepherds' camp (II 36–about 43; p. 12)
- **Source:** Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II resumes," p. 12).
- **Where and when:** From the wild to the shepherds' camp, right after the clothing in II.1. No duration is given.
- **Who:** Shamhat and Enkidu; the shepherds, who talk among themselves.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: she leads him by the hand, like a god, to the shepherds' camp by the sheep-pen (II 36–about 37).
  2. Narrator: the shepherds crowd around him and discuss him (about II 38–39).
  3. Shepherds (to one another): he is built just like Gilgamesh, tall and towering (II 40–about 41).
  4. Shepherds: this must be Enkidu, born in the uplands, with strength like a rock from the sky (about II 42–43).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "like a god [she led him,]" (II 36).
  - "the shepherds' camp, the site of the sheep-pen" (about II 37).
  - "The band of shepherds" (about II 38).
  - "tall of stature, *proud* as a battlement" (about II 41; *proud* is italic, so uncertain).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "It must be Enkidu, born in the uplands," (about II 42). The shepherds name him, though the text does not say how they know the name.
- **Gaps:** "[she led him,]" (II 36) is restored. Nothing is lost.
- **Repetitions:** The rock-from-the-sky formula (about II 43) is the one from Tablet I (I 125, about 152, I 270, about 293). The shepherds' "tall of stature" differs from the Uruk crowd's "shorter of stature" in the P passage used in II.6 (P 185).
- **Book figure (not text):** Fig. 3 (p. 12) is George's line drawing of a cuneiform fragment from Uruk (Oriental Institute, Chicago, A 3444), captioned with about II 42 (List of Figures).

### II.3 Bread and ale; Enkidu is groomed and "turned into a man" (about II 44–46, then P 90–about 111; pp. 13–14)
- **Source:** Standard version for about II 44–46. Then George: "Here Tablet II becomes fragmentary again, and the episode is best taken from" P (p. 13). The standard-version lines about II 47–58 are not given.
- **Where and when:** The shepherds' camp, continuous with II.2. No duration is given.
- **Who:** The shepherds, who set food before him; Shamhat, who speaks; Enkidu; the barber.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator (SB): they put bread and ale in front of him (about II 44–45).
  2. Narrator (SB): Enkidu does not eat the bread; he eyes it *askance* (about II 46).
  3. Narrator (P): he does not know how to eat bread or drink ale, never having been taught (P 90–about 91).
  4. Shamhat → Enkidu (P): eat the bread and drink the ale (about P 92–97).
  5. Narrator (P): he eats until he is full and drinks seven jugs of ale (P 100–about 101).
  6. Narrator (P): he relaxes, sings, and grows cheerful and bright-faced (about P 102–105).
  7. Narrator (P): the barber grooms his hairy body; he is anointed with oil and becomes a man (about P 106–107).
  8. Narrator (P): he puts on a garment, looks like a warrior, and takes up a weapon to fight lions (P 110–about 111).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "looked *askance*" (about II 46; italic, so uncertain).
  - Bread is "essential to life"; ale is "the lot of the land" (about P 96–97).
  - "a full seven jugfuls" (about P 101).
  - "The barber" (about P 106).
  - "became like a warrior" (P 110); "to do battle with lions" (about P 111).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "anointed with oil he turned into a man." (about P 107).
- **Gaps:** The standard-version text about II 47–58 is not given (fragmentary); P fills the gap. The P passage itself is complete, and the skips in P numbering are not gaps (see II.1).
- **Repetitions:** None within the scene. ⚠️ The SB version (he eyes the bread *askance*) and the P version (he does not know how to eat) stand side by side. In chapter 3, the P tablet itself first has him peering and staring at the bread (just before P 90, p. 169).

### II.4 Enkidu guards the shepherds at night (about II 59–62; p. 14)
- **Source:** Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II resumes again," p. 14).
- **Where and when:** The shepherds' camp, at night. It is a habitual activity, not a single night. No duration is given.
- **Who:** Enkidu; the sleeping shepherds and senior shepherds; wolves and lions. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: while the shepherds sleep at night, Enkidu kills wolves and drives off lions (about II 59–60; the night line is wholly restored).
  2. Narrator: the senior shepherds sleep while Enkidu, their shepherd boy, stays awake (about II 61–62).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "wolves," "lions" (II 60); "the senior shepherds" (about II 61); "their shepherd boy Enkidu" (about II 62).
- **Gaps:** About II 59 is restored in full. Parts of II 60 and about II 62 are restored.
- **Repetitions:** None. George's synopsis calls this role "the shepherds' watchman" (p. 11). Where the SB has "their shepherd boy," the P tablet in chapter 3 calls him their "watchman" (p. 170).

### II.5 The wedding guest: Enkidu learns of the king's right to the bride (about II 63–64, then P 135–165; pp. 14–15)
- **Source:** Standard version for about II 63–64. Then "another lacuna intervenes in Tablet II," filled again from P (George, p. 14). The standard-version lines about II 65–102 are not given.
- **Where and when:** Presumably the shepherds' camp (⚠️ the place is not restated). A traveler is passing on his way to a wedding banquet in Uruk. The time of day is not given.
- **Who:** A traveling "fellow," invited to a wedding; Enkidu, who speaks to Shamhat; Shamhat, who calls the fellow over; the fellow, who answers Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator (SB): a man invited to a wedding is on his way to Uruk for the feast (about II 63–64).
  2. Narrator (P): Enkidu, who is taking his pleasure with Shamhat, looks up and sees the man (P 135–about 136).
  3. Enkidu → Shamhat (P): bring him here; I want to know why he has come (about P 137–141).
  4. Narrator (P): Shamhat calls to the man and goes up to him (about P 142–143).
  5. Shamhat → fellow (P): where are you hurrying, and why is your journey so hard? (P 145–about 146).
  6. Fellow → Enkidu (P): he is invited to a wedding, since marriage is the people's lot, and he will heap the ceremonial table with fine food (about P 147–152).
  7. Fellow → Enkidu (P): for Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, the bride's *veil* will be parted for him as the one who chooses first. He says this twice (about P 153–157).
  8. Fellow → Enkidu (P): Gilgamesh sleeps with the bride before the bridegroom does; the gods decreed it, and she was destined for him from his birth (about P 158–162).
  9. Narrator (P): hearing this, Enkidu goes pale with anger (P 165).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "the ceremonial table" (about P 151).
  - "the king of Uruk-the-City" (about P 153).
  - "the *veil* will be parted" (P 155; *veil* is italic, so uncertain); "the one who picks first" (P 155).
  - "By divine consent" (about P 161).
  - "his face paled in anger" (P 165).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "he first of all, the bridegroom after." (P 160).
  - "when his navel-cord was cut, for him she was destined." (about P 162).
- **Gaps:** About II 63–64 are partly restored, with conjectural pieces. The standard-version text about II 65–102 is not given (lacuna). After P 165, "* * *" marks a lacuna of more than one line (George's convention, p. lvii): about P 166–174 are lost. In chapter 3, George notes that the rest of Enkidu's reaction "is lost in a lacuna" (p. 171).
- **Repetitions:** The "veil" couplet is said twice, the second time with Gilgamesh's name added (about P 153–157).
- **George's notes:** George's synopsis calls this *droit de seigneur* (p. 11). In the introduction he calls it "the right of first night" (*ius primae noctis*) and says that such things did not happen in Babylonia in the historical period. Because the text says the practice was divinely sanctioned, he argues it "could not have been an abuse in the context" (pp. xlviii–xlix).

### II.6 Enkidu enters Uruk and blocks Gilgamesh's path (P 175–about 201; p. 15)
- **Source:** P only, continuing after the lacuna (about P 166–174).
- **Where and when:** The way into Uruk, then a street of Uruk. It happens during the wedding, at night ("Gilgamesh met with the maiden by night," about P 197). The journey's length is not stated.
- **Who:** Enkidu, walking in front, with Shamhat behind; a crowd of Uruk's people, who talk among themselves; the young men; Gilgamesh; the maiden.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Enkidu leads the way and Shamhat follows (P 175).
  2. Narrator: he walks into Uruk, and a crowd forms around him (about P 176–177).
  3. Narrator: he stops in the street while the people gather and talk about him (P 180–about 181).
  4. People (among themselves): he has Gilgamesh's build but is shorter and heavier-boned; he must be the one born in the uplands, raised on animals' milk (about P 182–187).
  5. Narrator: Uruk held regular festivals of sacrifice; the young men celebrated and set up a *champion* as a rival for the godlike Gilgamesh (P 190–195).
  6. Narrator: the bed was prepared for the goddess of weddings, and Gilgamesh met the maiden at night (about P 196–197).
  7. Narrator: Enkidu steps out into the street and blocks Gilgamesh's way (P 200–about 201).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "shorter of stature, and bigger of bone" (P 185).
  - "animals' milk" (about P 187).
  - "regular festivals of sacrifice" (P 190).
  - "a *champion*" (about P 191; italic, so uncertain); "a rival" (P 195).
  - "the goddess of weddings" (about P 196).
  - "Forward came (Enkidu)" (P 200). The name is in round brackets: George supplies it for clarity. ⚠️ His list of conventions does not include round brackets (pp. lvi–lvii).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "Gilgamesh met with the maiden by night." (about P 197).
- **Gaps:** "[first,]" (P 175) and part of about P 186 are restored. The skips in P numbering are not gaps (see II.1).
- **Repetitions:** The people's talk echoes the shepherds' talk in II.2 (the likeness to Gilgamesh; born in the uplands), but says "shorter" where the shepherds said "tall." The SB version of the rival-and-bed couplet follows in II.7 (about II 109–110), with "a substitute" in place of "a rival."
- **George's note (chapter 3 synopsis of P):** Enkidu "arrives at the very moment when, as it seems, the young men choose one of their number to challenge the king. Presumably we are to understand that Enkidu then fills this role" (p. 166). George hedges this with "as it seems" and "Presumably."

### II.7 The crowd around Enkidu; he bars the wedding-house door; Gilgamesh and Enkidu fight (II 103–about 117; p. 16)
- **Source:** Standard version (George: "The text of Tablet II becomes legible once more," p. 15).
- **Where and when:** Uruk: the street, the door of the wedding house and "the Square of the Land." At night, during the wedding (from II.6). The fight's length is not stated.
- **Who:** The people of Uruk around Enkidu; Enkidu; Gilgamesh. No one speaks.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: all Uruk stands and crowds around Enkidu (II 103–about 106).
  2. Narrator: they kiss his feet as if he were an infant; a broken half-line follows (about II 107–108).
  3. Narrator: the bed is ready for the goddess of weddings, and a substitute has been set up for the godlike Gilgamesh (about II 109–110).
  4. Narrator: Enkidu plants his foot in the doorway of the wedding house and will not let Gilgamesh in (about II 111–112).
  5. Narrator: they grapple at the door and fight on into the street and the Square of the Land (about II 113–114).
  6. Narrator: the doorposts shake and the wall trembles (II 115).
  7. Narrator (restored repeat): the fight in the street and square, and the shaking doorposts and wall, are told again (about II 116–117).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "Like a babe-in-arms" (about II 107).
  - "a substitute" (II 110).
  - "the door of the wedding house" (about II 111).
  - "the Square of the Land" (about II 114).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "The door-jambs shook, the wall did shudder," (II 115). This line is repeated.
- **Gaps:** About II 108 breaks off unrestored. Small restorations in II 103–about 107. About II 116–117 are restored in full as a repeat of about II 114–115. After this, the standard-version text about II 118–161 is not preserved; P fills only the end of the fight (see II.8).
- **Repetitions:** II 103–about 107 repeat, with "him" for "it," the crowd lines of Gilgamesh's first dream in Tablet I (about I 251–255), including the kissing of feet. About II 109–110 is the SB form of the P couplet in II.6 (P 195–about 196), with "substitute" for "rival." The combat couplet is told twice (about II 114–115, then, restored, about II 116–117).
- **Book note:** P's own fuller version of this fight (about P 204–225, chapter 3, p. 172) describes the two as bent-backed like bulls, smashing the doorpost. George does not use it here because the SB text is legible.

### II.8 The fight ends; Enkidu acclaims Gilgamesh's kingship (about P 228–240; p. 16)
- **Source:** George: "Another lacuna intervenes, again partly to be filled by" P (p. 16). These are P's last lines: "At this point the Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet ends" (p. 16).
- **Where and when:** The end of the fight, in Uruk's street and square (II.7), right after Gilgamesh breaks off.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, who acts; Enkidu, who speaks to Gilgamesh. Ninsun and Enlil are named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Gilgamesh goes down on one knee, with one foot on the ground (about P 228).
  2. Narrator: his anger cools, and he stops fighting (P 230).
  3. Narrator: after he stops, Enkidu speaks to him (about P 231–232).
  4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: your mother, the goddess Ninsun, bore you as one of a kind (P 235–about 236).
  5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: you are raised above all warriors, and Enlil destined you to be king of the people (about P 237–240).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "one foot on the ground" (about P 228).
  - "As one unique" (P 235); "the wild cow of the fold, the goddess Ninsun" (about P 236).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "to be king of the people Enlil made it your destiny!" (P 240).
- **Gaps:** None within these P lines. The SB text breaks off after about II 117, and George picks up P at the point where Gilgamesh kneels. He does not repeat P's own version of the fight, which the SB covers. After these lines comes another lacuna (p. 16): the rest of the standard-version text about II 118–161 is lost, and P has ended.
- **Repetitions:** The breaking-off line is said twice (P 230, then about P 231).
- ⚠️ **Who won:** The text says only that Gilgamesh knelt with one foot on the ground, that his anger cooled and that he stopped. Enkidu then praises his unique birth and god-given kingship. George's synopsis reads this as the fight going on until Enkidu "accepts Gilgamesh's supremacy" (p. 11). His chapter 3 synopsis of P says the tablet "ends with his acceptance that Gilgamesh's kingship is legitimate" (p. 166).

### II.9 Gilgamesh presents Enkidu to Ninsun; Ninsun speaks of Enkidu's lack of kin (II 162–about 177; pp. 16–17)
- **Source:** Standard version. George: "After another lacuna Tablet II resumes with an episode in which Gilgamesh introduces Enkidu to his mother" (p. 16).
- **Where and when:** Not stated. ⚠️ Presumably wherever Ninsun is. No interval after the fight is given.
- **Who:** A speaker who praises Enkidu (⚠️ the name is lost; by George's note, Gilgamesh); Ninsun, who speaks to Gilgamesh; Enkidu, present and listening (see II.10).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Speaker (Gilgamesh, per George's note) → Ninsun: the end of a speech about Enkidu: the strongest in the land, strong as a rock from the sky, tall and towering (II 162–about 164).
  2. Narrator: Ninsun begins to speak to her son (II 165–about 168).
  3. (Lacuna: about five lines lost.)
  4. Ninsun → Gilgamesh (fragment): something done "Bitterly"; Enkidu has no *kith or kin*; with long shaggy hair, born in the wild, he has no *brother* (about II 174–177).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "*proud* as a battlement" (about II 164; restored, and *proud* is uncertain).
  - "no [*kith or kin.*]" (II 175).
  - "Shaggy hair hanging loose" (about II 176).
  - "no [*brother.*]" (about II 177).
- **Gaps:** A lacuna precedes II 162 (see II.8). II 162–about 168 are heavily restored. After about II 168, "* * *" marks a lacuna of more than one line (p. lvii): about II 169–173 are lost (the next marker is II 175). About II 174 and 176 break off mid-line, unrestored. The key words "[*kith or kin*]," "[*has*]" and "[*brother*]" are restored and conjectural.
- **Repetitions:** II 162–about 164 repeat the Enkidu formula from Tablet I and the shepherds' words in II.2. The speech-introduction is doubled ("The mother of Gilgamesh …" / "Wild-Cow Ninsun …"), as in Tablet I (about I 259–260, 286–287).

### II.10 Enkidu weeps; the two take hands; Gilgamesh asks why (about II 178–191; p. 17)
- **Source:** Standard version.
- **Where and when:** The same place as II.9, right after Ninsun's words.
- **Who:** Enkidu and Gilgamesh, who speak to each other. Ninsun is presumably still present (⚠️ not stated).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Enkidu, standing there, hears her words, thinks them over and sits down *weeping* (about II 178–179).
  2. Narrator: his eyes fill with tears; his arms go limp; his strength *drains away* (II 180–about 181).
  3. Narrator: they take hold of each other and *join* hands; both lines are broken (about II 182–183).
  4. Narrator: Gilgamesh (broken) speaks to Enkidu (about II 184–185).
  5. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: why are you in tears, limp and drained? (about II 186–187).
  6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: my friend, my heart is grieved (about II 188–189; broken at the end).
  7. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: sobbing makes his *legs* shake, and terror has entered his heart (II 190–about 191).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "he sat [down *weeping.*]" (about II 179); "[*linked*] their hands" (about II 183); Gilgamesh now calls him "my friend" (about II 186), and Enkidu answers in kind (about II 189).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "terror has entered my heart." (about II 191).
- **Gaps:** About II 182–184 are broken and unrestored. "[down *weeping.*]," "[*ebbed away.*]" and "[*linked*]" are conjectural. In II 190, "[my *legs* do]" is restored, with *legs* uncertain. About II 189 ends in "…". The standard-version text about II 192–215 is lost; it is filled from Y in II.11.
- **Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's question (about II 186–187) repeats the narrator's description (II 180–about 181) word for word, turned into the second person.

### II.11 Gilgamesh proposes killing Humbaba in the Forest of Cedar; Enkidu warns him (about Y 89–119, then II 216–about 229; pp. 17–18)
- **Source:** Y, which "fills another gap in the standard version" (George, p. 17), for about Y 89–119. Then "The text of Tablet II resumes" (p. 18) at II 216.
- **Where and when:** Not stated. It continues the conversation from II.10, with Gilgamesh answering Enkidu's distress. No time is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Enkidu, who speak to each other. Humbaba, Enlil, Adad and the Igigi are named.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator (Y): Gilgamesh speaks to Enkidu (about Y 89–90).
  2. (Lacuna: about Y 91–96 lost.)
  3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (Y): let us kill ferocious Humbaba and end his power (Y 97–about 98).
  4. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (Y): let us take him by surprise in his lair in the Forest of Cedar (Y 100–about 101).
  5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (Y): I knew Humbaba in the uplands when I roamed with the herd (about Y 106–107; the speech-introduction is about Y 102–105).
  6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (Y): the forest is a wilderness sixty leagues across; who would go in? (about Y 108–109).
  7. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (Y): Humbaba's voice is the Flood, his speech fire, his breath death; why do you want this? His ambush is a battle no one can win (Y 110–115).
  8. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (Y): he will climb the forest's slopes (about Y 116–119).
  9. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (SB): how can we go there? Enlil set the guardian to frighten men away and keep the cedars safe; it is a journey not to be made and a creature not to be looked at (II 216–about 219). ⚠️ The guardian is not named until two lines later. George's glossary says Enlil appointed Humbaba to protect the timber (p. 222).
  10. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (SB): the guardian's *reach* is wide, and Humbaba's voice is the Flood (II 220–about 221).
  11. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (SB): his speech is fire and his breath death; he can hear the forest stir sixty leagues away; who would go in? (about II 222–224).
  12. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (SB): Humbaba is second only to Adad; who among the Igigi gods could stand against him? (II 225–about 226).
  13. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (SB): Enlil gave him the task of terrifying men; whoever goes into his forest is gripped by trembling (about II 227–229).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "ferocious \*Humbaba" (Y 97); "his lair" (about Y 101).
  - "For sixty leagues the forest is a wilderness" (about Y 108). The Y text gives sixty leagues as the forest's extent, while the SB gives it as Humbaba's hearing range ("at sixty leagues' distance," about II 223).
  - "his *reach* is wide" (II 220; restored, and *reach* is uncertain).
  - "the Igigi" (about II 226). George's glossary: one of the two divisions of the pantheon, later the great gods of heaven (p. 222).
  - "seized by the tremors" (about II 229).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "\*Humbaba, his voice is the Deluge, / his speech is fire, and his breath is death!" (Y 110–about 111). This formula recurs.
  - "Adad ranks first, and Humbaba second." (II 225). This formula recurs.
- **Gaps:** About Y 91–96 are lost ("* * *"). About Y 97–101 are partly lost or restored, and "[*the forest's*]" (Y 119) is conjectural. The first SB lines (II 216 to about 221) are partly restored. Between Y 119 and II 216, George's linking note says only "The text of Tablet II resumes" (p. 18). ⚠️ Between the markers II 216 and II 220 there are six printed lines for four numbers, so the counts for beat 9 are uncertain.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu gives the Humbaba warning twice in this scene (Y 110–about 111, then about II 221–222). Within the SB speech, the couplet about Enlil keeping the cedars safe is said twice (about II 218–219, 227–228). Enkidu repeats the whole SB warning to the elders (II.15, about II 274–286), and the senior advisers repeat part of it (II.15, about II 291–299).

### II.12 Gilgamesh rebukes Enkidu's fear: "let us hie to the forge" (II 230–about 241; p. 19)
- **Source:** Standard version.
- **Where and when:** Continuous with II.11.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh speaks to Enkidu.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: why are you talking like a coward? Your weak words trouble me (II 230–about 233).
  2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: a man's days are limited, and all he does is wind; a broken line follows (about II 234–236).
  3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: you grew up in the wild; lions feared you and grown men ran from you; your heart has been tested in battle (about II 237–240).
  4. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: come, let us go to the forge (about II 241).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "like a coward" (about II 232); "even lions were afraid of you" (about II 238); "tested in" combat (II 240, restored); "the forge" (about II 241).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "As for man, [his days] are numbered, / whatever he may do, it is but wind," (about II 234–235).
- **Gaps:** About II 236 is broken at both ends and unrestored. Small restorations throughout. After about II 241, "A short lacuna follows" (p. 19), filled from Y (II.13).
- **Repetitions:** None within the scene. "Born … in the wild" echoes Ninsun's words (about II 177) and the Tablet I epithets.

### II.13 At the forge: the weapons are cast (Y 162–about 171; p. 19)
- **Source:** "A short lacuna follows. It can be filled from" Y (George, p. 19). The standard-version lines about II 242–257 are not given.
- **Where and when:** The forge, where the smiths sit in consultation. ⚠️ Presumably in Uruk, though this is not stated; the next lines are in Uruk's street. It follows Gilgamesh's call to go to the forge. No duration is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh and Enkidu; the smiths. An unnamed speaker (⚠️ by context, Gilgamesh continuing) asks for the hatchets to be cast.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Speaker (Gilgamesh, by context): let them cast hatchets for us while we watch (Y 162).
  2. Narrator: hand in hand they hurry to the forge, where the smiths are conferring (about Y 163–164).
  3. Narrator: they cast great hatchets, and axes of three talents each (Y 165–about 166).
  4. Narrator: they cast great daggers, with two-talent blades, half-talent handle crests and half-talent gold mountings (about Y 167–170).
  5. Narrator: each of the two carries ten talents (about Y 171).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "the smiths were sitting in consultation" (about Y 164).
  - "Great hatchets"; "axes weighing three talents apiece" (Y 165–about 166).
  - "Great daggers"; blades of "two talents apiece"; "one half of a talent the crests of their handles"; "the daggers' gold mountings," "half a talent apiece" (about Y 167–170).
  - Material named: gold (the dagger mountings). No other metal is named.
- **Short quotations:**
  - "Gilgamesh and Enkidu bore ten talents each." (about Y 171).
- **Gaps:** Parts of Y 162 are restored. The rest is complete.
- **Repetitions:** None. Taking each other by the hand echoes the joined hands of II.10 (about II 183).
- ⚠️ **Weights:** The text gives no separate weight for the hatchets and does not show how the items add up to ten talents per man.

### II.14 The assembly of Uruk: Gilgamesh announces the expedition to the elders and the young men (about Y 172–187, then II 258–about 271; pp. 19–20)
- **Source:** Y continues for about Y 172–187. Then "The text of Tablet II resumes" (p. 20) at II 258.
- **Where and when:** Uruk, with its seven gates bolted; the street, where Gilgamesh sits on his throne before the seated crowd. It follows the forging. No time of day is given.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, who speaks; the elders of Uruk and the young men of Uruk, who are addressed; the crowd. Enkidu is present (he speaks in II.15).
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator (Y): he (⚠️ unnamed; by context Gilgamesh) bolts Uruk's seven gates and calls the assembly, and the crowd gathers (about Y 172–173).
  2. Narrator (Y): in the street of Uruk, Gilgamesh *takes his seat* on the throne, with the crowd sitting before him (about Y 174–177).
  3. Narrator (Y): Gilgamesh addresses the elders (about Y 178–179).
  4. Gilgamesh → elders (Y): he wants to go to ferocious Humbaba, the god everyone talks about, whose name every land repeats (Y 180–about 183).
  5. Gilgamesh → elders (Y): he will defeat him in the Forest of Cedar, show the land that Uruk's son is mighty, fell the cedar and win lasting fame (about Y 184–187).
  6. Narrator (SB): Gilgamesh addresses the young men of Uruk (II 258–about 259; wholly restored).
  7. Gilgamesh → young men (SB): listen, you who know *combat*; I will take the long road to Humbaba's home and fight a battle I do not know, on a road I do not know (II 260–about 264).
  8. Gilgamesh → young men (SB): bless my journey, so I can see you again safely and come home happy through Uruk's gate (II 265–about 267).
  9. Gilgamesh → young men (SB): when I return I will hold the New Year festival twice in one year; there will be celebrations, and drums will sound before Ninsun (about II 268–271).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "the sevenfold gates of Uruk" (about Y 172).
  - "his throne" (Y 175; "[*seated himself on*]" is conjectural).
  - "the god of whom men talk" (about Y 182). Gilgamesh calls Humbaba a god here.
  - "Uruk's offshoot" (Y 185).
  - "I will cut down the cedar" (about Y 186).
  - "who understand [*combat*!]" (about II 261).
  - "Uruk's gate" (about II 267).
  - "New Year [twice over,]"; "twice in the year" (about II 268–269).
  - "[Wild-Cow] Ninsun" (about II 271).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "I will establish for ever a name eternal!" (about Y 187).
- **Gaps:** About Y 172–181 are heavily restored, and several pieces are conjectural, including the assembly, the throne, the elders as audience and Humbaba's name in Gilgamesh's first sentence. About Y 174 begins with an unrestored gap. The standard-version lines about II 242–257 are not given. II 258–about 271 are partly restored, and the speech-introduction (II 258–about 259) is restored in full.
- **Repetitions:** The two speeches cover the same ground for two audiences, the elders (Y) and the young men (SB), and each opens with "Hear me, O …".
- **George's note:** Among the epic's passing explanations, George lists "how there came to be two New Year's Days in the Babylonian calendar" (p. xvi). ⚠️ He does not cite the line there; about II 268–269 is the passage in Tablets I–II that fits.

### II.15 Enkidu and the senior advisers counsel against the expedition (about II 272–299; pp. 20–21)
- **Source:** Standard version.
- **Where and when:** The assembly in Uruk, continuous with II.14.
- **Who:** Enkidu, who speaks to the elders and the young men; the senior advisers, who rise and speak to Gilgamesh.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Enkidu gives his counsel to the elders and the young men (about II 272–273).
  2. Enkidu → elders and young men: tell him not to go to the Forest of Cedar; it is a journey not to be made and a creature not to be looked at; the guardian's *reach* is wide (about II 274–277).
  3. Enkidu → elders and young men: he repeats Humbaba's voice of the Flood, fiery speech, deadly breath and sixty-league hearing; who would go in? (about II 278–281).
  4. Enkidu → elders and young men: Humbaba is second only to Adad, no Igigi god could oppose him, Enlil set him to terrify men, and whoever enters trembles (about II 282–286).
  5. Narrator: the senior advisers rise and give Gilgamesh sound advice (about II 287–288).
  6. Senior advisers → Gilgamesh: you are young and carried away by your feelings; you do not understand what you are talking about (about II 289–290).
  7. Senior advisers → Gilgamesh: they repeat the Humbaba warning (voice, speech, breath, hearing; second to Adad; Enlil's task for him) (about II 291–299).
- **Specifics, exactly as given:**
  - "Tell him not to go" (about II 274).
  - "This *Humbaba*" (about II 278; the name is italic here, so the reading is uncertain).
  - "The senior advisers" (about II 287).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "You are young, Gilgamesh, borne along by emotion," (about II 289).
- **Gaps:** Enkidu's speech is partly restored, especially about II 278–284. The advisers' speech is complete.
- **Repetitions:** Enkidu's counsel (about II 275–286) repeats his warning to Gilgamesh in II.11 (II 220–about 229) almost word for word. The advisers repeat the same warning a third time (about II 291–299), leaving out the "journey not to be made" lines and the trembling line. Across Tablet II, the Humbaba formula (voice of the Flood, speech of fire, breath of death) is spoken four times (Y 110–about 111; about II 221–222; about II 278–279; about II 291–292).

### II.16 Gilgamesh laughs at the warnings; the end of the tablet is lost (II 300–about 303; p. 21)
- **Source:** Standard version. The last surviving lines are restored. ⚠️ My inference: they are restored from the parallel in Y, which has the same four lines at about Y 201–204 (chapter 3, p. 177).
- **Where and when:** The assembly, continuous with II.15.
- **Who:** Gilgamesh, who speaks to Enkidu; the senior advisers, whose words he has heard.
- **Play-by-play:**
  1. Narrator: Gilgamesh hears the senior advisers and turns to Enkidu with a laugh (II 300–about 301).
  2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (restored): am I supposed to be afraid now, and change my mind out of fear of Humbaba? (about II 302–303).
  3. (Lost: the rest of Tablet II, "perhaps twenty lines" with more of Gilgamesh's reply to his counselors; George, p. 21.)
- **Specifics, exactly as given:** "with a [laugh at *his friend*] Enkidu" (about II 301).
- **Short quotations:**
  - "[‘Now, my friend, how frightened I am!]" (about II 302).
- **Gaps:** About II 301 is partly restored ("*his friend*" is conjectural), and about II 302–303 are restored in full. The end of the tablet is lost: "perhaps twenty lines containing more of Gilgamesh's reply to his counsellors" (George, p. 21).
- **Repetitions:** None.

## 3. Elements list (Tablet II)

### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Enkidu** (II.1–II.16). "like a god" (Shamhat's words); "This fellow" and "born in the uplands" (the shepherds' words); "tall of stature, *proud* as a battlement" (the shepherds and about II 164); "shorter of stature, and bigger of bone" (the Uruk crowd, P); "their shepherd boy Enkidu"; "my friend" (Gilgamesh's words). In Ninsun's words, he has "no [*kith or kin*]" and "no [*brother*]."
- **Shamhat**, "the harlot," "\*Shamhat" (P form: Shamkatum) (II.1–II.3, II.5–II.6).
- **The shepherds**: "The band of shepherds," "the senior shepherds" (II.2–II.4).
- **The barber** (II.3).
- **The fellow**, a wedding guest on his way to Uruk (II.5).
- **The people of Uruk**: the crowd, "The land of Uruk," "the menfolk," and the young men who set up a *champion* (II.6–II.7).
- **The maiden / the wife-to-be**, and the bridegroom (II.5–II.6). Never named.
- **Gilgamesh** (II.5–II.16). "the king of Uruk-the-City"; "like a god"; "the fellow whose features were fair"; "As one unique," raised "High over warriors," king by Enlil's decree (Enkidu's words); "Uruk's offshoot" (his own words); "You are young, Gilgamesh" (the advisers' words).
- **Ninsun** (II.8–II.9, II.14): "the wild cow of the fold, the goddess Ninsun"; "The mother of Gilgamesh"; "Wild-Cow Ninsun."
- **The smiths** (II.13).
- **The elders of Uruk**; **the senior advisers**; **the young men of Uruk** "who understand *combat*" (II.14–II.15).
- **Humbaba** (II.11, II.14–II.15), named but not present: "ferocious \*Humbaba"; "He who guards the Forest of Cedar"; his voice is "the Deluge," his speech "fire," his breath "death"; he hears the forest "at sixty leagues' distance"; second only to Adad; "the god of whom men talk" (Gilgamesh's words). George's glossary: the monstrous guardian of the Forest of Cedar, appointed by Enlil (p. 222).

### Places

- Uruk ("Uruk-the-City," "Uruk-the-Sheepfold") (II.1, II.5–II.7, II.14).
- Eanna, "the dwelling of Anu" (II.1).
- The shepherds' camp by the sheep-pen (II.2–II.5).
- A street of Uruk; the wedding house and its door; "the Square of the Land" (II.6–II.7).
- The Forest of Cedar, a sixty-league wilderness with Humbaba's lair; the uplands where Enkidu knew Humbaba (II.11, II.14–II.15).
- The forge (II.13).
- Uruk's seven gates; Gilgamesh's throne in the street; Uruk's gate (II.14).

### Objects and materials
- Shamhat's garment, shared between the two of them (II.1).
- Bread; ale, seven jugs (II.3).
- Oil (anointing); a garment; a weapon (II.3).
- The ceremonial table and the wedding food; the *veil* (II.5).
- The bed laid out for the goddess of weddings (II.6–II.7).
- The door of the wedding house, the doorposts, the wall (II.7).
- Great hatchets; axes of three talents each; great daggers with two-talent blades, half-talent handle crests and half-talent gold mountings; ten talents carried by each man (II.13).
- The throne; drums (II.14).

### Creatures
- The animals of the wild (II.1).
- Sheep (the sheep-pen) (II.2).
- Lions (Enkidu arms himself against them, drives them off, and is feared by them) (II.3–II.4, II.12).
- Wolves (II.4).
- Animals' milk, on which Enkidu was raised (II.6).
- The herd Enkidu roamed with when he knew Humbaba (II.11).

### Gods and what they do
- **Anu**: Eanna is his dwelling (II.1).
- **The goddess of weddings** (unnamed): the bed is laid out for her (II.6–II.7).
- **The gods (unnamed)**: they sanctioned the king's right to the bride (II.5).
- **Enlil**: made Gilgamesh king by destiny (II.8); set Humbaba to terrify men and keep the cedars safe (II.11, II.15).
- **Ninsun**: bore Gilgamesh; speaks about Enkidu (II.8–II.9); the drums are to sound before her (II.14).
- **Adad**: ranks first, with Humbaba second (II.11, II.15). Glossary: the Storm God, associated in the epic with the Forest of Cedar (p. 221).
- **The Igigi**: none of them could oppose Humbaba (II.11, II.15).
- **Humbaba**, called a god by Gilgamesh (II.14).

### Numbers and time
- Seven days and seven nights of coupling (II.1).
- Seven jugs of ale (II.3).
- Night watches (II.4).
- The wedding and the confrontation take place at night (II.6).
- Sixty leagues: the forest's extent in Y (II.11), Humbaba's hearing range in SB (II.11, II.15).
- Weapons: 3 talents per axe; dagger blades 2 talents; handle crests ½ talent; gold mountings ½ talent; 10 talents carried by each man (II.13).
- Seven gates (II.14).
- New Year celebrated twice in one year (II.14).
- About 20 lines lost at the end (II.16).

### Dreams and omens
- In P, the lovemaking is timed to Gilgamesh's telling of his dream (II.1).
- Destiny statements: the bride was destined for the king from his birth (II.5); Enlil destined Gilgamesh to be king (II.8).
- Trembling seizes anyone who enters Humbaba's forest (II.11, II.15).

### Customs and rituals
- Dressing someone in part of one's own garment (II.1).
- Bread as "essential to life," ale as "the lot of the land" (II.3).
- A barber's grooming, anointing with oil, then a garment and a weapon, which together make Enkidu a man (II.3).
- Keeping night watch over the flocks against wolves and lions (II.4).
- Marriage as the people's lot; the wedding banquet and ceremonial table; the *veil* parted for the king, who sleeps with the bride before the groom, "By divine consent" (II.5).
- Regular festivals of sacrifice; the young men setting up a *champion*, "rival" or "substitute" for Gilgamesh; a bed laid out for the goddess of weddings (II.6–II.7).
- The crowd kissing Enkidu's feet (II.7).
- Ending a fight by kneeling with one foot on the ground (II.8).
- Taking hands (II.10, II.13).
- Having weapons cast by smiths (II.13).
- Bolting the city gates, calling the assembly, the king enthroned in the street, addressing the elders and then the young men (II.14).
- Asking a blessing before a journey; promising a double New Year festival and drums before Ninsun on return (II.14).
- Counsel from the senior advisers (II.15).

## 4. Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **How much of Tablet II is the standard version.** Large stretches are filled from P and Y. George warns that the P lines differ "slightly" (p. 11) and that Y has "many differences" overall (pp. 172–173). The stretches that are SB text: II 1; II 36–about 46; about II 59–64; II 103–about 117; II 162–about 191; II 216–about 241; II 258–about 303. The rest of the SB text is lost: about II 2–35, 47–58, 65–102, 118–161, 169–173, 192–215, 242–257, and about 20 lines at the end. These stretches are either filled from P or Y or not filled at all.
- ⚠️ **Lacunas with no fill**: about P 166–174, the rest of Enkidu's reaction to the wedding guest ("lost in a lacuna," p. 171); about Y 91–96, the start of Gilgamesh's proposal; about II 169–173, most of Ninsun's speech; and the last ~20 lines (p. 21).
- ⚠️ **Why Enkidu weeps** (II.10). In the SB the weeping follows Ninsun's words that he has no kin and no brother (II 175–about 177), but those words are mostly restored and conjectural. On the parallel Y passage George writes: "For reasons that are not clear because of the broken state of the tablet, Enkidu is overcome with misery" (p. 173).
- ⚠️ **Who speaks II 162–about 164.** The name is lost. George's note says the episode is Gilgamesh introducing Enkidu to his mother (p. 16).
- ⚠️ **Who won the fight** (II.8). The text says only that Gilgamesh knelt, his anger cooled and he stopped, and that Enkidu then praised his kingship. George's synopses call it Enkidu accepting Gilgamesh's supremacy (p. 11) and the legitimacy of his kingship (p. 166).
- ⚠️ **Whether Enkidu takes the young men's "champion" role** (II.6). George: "as it seems … Presumably" (p. 166). The word changes by source: *champion* and "rival" in P (about P 191, P 195), "substitute" in SB (II 110). *champion* is uncertain.
- ⚠️ **The *veil*** (P 155) is an uncertain rendering.
- ⚠️ **The right of first night.** George says such things did not happen in Babylonia in the historical period. He adds that because the text calls the practice divinely ordained, it "could not have been an abuse in the context," while also saying the Babylonian audience, like Enkidu, would have reacted with horror (pp. xlviii–xlix).
- ⚠️ **Gilgamesh's wedding night** (II.6–II.7). P says Gilgamesh met the maiden by night, then that Enkidu blocked his path in the street (about P 197–201). The SB says Enkidu blocked the door of the wedding house and kept him out (about II 111–112). The text does not settle whether Gilgamesh was stopped before or after reaching the bride.
- ⚠️ **Bread** (II.3): in the SB Enkidu eyes the bread *askance*, while in P he does not know how to eat it. George joins the two versions without comment.
- ⚠️ **Enkidu's height**: "tall of stature" (the shepherds, about II 41) versus "shorter of stature" (the Uruk crowd, P 185).
- ⚠️ **Sixty leagues**: the forest's extent in Y (about Y 108), Humbaba's hearing range in SB (about II 223, II 280, about II 293).
- ⚠️ **Unnamed subjects**: the speaker of Y 162 and the "He" who bolts the gates (about Y 172). By context it is Gilgamesh in both cases.
- ⚠️ **Weights**: the hatchets' weight is not given, and the text does not show how the items make up ten talents per man (II.13).
- ⚠️ **"(Enkidu)" in round brackets** (P 200) is a translator's clarification. Round brackets are not among the conventions George lists (pp. lvi–lvii).
- ⚠️ **Line counts**: between II 216 and II 220 there are six printed lines for four numbers (II.11). In P and Y, the numbers skip ahead throughout because verses take up more than one tablet line (p. lv).
- ⚠️ **What George's chapter 1 leaves out of Y.** Before about Y 71, the Y tablet in chapter 3 has lines George does not place in Tablet II: the two kiss and become friends, Gilgamesh says he has found the counselor of his dreams, and Enkidu thanks the harlot (p. 173). Its account of the elders' answer (about Y 188–200, p. 177) also differs from the SB advisers' speech (II.15). Cross-reference only; see the chapter 3 file.

## 5. Leads (structure of the text only)
- **Lead:** **Fulfillment of the Tablet I dreams.** The crowd formula of Dream 1 (Uruk crowding around and kissing the object's feet, about I 251–255) is reused for Enkidu in Uruk's street (II 103–about 107). Ninsun's "mightiest in the land … rock from the sky" prophecy (about I 268–270) returns in the shepherds' words (about II 42–43) and in the presentation to Ninsun (II 162–about 164).
- **Lead:** **A threefold warning.** Enkidu's Humbaba warning is spoken to Gilgamesh (II.11), repeated to the elders and young men (II.15), and repeated a third time by the senior advisers (II.15). It is built on three formulas: the voice/speech/breath triad, "Adad ranks first, and Humbaba second," and Enlil's charge to keep the cedars safe.
- **Lead:** **Two audiences, two speeches.** Gilgamesh addresses the elders (Y) and then the young men (SB) (II.14). Enkidu then counsels both groups (II.15).
- **Lead:** **The civilizing sequence** runs in fixed steps: sex (II.1), clothing (II.1), being led "like a god" (II.2), bread and ale (II.3), grooming and oil (II.3), garment and weapon (II.3), and a social role as watchman (II.4).
- **Lead:** **Doubled couplets within a scene**: the "veil" couplet (about P 153–157), the combat-and-doorpost couplet (about II 114–117), and Ninsun's doubled speech-introduction (II 165–about 168). These match the doubling habits of Tablet I.
- **Lead:** The narrator's description of Enkidu's distress is repeated word for word as Gilgamesh's question (II 180–about 181 → about 186–187), the same report-then-repeat pattern as Tablet I's hunter scenes.
