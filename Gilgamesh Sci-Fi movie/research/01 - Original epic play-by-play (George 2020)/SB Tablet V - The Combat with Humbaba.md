# Tablet V. The Combat with Humbaba: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1, pp. 36-46. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How to read these notes**
- Line numbers are George's margin numbers, printed every fifth line (shown as `{l.…}` in the extracted text). Any line number that does not fall on a printed marker is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about."
- Where Tablet V is broken in the middle of Humbaba's pleas, George fills the gap with lines from a Middle Babylonian fragment found at Ugarit. He prints no line numbers for them. They are marked "(older tablet)" below.
- In the play-by-play, [square brackets] mark words George restores where the tablet is broken, "(uncertain)" marks his italics (insecure reading or rendering, Note on the Translation, p. lvi), and "…" marks text that is lost.
- Speech is written as speaker → listener: the gist.

## George's synopsis (p. 36, paraphrased)
Standing at a distance, the two look in wonder at the cedar mountain, a dense jungle loud with animal life, and Gilgamesh freezes with awe. Nervously reassuring one another, they draw their weapons and slip into the forest. Humbaba, the forest's ruler, becomes aware of them. Shamash tells them to strike fast and surprise him before he can wrap himself in the seven protective auras. Humbaba faces them and charges Enkidu with betrayal. Enkidu presses for quick action, the two attack, and Shamash blinds Humbaba with the thirteen winds so that his favorite wins. Humbaba begs to be spared, but Enkidu keeps breaking in, urging Gilgamesh to kill him before the gods learn of it. Humbaba curses them, they kill him at once, and they start felling cedar in the holy groves. Enkidu turns one exceptionally fine cedar into a huge door to decorate the temple of the god Enlil.

## Scenes

### V.1 The Forest of Cedar seen from its edge
**Lines and pages:** V 1-about 26; pp. 36-37 (p. 37 begins at V about 19).
**Where and when:** at the border of the Forest of Cedar, looking at its entrance and at the Mountain of Cedar. It follows the lost end of Tablet IV (the final approach). No time of day is given; the monkeys' drumming happens every day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu (neither speaks). Humbaba is named but not present.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The two stand and wonder at the forest, looking up at the tall cedars and at the way in.
2. Humbaba's comings and goings have worn a track; the paths run straight and are well used.
3. They see the Mountain of Cedar, where gods and goddesses have their seat and throne.
4. The cedar spreads its bounty over the land, and its shade is pleasant and delightful.
5. The thorn bushes are densely [tangled] and the trees form a covering canopy; cedars and gum trees, [interwoven], block every way in.
6. Cedar saplings [spring up] (uncertain) for a league around, and cypresses [crowd together] (uncertain) over two-thirds of a league.
7. The cedars, crusted with resin, are sixty cubits tall; the resin [seeps] out and drips like rain, running off (uncertain) into the ravines.
8. A bird starts singing all through the forest, and [hen birds] reply, making a ceaseless noise.
9. A [lone] (uncertain) tree-cricket starts a loud chorus; … sing and make the … pipe loudly (two words lost).
10. A wood pigeon moans and a turtle dove answers.
11. At the stork's [call] and the francolin's [cry] the forest exults, the second time "amid plenty."
12. [Mother monkeys] sing loudly and a young monkey screeches; [like a troupe] (uncertain) of musicians and drummers, they beat out a rhythm every day before Humbaba.

**Specifics:**
- Plants and products: tall cedars; thorn; gum trees; saplings; cypresses; resin.
- Measurements: saplings for a league around; cypresses over two-thirds of a league; cedars sixty cubits high.
- Places: the forest entrance; Humbaba's track; the Mountain of Cedar as the seat and throne of gods and goddesses; ravines.
- Animals, in the order given: a bird; hen birds; a tree-cricket; a wood pigeon; a turtle dove; a stork; a francolin; mother monkeys; a young monkey.
- Sound: a ceaseless din; a noisy chorus; a pipe; a band of musicians and drummers; a daily rhythm.
**Short quotation:**
- "Stood they and stared at the Forest, they gazed at the height of the Cedars," (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 29). Thompson 1928 calls the mountain the home of the immortals and the shrine of Irnini (a form of Ishtar, his note), where George 2020 has the seat and throne of gods and goddesses; Thompson's text breaks off before the wildlife passage, which George owes to newer sources.

**Gaps:** restored (V about 7-24): the opening words of the abundance line, the word "thick" (V 10), "all entwined," "oozed" (V 15), the first word of the bird line, "hen birds," the words for the stork's call and the francolin's cry, and "monkey mothers." Uncertain restorations: the verbs for the saplings and the cypresses, "flowing freely," "solitary" and "like a band" (V 25). Two words are lost in V 20. The rest is plain text. George's preface says new sources "enlarged the description of the Cedar Forest" in this edition (p. xii). Between V 5 and V 10 there is one printed line fewer than the margin numbers require; George's note says such mismatches come from verses doubled on one line or long lines split in two (Note on the Translation, p. lv).
**Repetitions:** the opening picture (the two standing in wonder at the forest) recurs at V about 53 (scene V.3). The forest's exulting is said twice (V about 22-23).

### V.2 Gilgamesh's terror; the two friends steady each other
**Lines and pages:** V about 27-50; pp. 37-38 (p. 38 begins at V 45).
**Where and when:** at the forest's edge, in the cedar's shadow, straight after V.1.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu (they speak in turn).

**Play-by-play:**
1. When the cedar's shadow falls on him, Gilgamesh is seized by [terror]: his arms go [stiff] and his legs tremble.
2. [Enkidu] → Gilgamesh: he urges that they [push deep] into the forest, set off and shout aloud.
3. [Gilgamesh] → Enkidu: he asks [why] they are shaking like cowards when they have crossed every mountain; the rest of his speech (something about what is before them and about withdrawing) is broken.
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (George labels the speaker "Enkidu"): his friend is a proven fighter, and a man who has fought does not fear death. Having been spattered with blood, Gilgamesh need not fear it. He should [go] berserk and work himself into a frenzy like a dervish, let [his war cry] boom like a kettle drum, and shake the stiffness out of his arms and the trembling out of his legs.
5. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (George labels the speaker "Gilgamesh"): they will hold hands and [go on] together, with their minds on the fight. They should put death out of mind and [look for] (uncertain) life. The prudent man is the one who walks beside his companion.
6. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he cites a saying, that the one who leads should look after himself and get his comrade through safely; and he says such men have won a name that lasts for days to come (the text says "they" without spelling out who).

**Specifics:** the cedar's shadow; stiff arms; trembling legs; cowards; all the mountains crossed; spattered with blood; berserk; a dervish's frenzy; a kettle drum; holding hands; a lasting name.
**Short quotation:**
- "O forget death, and be fearful(?) of nothing(?)" (Thompson 1928, Tablet IV, p. 28). Thompson 1928 places this exchange in his Tablet IV and reads it the other way round: there Enkidu's hands have grown weak and Gilgamesh encourages him, praising Enkidu as skilled in war. George 2020 has Gilgamesh struck with terror and Enkidu telling him he is a tested fighter.

**Gaps:** restored: the words for terror and stiffness, the two speakers' names, Enkidu's opening urgings, Gilgamesh's "why" and "we who," the beginning of his broken question, "friend," "go," "your shout," "like," "on," "let," the openings of the prudent-man line and of the saying, and "for days"; the verb "seek" is uncertain. V about 37-38 is broken. The speaker labels are George's additions. George's preface says new sources settled, for the first time, the right order of the friends' conversation before the battle (p. xii).
**Repetitions:** Enkidu's wish that the stiffness and trembling leave Gilgamesh (V about 44) repeats the description of his terror (V about 29-30). The saying about the one who leads echoes the elders' proverb in Tablet III (III about 4-5, about 218-219).

### V.3 The approach with weapons drawn
**Lines and pages:** V about 51-60; p. 38.
**Where and when:** at some far-off (uncertain) spot in the forest, then into it, straight after V.2.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The two reach a "distant" (uncertain) … ; they [stop] talking and stand still.
2. They stand gazing in wonder at the forest; … (a whole line is lost).
3. Swords are at once … and drawn from their sheaths … (broken).
4. The axes have been smeared with …; hatchet and sword are in … (broken).
5. Someone …; they creep into … (broken). George's synopsis supplies the sense: they draw their weapons and sneak into the forest.

**Specifics:** swords; scabbards; axes "smeared" with something; a hatchet.
**Short quotation:**
- "[Still’d into silence(?)] their speech, and they themselves (suddenly) stopping." (Thompson 1928, Tablet IV, p. 28). Thompson 1928 has the two arrive at a "barrier(?)" and break off his Tablet IV there.

**Gaps:** nearly every line is broken: V about 54 is completely lost, and V 55-60 keep only their opening words. "Distant" (V about 51) is italicized; the words at the start of V about 51, the verb for ceasing, and "and" in V about 58 are restored.
**Repetitions:** V about 53 repeats V 1.

### V.4 Humbaba senses intruders
**Lines and pages:** V about 61-72; p. 38.
**Where and when:** inside the forest; Humbaba mentions his own bed. No time given.
**Who:** Humbaba, alone, speaking to himself.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba [speaks to himself] (the introduction is restored).
2. Humbaba (to himself): a string of broken questions: whether some … went …; why … are disturbed; why his own … are …; something in terror (uncertain); how indeed …; something in his very bed.
3. Humbaba (to himself): he decides it must be Enkidu …, who in goodwill …; and if a word to …, he calls on Enlil to curse him.

**Specifics:** Humbaba's bed; Enkidu suspected by name; Enlil invoked for a curse.
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain text, and the lines are too broken to quote usefully.
**Gaps:** every line is broken; the speech introduction (V about 61) is entirely restored. "In terror" (V about 66) is italicized. George's synopsis gives the sense: Humbaba becomes aware of the intruders.
**Repetitions:** none in this passage.

### V.5 Enkidu: "two are two"
**Lines and pages:** V about 73-89; p. 39 (p. 39 begins at V about 73).
**Where and when:** in the forest, after Humbaba's muttering. No time given.
**Who:** Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: something about Humbaba (broken); then a proverb: a single friend is only one, but [two are two].
2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: more sayings on the strength of pairs: even weak, two …; where one man alone [cannot climb] a sloping rampart, two [succeed] (uncertain); two triplets …; a rope of three strands [does not break easily]; two pups of a strong dog [overpower it] (uncertain).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: broken advice: stand firm …; something about an arrow's flight …; the journey Gilgamesh is making (uncertain) …; once they have left …; something carried …; its two ….
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: something about the winds (uncertain) of Shamash; a line saying that a tempest is behind and wind [in front] (whose is not said); and the advice to speak to Shamash so that he [lends them his winds] (uncertain).

**Specifics:** the "two are two" proverb; a sloping rampart; two triplets; a three-stranded rope; a strong dog's two pups; an arrow; a tempest; wind; Shamash's winds.
**Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928 has no text for this passage, and George's wording is not needed.
**Gaps:** most lines are broken at the end. Restored: the second half of the "two are two" line, the clause about climbing the rampart, the rope's not breaking, and the wind "in front"; uncertain: the verbs about succeeding and overpowering, "making," "winds," and the request for Shamash's winds. Whose rear and front are meant in V about 88 is not stated. ⚠️
**Repetitions:** a run of proverbs about the strength of two (V 75-80).

### V.6 Gilgamesh prays; Shamash answers from the sky
**Lines and pages:** V 90-100; p. 39.
**Where and when:** in the forest, directly after Enkidu's advice to appeal to Shamash. No time is stated, though Gilgamesh's tears are said to flow in the sunlight (V about 91).
**Who:** Gilgamesh (speaker) → Shamash; Shamash (a voice from the sky) → the heroes.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh raises his head, [weeping before Shamash], his tears running down in the sun's rays.
2. Gilgamesh → Shamash: he asks the god [to remember] the day he put his trust in him, to come to his help now and … (lost), and [to protect] Gilgamesh, the shoot that sprang from the heart of Uruk.
3. Shamash hears, and at once a voice [calls out from the sky].
4. Shamash → the heroes: they must not be afraid but must confront Humbaba. Humbaba must not [get into his forest], must not go down to the grove, and must not … (lost).
5. Shamash → the heroes: Humbaba must not put on his seven cloaks; right now he has one on and has taken the other six off.

**Specifics:** tears in the sun's rays; Gilgamesh's self-description as the offshoot from Uruk's midst (V about 94); a voice from the sky; Humbaba's forest and grove; seven cloaks, one worn and six removed. George's synopsis calls them the seven auras that protect Humbaba, and his Introduction describes Humbaba as wrapped for protection in seven radiant, deadly auras (p. xxxiii).
**Short quotation:**
- "Thou shall put on seven garments" (Thompson 1928, Tablet IV, p. 27). In Thompson 1928 these broken words come in a speech whose speaker he thinks is "probably Enkidu," at the Gate of the Forest, not from Shamash.

**Gaps:** restored: the words of weeping, "his tears," the plea not to forget, the plea for protection, the words describing the voice from the sky, Humbaba's entering the forest, "must not" in V about 99, and the number "one" in V 100. V about 93 and V about 98 have lost their ends.
**Repetitions:** the formula of lifting the head and weeping before Shamash with tears in the sun's rays is used later for Humbaba at each plea (V 185-about 186; the Ugarit lines; V about 256-257; V about 272-273). Humbaba calls Gilgamesh the offshoot from Uruk's midst at V 170.

### V.7 Humbaba bellows
**Lines and pages:** V about 101-106, then a short gap; pp. 39-40 (p. 40 begins at V about 103).
**Where and when:** in the forest, right after Shamash's words.
**Who:** "They" (not identified; see Gaps); Humbaba, the guardian of the forests.

**Play-by-play:**
1. "They" … like a ferocious wild bull about to charge … (broken).
2. Humbaba roars once, a terrifying roar: the guardian of the forests is bellowing.
3. … (a whole line lost).
4. Humbaba [thunders] like the Storm God.
5. George's note (p. 40): a short gap follows, and when the text picks up again the heroes are standing face to face with the forest's guardian.

**Specifics:** a wild bull ready to charge (a simile); a single terrifying bellow; Humbaba as guardian of the forests; thunder like the Storm God's (the god is not named here; George's glossary gives Adad as the Storm God, with a special link to the Forest of Cedar in the epic, p. 221).
**Short quotation:**
- "He like a mighty wild bull . . ." (Thompson 1928, Tablet IV, p. 27). In Thompson 1928 the next lines have "he" fling the portal away and shout a challenge to the forest's guard.

**Gaps:** V about 101 is broken after "They"; V 105 is wholly lost; the verb "thundering" (V about 106) is restored. The gap after V about 106 runs to V about 114, about eight lines.
**Repetitions:** none in this passage.

### V.8 Face to face: Humbaba taunts the two and accuses Enkidu
**Lines and pages:** V 115-about 124; p. 40.
**Where and when:** in the forest, face to face, after the short gap.
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker) → Gilgamesh, then → Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba → Gilgamesh: fools, he says, should seek advice from the crude and boorish; he asks why Gilgamesh has come before him.
2. Humbaba → Enkidu: he insults Enkidu as the offspring of a fish, fatherless, hatched from terrapin and turtle, never nursed by a mother.
3. Humbaba → Enkidu: he says he watched Enkidu when he was young but stayed away from him, and asks whether Enkidu's … (lost) would have filled (uncertain) his stomach.
4. Humbaba → Enkidu: [now] Enkidu has treacherously brought Gilgamesh to him and stands there like a hostile foreigner.
5. Humbaba (threat): he will cut Gilgamesh's throat and windpipe and give his flesh to the forest birds, the greedy eagle and the vulture.

**Specifics:** "spawn of a fish"; "hatchling" of terrapin and turtle; no father; no mother's milk; watched in his youth; treachery; a warlike stranger; throat and gullet; forest birds; eagle and vulture.
**Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928 has no text for this confrontation, and George's wording is not needed.
**Gaps:** "have sated" (V 120) is italicized and the word before it is lost; "now" (V about 121) is restored. The rest is plain text.
**Repetitions:** the threat to feed someone's flesh to the forest birds, eagle and vulture, returns in Humbaba's second plea, said this time of Enkidu (V about 193).

### V.9 Gilgamesh wavers; Enkidu urges him on
**Lines and pages:** V 125-about 142; pp. 40-41 (p. 41 begins at V about 136).
**Where and when:** face to face with Humbaba, straight after his taunts.
**Who:** Gilgamesh → Enkidu; Enkidu → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: Humbaba's face has changed. They came boldly to his lair to beat him, yet a heart that has been frightened cannot settle at once.
2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he asks why Gilgamesh talks like a coward and says such weak words trouble him.
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: they have just one [task] (uncertain), and the metal is already flowing into the mold. He uses images from metalworking and the flood: should they spend an hour stoking the furnace, or an hour blowing on (uncertain) the coals? Unleashing the Deluge is a matter of cracking the whip.
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he must [not] hold back or retreat, but strike hard, [like] a lion. Two lines are lost. Then: [let] the South Wind [seize] Humbaba as a hostage.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he should remember his god Lugalbanda and keep in mind every dream he had.

**Specifics:** Humbaba's changed features; his lair; copper poured into a mold; a furnace; coals; an hour (twice); the Deluge; a whip; a lion; the South Wind taking a hostage; Lugalbanda as Gilgamesh's god; the dreams of the journey.
**Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928 has no text for this exchange, and George's wording is not needed.
**Gaps:** the word "task" (V about 132) is an uncertain restoration; "blow on" (V about 134) is italicized; the negative "don't," the words "like that of a," "let" and "take him" are restored; V about 138-139 is lost apart from a word or two.
**Repetitions:** the "one task" phrase echoes Enkidu's dream readings in Tablet IV (Schøyen2, Ha1). The reminder of "all the dreams" points back to the dreams of Tablet IV.

### V.10 The combat and the thirteen winds
**Lines and pages:** V about 143-165; p. 41.
**Where and when:** in the heart of the forest (V about 147), on the mountain, straight after Enkidu's urging.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu, Humbaba (speaks), Shamash (acts).

**Play-by-play:**
1. Taking his friend's advice, Gilgamesh moves [forward]. He strikes the bedrock "nine times" (uncertain) and the mountain splits apart.
2. He attacks savagely, like a lion, and Enkidu leaps in like a "puma" (uncertain).
3. They seize Humbaba in the depths of the forest. His dreadful auras fill the forest, and they grab hold of the auras with their hands.
4. Humbaba roars, howling to himself: he threatens to raise "them" and climb up into heaven, or to strike the earth and send "them" down into the Ocean Below.
5. He lifts them, but the sky is out of reach; he strikes the ground, but the bedrock holds firm.
6. The ground cracks open under their heels, and as they spin around they smash Mounts Sirion and Lebanon.
7. The white clouds go black and rain death on them like mist.
8. Shamash sends the great gale-winds against Humbaba, the same thirteen as in Ninsun's prayer and in the same order: 1 South, 2 North, 3 East, 4 West, 5 Blast, 6 Counterblast, 7 Typhoon, 8 Hurricane, 9 Tempest, 10 Devil-Wind, 11 Frost-Wind, 12 Gale, 13 Tornado.
9. With the thirteen winds risen, Humbaba's face goes dark: he can neither charge ahead nor kick back.
10. Then Gilgamesh's weapons overcome Humbaba.

**Specifics:** nine blows (uncertain); bedrock; the mountain broken; lion and "puma" (uncertain) similes; Humbaba's auras, seized by hand; the sky; the Ocean Below; Mounts Sirion and Lebanon (George's glossary: Sirion is the Anti-Lebanon range, including Mount Hermon, p. 224); clouds turned from white to black; death like mist; thirteen named winds; Humbaba unable to move forward or back.
**Short quotation:**
- "Unto th’ entreaty of Gilgamish hearken’d the Sun-god in heaven, / Wherefore against Humbaba he raised mighty winds:" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 31). Thompson 1928 takes this from the Hittite version and counts eight winds, not thirteen.

**Gaps:** "forward" (V about 143) and the "I shall" at the start of Humbaba's two boasts (V about 151-152) are restored; "nine times" and "puma" are italicized. The rest is plain text.
**Repetitions:** V about 159-165 fulfills, almost word for word, the request Ninsun asked Aya to pass on to Shamash (III about 87-93): the same thirteen winds in the same order, Humbaba's face growing dark, and Gilgamesh's weapons winning.

### V.11 Humbaba's first plea, to Gilgamesh; Enkidu objects
**Lines and pages:** V about 166-184; p. 42 (p. 42 begins at V about 167).
**Where and when:** where Humbaba has been overpowered, directly after the winds.
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker) → Gilgamesh; Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba → Gilgamesh (begging for his life): Gilgamesh is so young, newly born to his mother, yet truly the son of [Wild-Cow Ninsun].
2. Humbaba → Gilgamesh: at Shamash's order Gilgamesh has leveled ten mountains; Humbaba addresses him as the shoot from Uruk's heart and as king.
3. Humbaba → Gilgamesh: a dead man has never been of use (uncertain) to a master, but [a living slave earns] for his owner. He begs for mercy, says he is utterly [crushed] by misery, and asks to stay in the forest and serve Gilgamesh's every wish.
4. Humbaba → Gilgamesh (an offer): he will grow and guard for Gilgamesh as many trees as he wants, myrtle, cedar and cypress, fine straight timber to make his palace splendid.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he must not listen to Humbaba's pleas or let them into his thoughts at all.
6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: if Humbaba gets back to his home, the two of them are as good as never born. [He will tie] them up in the Forest of Cedar and [then] go into the grove and put [his auras] on.

**Specifics:** Gilgamesh's youth; son of Wild-Cow Ninsun; ten mountains flattened at Shamash's command; Gilgamesh hailed as king; slave and owner; master; myrtle, cedar, cypress; straight timber for the palace; Humbaba's home and grove; his auras.
**Short quotation:**
- "be [thou] now my [master], and I’ll be thy henchman:" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 31, from the Hittite version). In Thompson 1928, Enkidu answers that Gilgamesh must not accept the offer, since Humbaba must not stay alive.

**Gaps:** restored: the name "Wild-Cow Ninsun," the words "but a slave" and "brings profit," "prostrated," "friend," "why," "he will bind," "then" and "on his auras"; "please" (V about 171) is italicized.
**Repetitions:** the phrase "offshoot sprung from Uruk's midst" repeats Gilgamesh's words in his prayer (V about 94).

### V.12 Humbaba's second plea, to Enkidu; Enkidu: "finish him"
**Lines and pages:** V about 184-204; pp. 42-43 (p. 43 begins at V about 197).
**Where and when:** the same place, straight after Enkidu's objection.
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker) → Shamash (weeping) and → Enkidu; Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba [hears Enkidu] speaking against [him].
2. Humbaba [raises his head and weeps before Shamash], [tears] running down [in the sun's rays].
3. [About three lines lost: V about 187-189.]
4. Humbaba → Enkidu: Enkidu knows the paths of his forest, and he knows every art of speaking. Humbaba wishes he had hanged Enkidu from a sapling at the forest's entrance and fed his flesh to the forest birds, eagle and vulture.
5. Humbaba → Enkidu: his freedom now depends on Enkidu, who should tell Gilgamesh to spare his life.
6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: Humbaba, the guardian of the Forest of [Cedar], must be finished off, killed and stripped of his power. (He says this twice.)
7. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: before Enlil, the chief god, hears what they are doing, and before the [great] gods turn on them in anger (Enlil in Nippur, Shamash in [Larsa] …), Gilgamesh should secure for himself a [lasting fame] (uncertain): the story of how he [killed the fierce] (uncertain) Humbaba.

**Specifics:** the paths of the forest; skill in speech; a sapling at the forest entrance; forest birds, eagle and vulture; Humbaba's release in Enkidu's hands; Humbaba as guardian of the Forest of Cedar; Enlil as foremost god; the great gods; Enlil in Nippur; Shamash in Larsa; enduring fame. George's glossary: Nippur was Enlil's cult center (p. 223); Larsa, between Uruk and Ur, held one of Shamash's cult centers (p. 223).
**Short quotation (George 2020; no public-domain wording exists for this passage):**
- "finish him, slay him, do away with his power!" (V about 197; said again at once, and in each of Enkidu's later replies)

**Gaps:** restored: the words saying that Humbaba heard Enkidu, "him," the weeping formula, "his tears," the sunlight phrase, "my" in the release line, "Cedar" (twice), "great" and "Larsa"; uncertain: the words for fame and for slaying the fierce Humbaba. A lacuna (* * *) of about three lines follows V about 186. V about 202 breaks off after "Larsa."
**Repetitions:** the weeping formula (see V.6). Humbaba's regret that he did not feed Enkidu's flesh to the forest birds repeats his threat against Gilgamesh (V about 124). The plea that Enkidu tell Gilgamesh to spare him recurs in the Ugarit lines (V.13) and at V about 263-264 (V.14). Enkidu's speech (finish him; before Enlil hears; win fame) recurs at V about 244-254 (restored) and V 265-270.

### V.13 Humbaba's third plea (supplied from Ugarit); Enkidu's reply (restored)
**Lines and pages:** V 205-about 206; then the standard text is lost (about V 207-243) and George inserts unnumbered lines from a fragment found at Ugarit (older tablet); then V about 244-254, wholly restored; pp. 43-44 (p. 44 begins at V about 246).
**Where and when:** the same place, straight after Enkidu's second "finish him."
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker) → Shamash and → Enkidu; Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba hears Enkidu's abuse (restored) and [raises] his head, [weeping before Shamash].
2. George's note (p. 43): the standard text of the third plea is missing; in its place he offers lines from a Middle Babylonian fragment found at Ugarit, which he judges a good fit. He quotes it:
3. (Older tablet) On hearing this, Humbaba lifts his head and weeps before [Shamash], his tears running in the sun's rays.
4. (Older tablet) Humbaba (the addressee is unclear; he speaks of Enkidu in the third person and then turns to Shamash): when Enkidu slept among his animals, Humbaba never shouted to [him]; and Mounts [Sirion] and Lebanon, where cedars grow, had never been [despoiled] (uncertain).
5. (Older tablet) Humbaba → Shamash: since Shamash is his lord and judge: Humbaba had no mother to give him birth [and no father to raise him]; a mountain bore him and Shamash raised him.
6. (Older tablet) Humbaba → Enkidu: his freedom lies [with Enkidu], who should [tell Gilgamesh] to spare his life.
7. George's note (p. 43): Enkidu's answer can be restored from the earlier one.
8. [Enkidu → Gilgamesh, all restored from V about 196-204: Humbaba, guardian of the Forest of Cedar, must be finished off, killed and stripped of his power (twice); before Enlil hears and the great gods grow angry (Enlil in Nippur, Shamash in Larsa …), Gilgamesh should win lasting fame for killing the fierce Humbaba.] (V about 244-254)

**Specifics:** Enkidu once sleeping with his animals; Sirion and Lebanon as cedar-growing mountains; Shamash as Humbaba's lord and judge; a mountain as Humbaba's mother; Shamash as the one who raised him; no mother and no father.
**Short quotation (George 2020; the Ugarit fragments were found in 1994, so no public-domain wording exists):**
- "[it was a] mountain that bore me and you that reared me!" (Ugarit lines; George prints no line number)

**Gaps:**
- The standard version's third plea is lost (about V 207-243 by the margin numbers).
- In the Ugarit lines, "him," "Sirion," the opening words of the "lord and judge" line, the line about the father, "it was a," "with you" and "tell Gilgamesh" are restored; the word for "despoiled" is an uncertain restoration.
- V about 244-254 is entirely in brackets.
**George's note on the source:** three fragments and a complete tablet of Gilgamesh were dug up in 1994 at Ras Shamra (ancient Ugarit), in the House of Urtenu; they date no later than the twelfth century BC and were published in 2007. The fragments relate to Tablets IV-V and are very corrupt, but the passage of Humbaba's plea is readable and has been used to restore Tablet V (p. 197).
**Repetitions:** the weeping formula appears twice here, once in the standard version (V 205-about 206) and once in the Ugarit lines. The plea for Enkidu's help repeats V about 194-195. Enkidu's reply is restored as an exact repeat of V about 196-204.

### V.14 Humbaba's fourth plea, to Enkidu; Enkidu's third "finish him"
**Lines and pages:** V 255-270; p. 44.
**Where and when:** the same place, straight after V.13.
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker) → Enkidu; Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. [Humbaba] hears [Enkidu's abuse]; he lifts his head, [weeping before Shamash], [tears running] in the sun's rays.
2. Humbaba → Enkidu: broken lines: Enkidu entered …; in the clash of arms a prince …, but for the prince's palace servant the fighting is … (lost).
3. Humbaba → Enkidu: Enkidu sits before [Gilgamesh] like a shepherd, like his hired hand [carrying out his orders] (conjectural).
4. Humbaba → Enkidu: [his freedom] now rests with Enkidu …; he should tell Gilgamesh to [spare] his life.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the guardian of the Forest [of Cedar] must be [finished off], killed and [stripped of his power] (said once this time).
6. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: before [Enlil] hears what they are doing and the [great] gods turn angry (Enlil in Nippur, Shamash in [Larsa] …), Gilgamesh should make his [fame] (uncertain) last forever: the tale of how he killed (uncertain) the [fierce] Humbaba.

**Specifics:** clashing weapons; a prince; his palace retainer; a shepherd; a hired man; Enlil in Nippur; Shamash in Larsa.
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain text, and Enkidu's refrain is quoted in V.12.
**Gaps:** V about 258-260 is broken at the line ends. Restored: Humbaba's name and the words of his hearing the abuse, the weeping formula, "him," "my release," "spare," "of Cedar," the opening and closing words of Enkidu's refrain, the speech tag, "Enlil," "great," "Larsa" and "ferocious"; uncertain: the words for "doing his bidding," the word "fame," and the verb "slew."
**Repetitions:** the weeping formula; the plea that Enkidu ask Gilgamesh to spare him (V about 194-195, the Ugarit lines); Enkidu's speech (V about 196-204, V about 244-254). In this third occurrence, the refrain about finishing off the guardian appears once, not twice, as printed.

### V.15 Humbaba's curse; Enkidu brushes it aside
**Lines and pages:** V about 271-284; pp. 44-45 (p. 45 begins at V about 279).
**Where and when:** the same place, straight after Enkidu's third "finish him."
**Who:** Humbaba (speaker; the curse's addressee is not named); Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Humbaba hears [Enkidu's abuse]; he lifts [his head, weeping before Shamash], [tears running] in the [sun's rays].
2. [About five lines lost: V about 274-278.]
3. Humbaba (a curse): he wishes that neither of the two will live to grow old, and that nobody except his friend Gilgamesh will bury Enkidu.
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he complains that Gilgamesh is not listening to him; while the curses … (broken), [the curses should go back] (conjectural) into Humbaba's own mouth.

**Specifics:** "the pair of them"; not growing old; burial; Gilgamesh as Enkidu's only burier; curses sent back to the mouth that spoke them.
**Short quotation (George 2020; no public-domain wording exists for this line):**
- "besides Gilgamesh his friend, none shall bury Enkidu!" (V 280)

**George's comment:** in the Introduction George writes that the gods later condemn Enkidu to an early death without a family, which fulfills Humbaba's dying curse (p. xlix).
**Gaps:** a lacuna (* * *) of about five lines (V about 274-278) comes before the surviving end of the curse, so its opening is lost. The words of Humbaba's hearing the abuse, the weeping formula, "his tears flowing" and the sunlight phrase are restored; V about 283 is broken; the clause about the curses returning (V about 284) is conjectural.
**Repetitions:** the weeping formula, for the last time.

### V.16 The killing of Humbaba
**Lines and pages:** V 285-about 292; p. 45.
**Where and when:** the same place, on the mountain (the blood falls on it), right after Enkidu's words.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu (both act; no speech), Humbaba.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh [takes in his friend's words] and pulls out [the sword at] his side.
2. Gilgamesh [stabs] Humbaba in the neck.
3. Enkidu cuts out [his heart] and pulls it out along with the lungs.
4. … leaping up (what leaps up is lost).
5. "He" (which hero is not stated) takes the tusks [from] the head as spoils.
6. Plenty of [blood] and [gore] (both conjectural) pour down on the mountain.

**Specifics:** the sword at Gilgamesh's side; the neck wound; the heart and lungs; Humbaba's tusks taken as spoils; blood and gore on the mountain. Figure 6 (p. 45) is a drawing of Gilgamesh and Enkidu killing Humbaba, after an engraving on a bronze situla of the early first millennium BC (List of Figures); its caption is the line about the stab to the neck. It is an illustration, not text.
**Short quotation:**
- ". . . . [they cut off] the head of Humbaba." (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 31). Thompson 1928 ends his Tablet V with this line, the last of six badly broken lines of the Assyrian version; George 2020 has the stab to the neck, the heart and lungs, and the tusks, and mentions the head only on the raft (V.20).

**Gaps:** restored: the words for Gilgamesh hearing his friend, "the sword at," the verb "stabbed," "his heart" and "from"; conjectural: "blood" and "gore." The beginning of V about 289 is lost. George's note after V about 292 (p. 46): a short gap follows, during which the heroes defile the forest.
**Repetitions:** the line about plenty falling on the mountain is said twice (V about 291-292).

### V.17 The forest desecrated; Enkidu fears Enlil's question
**Lines and pages:** a short lacuna (about V 293-299), then V 300-about 306; p. 46.
**Where and when:** in the forest, after the killing; how long after is not stated.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (acts), Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh. Enlil is imagined as the questioner.

**Play-by-play:**
1. George's note (p. 46): during a short gap the heroes defile the forest.
2. Gilgamesh [tramples his way through the] forest to collect cedar resin [for Enlil's table].
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: they have laid the forest waste, and he asks how they will answer to Enlil in Nippur.
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh, voicing the question they expect to face (the asker is not named): you killed the guardian by force; what rage made you go on and trample the forest?

**Specifics:** cedar resin; Enlil's table (restored); a wasteland; Enlil in Nippur; the guardian; rage.
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain text for this passage.
**Gaps:** the gap before V 300 is known only from George's note. Restored: the words for trampling through the forest, "for the table," Enkidu's name, "my friend," "to," "how" and "in."
**Repetitions:** the trampling of the forest is mentioned by the narrator (V 300) and again in the imagined question (V about 306).

### V.18 The seven sons; felling the cedars
**Lines and pages:** V about 307-312; p. 46.
**Where and when:** in the forest, after V.17. How long the felling lasts is not stated.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu (both act).

**Play-by-play:**
1. The text says that "they" had by now killed "his" seven sons, named Cricket, Screecher, Typhoon, Screamer, Crafty, … (one name lost) and Storm. The killing itself is not narrated in what survives; "his" follows the account of Humbaba.
2. Their axes are hatchets weighing two talents each; … they cut off … (broken); each stroke cuts chips three and a half cubits long.
3. Gilgamesh fells the trees while Enkidu picks out the finest timber (uncertain).

**Specifics:** seven sons, named as printed: Cricket, Screecher, Typhoon, Screamer, Crafty, … (lost), Storm; hatchets of two talents each; chips of three and a half cubits per stroke; the best timber (uncertain).
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain text, and the names are listed above.
**Gaps:** one son's name is lost; V about 309 is broken at the start; "best timber" (V about 312) is italicized. The word "long" in V 310 is in parentheses in George's text.
**Repetitions:** none in this passage.

### V.19 Enkidu's door for Enlil
**Lines and pages:** V about 313-319; p. 46.
**Where and when:** in the forest, during the felling.
**Who:** Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: they have brought down a towering cedar whose crown reached the sky.
2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he is making it into a door measuring six rods high, two rods wide and one cubit thick, with its pole and its top and bottom pivots all cut from the same single piece.
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the Euphrates will carry the door to Enlil's house in Nippur, and the Nippur sanctuary will [rejoice].

**Specifics:** a cedar tall enough to reach the sky; door dimensions: 6 rods (height) × 2 rods (breadth) × 1 cubit (thickness); pole and both pivots in one piece; the Euphrates; Enlil's house in Nippur; the Nippur sanctuary. George's synopsis calls it a great door to adorn Enlil's temple.
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain text for this moment (Thompson 1928's door speech, Tablet IV, p. 27, belongs to a later episode in George's arrangement).
**Gaps:** "and" and "rejoice" (V about 319) are restored. The rest is plain text.
**Repetitions:** none in this passage.

## Elements list

### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Gilgamesh:** gripped by terror at the forest's edge (V.2); the "offshoot" from Uruk's midst (his own words, V about 94; Humbaba's, V 170); hailed as king (V 170); called very young and the son of Wild-Cow Ninsun by Humbaba (V about 167-168). V.1-V.20.
- **Enkidu:** insulted by Humbaba as a fish's spawn and a turtle's hatchling, without father or mother's milk (V about 117-118); likened to a hostile stranger (V about 122); said to know the ways of the forest and every art of speech (V 190-about 191); likened to Gilgamesh's shepherd and hired man (V about 261-262); once slept among his animals (Ugarit lines); accused of treachery (V.8). V.1-V.20.
- **Humbaba:** guardian of the forests (V about 104); thunders like the Storm God (V about 106); "Humbaba who guards the Forest of Cedar" in Enkidu's refrain (V.12-V.14); "the guardian" (V 305); the forest king (synopsis); born of a mountain and raised by Shamash, without mother or father (Ugarit lines); wears seven cloaks, also called auras; has tusks; has seven sons. V.1, V.4, V.6-V.20.
- **Humbaba's seven sons:** Cricket, Screecher, Typhoon, Screamer, Crafty, … (lost), Storm; already dead when mentioned (V.18).
- **Shamash:** speaks as a voice from the sky (V.6); Humbaba's lord and judge (Ugarit lines). V.5, V.6, V.10-V.15.
- **Ninsun:** named by Humbaba as Wild-Cow Ninsun, Gilgamesh's mother (V.11).
- **Lugalbanda:** Gilgamesh's god, to be kept in mind (Enkidu, V about 141). V.9.
- **Enlil:** the foremost god; Enlil in Nippur; his table, house and sanctuary. V.4, V.12-V.14, V.17, V.19.
- **A prince and his palace retainer:** in Humbaba's broken fourth plea (V about 259-260). V.14.

### Places
- The Forest of Cedar: its entrance, Humbaba's track and paths, the thorn thicket and canopy, ravines (V.1); its heart (V.10); the grove (V.6, V.11); a sapling at the entrance (V.12).
- The Mountain of Cedar, seat and throne of gods and goddesses (V.1); the mountain where the blood falls (V.16).
- Humbaba's lair (V.9), his home (V.11), his bed (V.4).
- All the mountains the heroes have crossed (V.2).
- Mounts Sirion and Lebanon (V.10, V.13).
- The sky and the Ocean Below (V.10).
- Nippur, with Enlil's house and sanctuary; Larsa (V.12-V.14, V.17, V.19).
- The Euphrates (V.19).
- Uruk, in the epithet for Gilgamesh (V.6, V.11).

### Objects and materials
- Trees and their products: sixty-cubit cedars crusted with resin; resin dripping like rain; gum trees; cypresses; saplings; thorn (V.1); myrtle, cedar and cypress offered as timber (V.11); resin for Enlil's table (V.17).
- Weapons: swords and sheaths; smeared axes; a hatchet (V.3); the sword at Gilgamesh's side (V.16); two-talent hatchets (V.18).
- Humbaba's seven cloaks (V.6) and dreadful auras (V.10, V.11).
- Body parts: throat and gullet (Humbaba's threat against Gilgamesh, V.8); heart and lungs; tusks taken as spoils; the head (V.16, V.20).
- Similes and proverbs: a kettle drum (V.2); a sloping rampart, a three-stranded rope (V.5); molten copper and a mold, a furnace, coals, a whip (V.9).
- The door: 6 rods high, 2 rods wide, 1 cubit thick, pole and pivots in one piece (V.19).
- A raft of cedar and cypress logs, lashed tight and loaded with cedar (V.20).

### Creatures
- In the forest: a bird and hen birds; a tree-cricket; a wood pigeon; a turtle dove; a stork; a francolin; mother monkeys and a young monkey (V.1).
- In similes: a wild bull (V.7); a lion (V.9, V.10); a "puma" (V.10, uncertain).
- In insults and threats: fish, terrapin and turtle (V.8); forest birds, eagle and vulture (V.8, V.12).
- In proverbs: a strong dog's two pups (V.5).
- Enkidu's animals (V.13).

### Gods and what they do
- **Shamash:** answers Gilgamesh's prayer from the sky and tells them to attack before Humbaba can put on more of his seven cloaks (V.6); sends the thirteen winds that blind Humbaba (V.10); Humbaba weeps to him at each plea (V.12-V.15); paired with his cult city Larsa (V.12).
- **Enlil:** called on by Humbaba to curse the intruder (V.4); the god whose hearing Enkidu fears (V.12-V.14); the god they must answer to for the forest (V.17); receives resin for his table and a cedar door for his house (V.17, V.19).
- **The great gods:** may turn against the heroes in anger (V.12-V.14).
- **The Storm God:** Humbaba's thundering is compared to his (V.7).
- **Lugalbanda:** Gilgamesh's god, to be remembered in the fight (V.9).
- **Gods and goddesses:** the Mountain of Cedar is their seat and throne (V.1).

### Numbers and time
- One league; two-thirds of a league; sixty cubits (V.1).
- Seven cloaks: one on, six off (V.6).
- Nine blows on the bedrock (uncertain) (V.10).
- Ten mountains leveled (V.11).
- Thirteen winds (V.10).
- Proverbs about pairs: two friends, two triplets, a three-stranded rope, two pups (V.5).
- Four pleas by Humbaba (the third supplied from Ugarit) and four answers by Enkidu, three of them the "finish him" speech (V.11-V.14).
- Seven sons (V.18); two-talent hatchets; chips three and a half cubits long (V.18).
- The door: 6 rods, 2 rods, 1 cubit (V.19).
- Time words: the monkeys drum every day (V.1); an hour, twice (V.9); the sun's rays in the weeping formula (V.6, V.12-V.15); everlasting fame and a name for days to come (V.2, V.12). No time of day and no length of stay is stated.

### Dreams and omens
- No dreams are told in this tablet; Enkidu tells Gilgamesh to keep all his dreams in mind (V about 142).
- A divine voice: Shamash speaks from the sky (V.6).
- A curse: Humbaba's dying curse that the two will not grow old and that only Gilgamesh will bury Enkidu (V.15); George says the gods later fulfill it (Introduction, p. xlix).

### Customs and rituals
- Prayer: raising the head and weeping before Shamash, with tears in the sun's rays (Gilgamesh, V.6; Humbaba, V.12-V.15).
- Battle custom: a loud shout, going berserk, a war cry like a kettle drum (V.2).
- Terms of surrender offered: Humbaba to live as a slave and tend timber for Gilgamesh's palace (V.11).
- Fame as the aim: everlasting renown (V.12-V.14); a lasting name (V.2).
- Trophies: the tusks taken as spoils; the head carried on the raft (V.16, V.20).
- Offerings to Enlil: resin for his table; a cedar door for his Nippur house, floated down the Euphrates (V.17, V.19).
- Burial, named in the curse (V.15).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ V about 51-72 (the approach with weapons and Humbaba's muttering) is too broken to give a connected sense; George's synopsis supplies the drawing of weapons, the creeping into the forest, and Humbaba's sensing their presence.
- ⚠️ Who "They" are in V about 101 (like a wild bull about to charge) is not stated.
- ⚠️ Whose rear and front are meant in V about 88 is not stated.
- ⚠️ In V about 151-153, what Humbaba lifts up ("them") is not stated; the lines before speak of his auras.
- ⚠️ In V 155, whose feet and who is whirling are not stated.
- ⚠️ The text speaks both of seven cloaks (V about 99) and of auras (V about 148-149, about 183); George's synopsis calls them seven auras.
- ⚠️ The third plea comes from a Middle Babylonian fragment found at Ugarit, not from the standard version; George says only that it fits well here (p. 43). Enkidu's third reply is wholly restored.
- ⚠️ The opening of Humbaba's curse is lost (V about 274-278).
- ⚠️ Which hero takes the tusks (V 290) is not stated.
- ⚠️ The killing of Humbaba's seven sons is not narrated in what survives; they are mentioned only as already dead (V about 307), after the gap in which, George says, the heroes defile the forest.
- ⚠️ The speaker of the imagined question about the trampled forest (V 305-about 306) is not named; Enkidu introduces it by asking how they will answer Enlil in Nippur.
- ⚠️ "Nine times," "puma," "please" (V about 171), "best timber," "They fashioned" and "bearing" are uncertain renderings.
- George prints no note at the end of Tablet V; the text stops with Gilgamesh on the raft with Humbaba's head.
- Thompson 1928 differs at several points: he places the friends' mutual encouragement and the seven garments in his Tablet IV (pp. 27-28), with Enkidu as the frightened one; he reads the mountain as the home of the immortals and a shrine of Irnini (Tablet V, p. 29); and, following the Hittite version, he has eight winds, not thirteen, and Humbaba offering himself as Gilgamesh's servant (Tablet V, p. 31).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124, "Tablet five") says Humbaba vows to disembowel Gilgamesh; George 2020 has him threaten to cut Gilgamesh's throat and gullet and feed his flesh to the birds (V about 123-124).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Gilgamesh pities Humbaba; George 2020's Tablet V gives Gilgamesh no words or stated feeling during the pleas, though Enkidu complains that Gilgamesh is not listening to him (V about 283).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Humbaba offers to make Gilgamesh king of the forest; George 2020 has him offer to stay in the forest as a living slave at Gilgamesh's command and to keep timber for his palace, while hailing him as king (V 170-about 177).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Gilgamesh kills Humbaba's seven sons; George 2020 says "they" had killed them and does not narrate the deed (V about 307).
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) has the heroes return home along the Euphrates; George 2020 names the Euphrates only as the river that will carry the door to Nippur, and the text ends with the two riding the raft, without saying where to (V about 318-323).
- Wikipedia's article captions a recently found Old Babylonian Tablet V in the Sulaymaniyah Museum; George 2020's preface credits newly published sources for the expanded forest description without naming that tablet in these pages (p. xii).

## Leads (structural observations only)
- The winds Ninsun prayed for in Tablet III (III about 87-93) arrive here in the same words and order (V about 159-165).
- After the first plea, Humbaba's pleas follow a fixed cycle: he hears Enkidu's abuse, weeps before Shamash, pleads, and ends by asking Enkidu to have Gilgamesh spare him; Enkidu answers each time with the same "finish him … before Enlil hears … win fame" speech. The cycle ends with the curse.
- Humbaba's threat to feed Gilgamesh to the eagle and vulture (V about 124) comes back as his regret that he did not do so to Enkidu (V about 193).
- "Typhoon" is the name of one of the thirteen winds (III 90, V about 161) and of one of Humbaba's seven sons (V about 308).
- The epithet "offshoot sprung from Uruk's midst" is used first by Gilgamesh of himself (V about 94) and then by Humbaba (V 170).
- The saying about the one who leads (V about 49) echoes the elders' proverb in Tablet III (III about 4-5).
- The tablet begins, and the approach later resumes, with the two standing in wonder at the forest (V 1, V about 53).
