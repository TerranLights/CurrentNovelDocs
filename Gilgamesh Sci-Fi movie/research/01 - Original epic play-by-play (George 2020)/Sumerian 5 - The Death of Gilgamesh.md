# Sumerian 5. The Death of Gilgamesh ("The great wild bull is lying down"): play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 2 ("The Sumerian Poems of Gilgamesh"), pp. 150-162. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**Line numbers.** George numbers this poem by manuscript rather than by a single traditional count. "M 5," "M 10" and so on are lines of the Mê-Turan text, which he calls "M" (p. 154). "N1" to "N4," with column and side ("N1 v 5," "N2 obv. 10," "N3 25," "N4 rev. 2´"), are other manuscripts; since George says a passage is found on tablets from Nippur (p. 154), these appear to be the Nippur tablets ⚠️ (he does not define the sigla in this section). An M number in parentheses, such as "(M 265)," gives the M equivalent of a line printed from an N tablet. A prime (´) means the count starts at the first surviving line of a broken column. George prints a number about every fifth line (the {l.…} markers in the extracted text); lines in between are counted from the nearest marker, and ranges that cannot be fixed are marked "about." Standard-version references (for example "VIII 152") come from the same markers in the SB tablets.

**Key.** "Restored" = inside George's square brackets; "conjectural" = his italic square brackets, a guess to fill the sense; "uncertain" = his italics on surviving text, a doubtful reading or rendering (Note on the Translation, p. lvi). "* * *" = a gap of more than one line (p. lvii). Indented lines are lines from a fuller manuscript that the base count leaves out (chapter convention, p. 99); they are flagged below.

**Quotations.** There is no public-domain translation of this Sumerian poem on hand, so George is quoted only for a few words where the exact phrase matters.

**George's caution (pp. 150, 122).** Several newly found manuscripts from Mê-Turan (Tell Haddad) have made the poem far better known, but parts of it remain hard to understand. He applies to it the warning he gives for "Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven," that his rendering there is provisional and not final (p. 122).

## George's synopsis (pp. 150-152, paraphrased)
- The poem opens by lamenting Gilgamesh, struck down: Namtar, Death's messenger, has hold of him, and he lies ill and delirious on his deathbed (p. 150).
- Enki, under his name Nudimmud, sends him a vision of the gods in assembly deciding his fate. They go over his life: the Forest of Cedar, his travels to the world's edge, and the old knowledge he got from Ziusudra, who survived the Deluge. Their difficulty: Gilgamesh is human, yet his mother is a goddess; is he to die or not? (p. 150)
- The verdict seems to come from Enki, whose role is solving problems. Ziusudra, he says, is the one mortal who ever won eternal life, and that was a special case (the story of Tablet XI). Gilgamesh, divine mother or not, must go down to the Netherworld like everyone else, but there he will rank as chief of the dead and judge them, as Ningishzida and Dumuzi do (p. 150).
- He will also be remembered on earth at a yearly Festival of Lights, where young men wrestle, as Gilgamesh and Enkidu wrestle in the Babylonian epic. Other sources put the festival in the year's fifth month (about August), with torches and braziers lit, and call it Gilgamesh's month, with young men wrestling and testing their strength in their doorways on the ninth day (pp. 150-151).
- Enlil then puts the dream's message more plainly: Gilgamesh was destined to be king but not to escape death. He should not despair, since below he will rejoin his family and his beloved Enkidu and count among the lesser gods (p. 151).
- Gilgamesh wakes up shaken. The text is broken here, but he apparently asks for advice. The whole dream is then repeated, most simply because he is telling it to his advisers, although the repetition never shifts from third to first person. They tell him not to grieve: even kings must die, and he should take comfort in his high rank after death (p. 151).
- After a gap, Enki prompts Gilgamesh to build his tomb. The break hides how Enki got the message to him, but Gilgamesh's son Urlugal seems to have had a part. The message answered where to put the tomb so that it could never be broken into: Gilgamesh's laborers turn the Euphrates aside and build a stone tomb in its bed (p. 151).
- His wives and household take up places in the tomb to go with him into the afterlife; George compares the group burials of whole elite households that Sir Leonard Woolley found in the 1920s in the "Royal Cemetery" at Ur, of the early third millennium. Gilgamesh gives gifts to the gods of Ereshkigal's court, as he does for Enkidu in Tablet VIII, and lies down. The door is closed with a great stone made for it, and the river is let back into its bed so the tomb cannot be found. Uruk mourns its king (pp. 151-152).
- Two endings survive. The worse-preserved one simply praises Gilgamesh as the greatest king. The other teaches that the dead live on in the memory of the living: statues set up in temples keep their names spoken and serve their funerary cult, and the gods have made men have families so that their line goes on (p. 152).

## Scenes

### S5.1. Lament: the great wild bull is lying down (M 1-19, p. 152)
**Where and when:** Gilgamesh's deathbed; the place is not named (Uruk is implied by his title "lord of Kullab"). The poem's opening.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, lying ill; Namtar, the power that holds him. Only the narrator's voice.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Ten lines each end with the refrain that he is lying down and will never get up again; each names Gilgamesh by a title or deed: the great wild bull; lord Gilgamesh; the one perfect in battle; the warrior with a shoulder-belt; the one perfect in strength; the one who brought the wicked low; the one who spoke wisely; the land's watchful one; the one who climbed the mountains; the lord of Kullab (M 1-10).
2. He lies on his deathbed, a bed of misery, never to rise (M 11-12).
3. He cannot stand or sit, eat or drink; he can only groan (M 13-14).
4. Namtar's lock holds him; he cannot get up (M 15).
5. He is like a fish hauled up from a pond in a net, like a gazelle trapped in a snare (M 16-17).
6. Namtar, without hands or feet, who carries men off at night; Namtar, who gores … (M 18-19).
7. A gap follows ("* * *").

**Specifics:** the refrain occurs twelve times (M 1-12). Titles: great wild bull, lord, warrior with shoulder-belt, watchful one of the land, lord of Kullab. Bed: deathbed, bed of woe. Images: a fish in a net, a gazelle in a snare. Namtar: a "lock," no hands, no feet, strikes by night, gores.

**Short quotations:**
- "The great wild bull is lying down" (M 1): the poem's ancient title.

**Gaps:** "in combat" (M 3) is in italics (uncertain). M 16-19 are damaged: words lost in M 16, and conjectural restorations for "net," "held fast," "snatches" and the clause about Namtar gripping Gilgamesh (M 16-19). A gap follows M 19 (about M 20-39).

**Repetitions:** the lying-down refrain (M 1-12). Namtar's lack of hands and feet comes back in Gilgamesh's speech (M 137, S5.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, which never narrates Gilgamesh's death. Parallels in wording:
- **Refrain:** after Enkidu's death Gilgamesh asks whether he too will lie down like him and never rise again, for all time (X 247-248, p. 82).
- **Epithets:** the prologue calls him a wild bull on the rampage, Lugalbanda's wild bull, perfect in strength, and the one who opened mountain passes (I 30, 35, 38, p. 2).
- **Who seizes:** here Namtar holds Gilgamesh. In the epic Namtar is the Netherworld's vizier, who receives gifts for Enkidu (VIII 152-156, p. 66), and the captor in Enkidu's death-dream is an unnamed man with Lion-Bird features (VII 168-184, pp. 58-59).
- **Names:** the epic never uses "lord of Kullab"; it calls the city Uruk-the-Sheepfold.

### S5.2. Six days sick; sleep and the dream begins (about M 40-48 = N2 obv. 4-10, pp. 152-153)
**Where and when:** the deathbed, called Namtar's bed. Gilgamesh has been ill six days. The text resumes after the gap.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Uruk and Kullab (named in broken lines); the god Nudimmud (Enki). No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Six days he lies ill, sweating like melting fat (N2 obv. 4-5).
2. Lord Gilgamesh lies ill; broken lines mention Uruk and Kullab and the talk of the land (about M 42-44).
3. The young lord falls asleep on Namtar's bed and dreams (M 45-47).
4. In the dream, the god Nudimmud opens his eyes (M 48).

**Specifics:** six days of illness. Sweat compared to melting fat (George's parenthesis "(melting)"). Namtar's bed. Nudimmud means "Man-fashioner," a name of Ea (Enki) that refers to his part in making mankind (Glossary, p. 224).

**Short quotations:**
- "opened his eyes" (M 48): the dream's opening, said again in the first person at M 139.

**Gaps:** every line is damaged. Restored: the verbs of lying and sweating, Gilgamesh's name, the phrase about lying on Namtar's bed, and the whole of Nudimmud's eye-opening. Conjectural: that he had a dream (M 47). Lost: the ends of the lines about Uruk and Kullab and the land's talk, and parts of M 45-47.

**Repetitions:** the dream's opening (M 47-48) is repeated by Gilgamesh at M 138-139 (S5.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart for Gilgamesh's own illness. Nearest: Enkidu's last illness, counted day by day up to the twelfth day (VII 254-260, pp. 60-61), which also follows a dream (VII 165-208). Here the illness lasts six days and the dream comes during it. Numbers: six days against twelve. The pairing six days and seven nights also appears in the epic for Gilgamesh's weeping over Enkidu (X 235, p. 82) and for Uta-napishti's sleep test (XI 209, p. 93).

### S5.3. The dream, part 1: the gods' assembly reviews Gilgamesh's deeds (M 49-68, p. 153)
**Where and when:** in the dream, at the gods' assembly, their place of ceremony.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (approaching); the assembled gods; Enlil, Enki and An named. The gods speak as a group.

**Play-by-play (dreamed):**
1. Gilgamesh comes forward in the gods' assembly (M 49-50).
2. The gods speak about him: he went down every road; he brought the one special cedar down from its mountain; he killed Huwawa in his forest; he set up monuments for later ages; he founded temples; he reached Ziusudra where he lives (M 51-57).
3. They go on: he brought back to the land Sumer's rites and customs, long forgotten, and set in order the rites of washing hands and mouth, after the Deluge had drowned every land's settlements (M 58-61).
4. A gap ("* * *"), about M 62-64.
5. Broken: "having brought …"; for that reason, they say, Gilgamesh should not be taken (M 65-66).
6. Enlil's will is reported to Enki; Enki answers An and Enlil (M 67-68).

**Specifics:** the deeds: every road, the unique cedar, Huwawa killed in his forest, monuments, temples, Ziusudra in his home, Sumer's forgotten rites and customs, hand- and mouth-washing rites, the Deluge that drowned all lands' settlements. Ziusudra ("Life of Distant Days") is the Sumerian name of Uta-napishti (Glossary, p. 224).

**Short quotations:**
- "you reached Ziusudra in his abode!" (M 57)

**Gaps:** restored: "the gods'," "the lord," "drawn nigh" (M 49-50), "on his account" (M 51), "after the" (M 61), "now Gilgamesh" (M 66), and all the names in M 67-68. In italics (uncertain): "on that account" (M 66), "spoke" (M 67). Lost: about three lines after M 61 and the end of M 65. George's synopsis puts the gods' problem as Gilgamesh's being human but a goddess's son (p. 150); in the surviving lines only Enki states it (S5.4).

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh repeats M 49-68 in his retelling at M 140-159 (S5.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the prologue's list of Gilgamesh's deeds (I 37-44, p. 2); George says this passage bears out the prologue's hint that Gilgamesh restored the order lost in the Flood (Introduction, pp. li-lii).
- **Events:** both lists include the long journey, reaching the Flood survivor, and restoring what the Deluge ruined; the epic says he rebuilt the cult centers the Deluge destroyed and established the rites for the people (I 43-44). Here the rites are named: washing of hands and mouth. The cedar and Huwawa (Tablets III-V) are on this list; the epic's prologue features the wall of Uruk instead (I 11-23), which this list omits.
- **Names:** Ziusudra for Uta-napishti; Huwawa for Humbaba; An for Anu.
- **Who:** here the gods recite the deeds in council; in the epic the narrator does, in the prologue.

### S5.4. The dream, part 2: Enki's verdict; Gilgamesh to govern the dead; the festival of wrestling and light (M 69-84, then 8 restored lines, pp. 153-154)
**Where and when:** the gods' assembly, in the dream, continuing S5.3.
**Who:** Enki, speaking to An and Enlil about Gilgamesh. The restored lines after M 84 continue the verdict; George's synopsis gives the final judgment to Enki (p. 150).

**Play-by-play (dreamed):**
1. Enki → An and Enlil: in the far-off days, nights and years, after the assembly had sent the Deluge to wipe out human seed, one man of all mankind was still alive among the gods: Ziusudra (M 69-75).
2. Enki: ever since, the gods have sworn by heaven's life and earth's life that humans shall not live forever (M 76-77).
3. Enki: now they consider Gilgamesh, and his mother is no reason to spare him (M 78-79).
4. Enki (the verdict): once dead, Gilgamesh as a ghost will govern the Netherworld and lead the dead; he will judge and decide cases, and his word will carry the weight of Ningishzida's and Dumuzi's (M 80-83).
5. "Then the young lord, lord Gilgamesh …" (M 84). Manuscript M breaks down here.
6. George's note: the M text becomes confused and stops a few lines later; a slightly different form of the passage (strictly, of its repetition from M 174 on) is on Nippur tablets and is used to fill the gap (p. 154).
7. Restored (all in brackets): Sissig, the Dream God and Utu's son, will give Gilgamesh light in the dark Netherworld. In future, when statues are made for named men, young warriors and spectators will stand in a half-circle around a doorway and hold wrestling bouts and contests of strength in front of it. In the Month of Torches, the ghosts' festival, no light will be set before them unless he is there.

**Specifics:** the oath by heaven's life and earth's life (M 76). The single survivor: Ziusudra (M 75). Gilgamesh's titles after death: governor of the Netherworld, chief of the dead (M 81). His models: Ningishzida ("Lord of the True Tree," the Netherworld's chamberlain) and Dumuzi (Glossary, pp. 221, 223). Sissig, Dream God, son of Utu. Funerary statues; a half-circle around a doorway; wrestling and trials of strength; the Month of Torches, festival of ghosts. George places the festival in the fifth month (about August) and cites a source on young men wrestling in doorways on the ninth day (p. 151).

**Short quotations:**
- "mankind should not have life eternal" (M 77)
- "governor of the Netherworld" (M 81)

**Gaps:** M 69-84 survive whole except the end of M 84. The eight lines after George's note are entirely restored from the Nippur retelling (compare M 180-187 in S5.7, where most of them survive).

**Book illustration:** drawing 17 stands here, captioned with the wrestling line. The list of illustrations identifies it as a limestone plaque showing wrestlers, mid-third millennium BC, from Tutub (Iraq Museum, Baghdad).

**Repetitions:** M 69-71 repeat the triple opening formula of "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" (its line 1). M 69-84 recur at M 160-174, and the restored lines at M 180-187 (S5.7). The governor-and-judge lines come back in Enlil's speech (M 121-125) and in the counsellors' reply (N1 vi 19-22).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the Deluge and its outcome in Tablets X-XI, and Ninsun's hint in Tablet III.
- **Events:** the epic tells the Flood at length in Uta-napishti's words (XI 11-205, pp. 86-92) and ends it with Enlil making Uta-napishti and his wife godlike (XI 203-205). It gives the general law through Uta-napishti: the Anunnaki in assembly, with Mammitum, set both Death and Life but keep the day of death hidden (X 319-322, p. 85). George connects this passage directly to that one and to Atram-hasis (Introduction, pp. xlvi-xlvii). Here Enki presents it as an oath sworn after the Deluge.
- **Who speaks / outcome:** the epic gives no divine verdict on Gilgamesh's own death; Uta-napishti asks who will call the gods together for him (XI 207, p. 93). His rank after death is only hinted at, when Ninsun asks Shamash whether Gilgamesh will not dwell with Ningishzida in the land of no return (III 106, p. 25; George, Introduction pp. lii-liii).
- **Divine parentage:** here his mother is no ground for mercy; the epic calls him two-thirds god and one-third human (I 48, p. 2) and a blend of divine and human flesh (X 268, p. 83).
- **Names:** Ziusudra for Uta-napishti; Enki for Ea; An for Anu. Sissig and the Month of Torches have no counterpart in the epic.
- **Wrestling:** George connects the festival wrestling with Gilgamesh and Enkidu's fight (p. 151); in the epic they grapple at the door of the wedding house in the Square of the Land (II 111-115, p. 16).

### S5.5. The dream, part 3: Enlil speaks to Gilgamesh (16 restored lines, then M 103-125, pp. 155-156)
**Where and when:** the dream, continuing S5.4.
**Who:** Enlil, called Great Mountain and father of the gods, speaking to Gilgamesh. Enlil speaks.

**Play-by-play (dreamed; the first 16 lines are wholly restored from the Nippur retelling):**
1. Enlil talks with Gilgamesh in the dream.
2. Enlil → Gilgamesh: he gave Gilgamesh kingship as his destiny, not life without end. Whatever life humans get, Gilgamesh should not be heartsick, despairing or crushed.
3. Enlil: mankind's curse has arrived, as he told him; what was decided when his umbilical cord was cut has arrived.
4. Enlil: six things have overtaken him: mortals' darkest day; mortals' lonely place; a flood-wave no one can stand against; a battle no one can escape; a fight with no equal; a combat without mercy.
5. Enlil: he must not enter the Great City with a heart tied in knots of anger; he should loosen it before Utu, untwisting it like palm fiber and peeling it like an onion.
6. Enlil: he is to go on, when the great Anunna gods take their seats at the funeral feast, to where the dead priests and priestesses lie: en, lagar, lumaḫ, nindingir, "the true one," guda and linen-clad priests (M 103-107).
7. Enlil: there are his father and grandfathers, mother, sisters and siblings, his dear friend, his little brother, and Enkidu, his young companion (M 108-111).
8. Enlil: in the Great City live governors and kings, and army chiefs and troop captains lie there; in the Great City, Aralli, those who go in never come out (about M 112-116).
9. Enlil: his sister, his sibling, his own dear ones and his city's elders will come to him from their houses (about M 117-120).
10. Enlil: he should not despair; he will count among the Anunna gods, ranking just below the great gods; he will govern the Netherworld, judge and decide cases, and his word will weigh as much as Ningishzida's and Dumuzi's (about M 120-125).

**Specifics:** Enlil's titles: Great Mountain, father of the gods. Kingship versus eternal life. The umbilical cord. Six things that overtook him (darkest day, lonely place, flood-wave, battle, combat, fight). Utu, before whom anger is undone; images of palm fiber and an onion. The Anunna gods' funeral banquet. Priestly titles: en, lagar, lumaḫ, nindingir, guda, the linen-clad, "the true one." Family: father, grandfathers, mother, sisters, siblings, little brother, Enkidu. Names for the Netherworld: the Great City, Aralli (Glossary: Aralli is a name of the Netherworld, p. 221; Irkalla, another such name, means "Great City," p. 223). The Anunna (Anunnaki): in later times the gods of the Netherworld (Glossary, p. 221).

**Short quotations:**
- "a destiny of kingship" (restored here; preserved at about N1 v 14)
- "your friend Enkidu" (M 111)

**Gaps:** the first 16 lines are wholly restored. In M 103-125, restored: the start of the banquet line (M 103), the end of the lagar line (M 104), all of the lines on governors, chiefs, captains, Aralli and the sister's and sibling's houses except the word "house" (M 112-118), the second half of M 119, and parts of M 125. In italics (uncertain): "conversed," "whatever life it has," "thus come" (twice), "siblings," "sibling's." The priestly titles are printed in italics as Sumerian terms.

**Repetitions:** the call not to despair twice; "has caught up with you" six times; "will come to you" several times. The whole speech comes again in the retelling (S5.7, about M 188-216), where the first 16 lines survive.

**Compared with the standard version:** partial parallels only.
- **The dead:** Enkidu's dream lists the dead in the House of Dust: crowned kings who once served Anu and Enlil; en- and lagar-priests, lustration- and lumahhu-priests, the great gods' gudapsû-priests; Etana, Shakkan, Ereshkigal and Belet-ṣeri (VII 194-205, p. 60). The priest lists overlap (en, lagar, lumaḫ/lumahhu, guda/gudapsû). Difference: in the epic the list is part of a frightening vision; here it reassures, with family and Enkidu waiting.
- **Kingship as destiny:** in the Old Babylonian Pennsylvania lines that George prints in the Tablet II gap, Enkidu tells Gilgamesh that Enlil destined him to be king of the people (P 239-240, p. 16). The epic's statement that death cannot be escaped comes from Uta-napishti (X 301-322, pp. 84-85), not from Enlil.
- **Who speaks:** Enlil comforts Gilgamesh here; in the epic Enlil sentences Enkidu to death (Hittite paraphrase, p. 54) and causes the Deluge (XI 172-176).
- **Reunion with Enkidu after death:** no counterpart.

### S5.6. Gilgamesh wakes (M 126-130, p. 156)
**Where and when:** the deathbed, on waking.
**Who:** the young lord Gilgamesh. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The young lord wakes: it was a dream; he shudders; it was a deep sleep (M 126-127).
2. He rubs his eyes; all is desolate silence (M 128).
3. Two lines begin "The dream …"; the rest of both is lost (M 129-130).

**Specifics:** a shudder, rubbing his eyes, a desolate silence.

**Short quotations:** none.

**Gaps:** restored: "lord" (M 126), "dream" and the deep-sleep clause (M 127), the verb of rubbing and "silence" (M 128). M 129-130 are nearly all lost. George's synopsis: the text is damaged here, but Gilgamesh seems to seek advice (p. 151).

**Repetitions:** none.

**Compared with the standard version:** waking from dreams recurs in the epic: on the road to the Forest of Cedar, Gilgamesh wakes and asks Enkidu whether he touched him and why he is startled (IV 96-97, p. 32; the same lines recur, mostly restored, after each of his Tablet IV dreams), and during his wanderings he lies down and wakes from a dream (IX 13, p. 69). Difference: those dreams come before a fight or a journey; this one comes on his deathbed and is about his own death.

### S5.7. Gilgamesh takes counsel and retells the dream (about M 131 to M 216 / N1 vi 1, pp. 156-159)
**Where and when:** after waking; place not given. The listeners are unnamed in the surviving lines (he consults "……"); in S5.8 they are restored, uncertainly, as counsellors.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, with the titles lord of Kullab, hero of the Shining Mountain, and smith of the great gods, speaking to his advisers. Gilgamesh speaks; his retelling quotes the gods, Enki and Enlil.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh, named with his titles (three indented lines from manuscript N4), consults someone, name lost (about M 131-132).
2. Gilgamesh (oath): he swears by the life of his mother Ninsun, of his father, pure Lugalbanda, and of his god Enki, lord Nudimmud (about M 133, with two indented lines).
3. Gilgamesh: asks whether he should act like a frightened child on Ninsun's knees (M 135); a broken line mentions a great mountain (M 136); Namtar, with no hands or feet, spares no one (M 137).
4. Gilgamesh: he had a dream, in which Nudimmud opened his eyes (M 138-139).
5. Gilgamesh retells the dream (M 140-216): the gods' review of his deeds (M 140-152, = S5.3); the gap; Enki's answer to An and Enlil about the Deluge, Ziusudra, the oath against eternal life and the verdict that Gilgamesh will govern the dead (M 153-174, = S5.4); a gap (about M 175-179); Sissig's light and the wrestling festival in the Month of Torches (M 180-187); Enlil's speech on kingship, what has overtaken him, the waiting priests and family, and his place among the Anunna (about M 188-216, = S5.5).

**Specifics:** new titles: hero of the Shining Mountain, smith of the great gods (N4 lines). The oath by Ninsun, Lugalbanda ("the pure") and Enki-Nudimmud. The picture of fear on his mother's knees. Namtar spares no one (M 137; at M 18 he takes men at night). Differences within the retelling: the dream starts with "my eyes" but then stays in the third person (George notes the missing switch, p. 151); the guda-priests come before the nindingir-priestesses and "the true one"; the army chiefs and troop captains lie "all alone"; three extra indented lines say someone "will come to you."

**Short quotations:**
- "on the knees of [Ninsun]" (M 135)

**Gaps:** M 131-139: much is restored (the opening titles, "lord of Uruk," the verb "took counsel," the descriptions of mother, father and god in the oath, Ninsun's name in M 135, the verb in M 137), and words are lost in M 136 and M 138 (conjectural: "I had a dream"). In italics (uncertain): "lord" of Uruk, and "my" in M 139. In the retelling: a gap after the deeds (as in S5.3) and another of about five lines before Sissig (M 175-179). The retelling needs less restoration than the first telling; the Sissig, festival and Enlil lines survive mostly whole here (N1 v 4 to vi 1), and George used them to fill S5.4-S5.5 (p. 154).

**Manuscript note:** indented lines (from a fuller manuscript): the three title lines (one of them at N4 obv. 10´), the oath's lines on Lugalbanda and on Enki-Nudimmud, and the three "will come to you" lines.

**Repetitions:** M 140-174 repeat M 49-84 word for word (apart from the person shift); M 180-216 repeat the restored and surviving lines of S5.4-S5.5. George calls it a complete repetition of the dream, most simply explained as Gilgamesh telling it to those he asks for advice (p. 151).

**Compared with the standard version:** telling one's dreams to a companion is a standard-version pattern: Gilgamesh tells his mother his dreams of Enkidu's coming (I 245-298, pp. 9-11, inside Shamhat's speech) and tells Enkidu his dreams on the road (Tablet IV, pp. 28-35); Enkidu tells Gilgamesh his dreams of the gods' council and the House of Dust (Tablet VII, pp. 54, 58-60).
- **Who speaks / listener:** here Gilgamesh dreams of his own death and tells counsellors (uncertain). In Tablet VII it is Enkidu who dreams of his own death and tells Gilgamesh.
- **Names:** Lugalbanda is Gilgamesh's "father" here. In the epic he is Gilgamesh's god: Shamash reminds Gilgamesh of "your god Lugalbanda" (V 141, p. 41), and Gilgamesh dedicates the Bull's horns to him (VI 165, p. 53); the prologue calls Gilgamesh Lugalbanda's wild bull (I 35, p. 2). George's Glossary: one tradition makes Lugalbanda Gilgamesh's father, another his guardian god (p. 223).

### S5.8. The counsellors answer: death spares no one; his destiny is unique (N1 vi 2-about 22, p. 159)
**Where and when:** right after the retelling.
**Who:** the advisers to whom Gilgamesh told the dream (restored and uncertain as "the counsellors"); Gilgamesh listens. The counsellors speak.

**Play-by-play:**
1. When Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab, has finished telling the dream, his listeners answer (N1 vi 2-5).
2. Counsellors → Gilgamesh: why is he weeping? For what reason …? (N1 vi 6-7).
3. Counsellors: no mother goddess has yet borne a man whom death will not take; since human seed first appeared, no such person has existed (N1 vi 8-10).
4. Counsellors, with images of capture: even a wrestler may be trapped in a throwing-net; a bird once netted does not get away; a deep-sea fish stops seeing the rushes once the young fisherman throws his net and it is caught (N1 vi 11-14).
5. Counsellors: no man of any kind climbs back out of the Netherworld; who has ever seen it? (N1 vi 15-16).
6. Counsellors: there will never be another king with a fate like his; among all men who bear names, none shares it (N1 vi 17-19).
7. Counsellors: he is to govern the Netherworld; his ghost will count among the Anunna gods; he will judge and decide, his word weighing as much as Ningishzida's and Dumuzi's (about N1 vi 19-22).
8. A gap ("* * *").

**Specifics:** the Mother Goddess, who bears every man. Images: a wrestler in a throwing-net; a bird fenced in by a net; a fish and the rushes; a young fisherman's net. All named men. The Anunna gods; Ningishzida and Dumuzi.

**Short quotations:**
- "Another king there will never be" (N1 vi 17, about)

**Gaps:** restored: most of the framing lines (N1 vi 2-5, including the word for the counsellors, itself uncertain), the opening question to Gilgamesh (N1 vi 6), the openings of N1 vi 8-9 (conjectural), "is the same" (N1 vi 19), and the key words of the last three lines (the governorship, the Anunna, the verdicts, all of the Ningishzida line). Words are lost in N1 vi 7, 9, 10, 11, 13, 15, 19. In italics (uncertain): "throw" (net), "fenced in," "rushes." Even the identity of the speakers is uncertain.

**Repetitions:** the governor-and-judge lines repeat Enki's verdict (M 80-83) and Enlil's promise (M 121-125). The phrase about all men who are given names recurs in the festival lines (S5.4) and the didactic ending (S5.15).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Uta-napishti's discourse on death (X 301-322, pp. 84-85) and Gilgamesh's despair after the sleep test (XI 244-246).
- **Events / images:** Uta-napishti says man is broken off like a reed; no one sees Death's face or hears his voice; the mayfly floats and suddenly is gone; the gods fixed Death and Life but keep the day hidden (X 301-322). Here the images are of capture (nets, snares), and the point is that nobody comes back up from the Netherworld. In the epic Gilgamesh uses a capture image himself: a thief has seized his flesh, and Death waits wherever he turns (XI 244-246, p. 94).
- **Who speaks:** one immortal (Uta-napishti) in the epic; a group of advisers (uncertain) here.
- **Outcome:** the epic offers no comfort of rank after death; here the advisers point to his unique destiny as ruler of the dead.

### S5.9. The tomb is designed; Enki reveals the dream's solution; Urlugal solves it (M 235-238, p. 160)
**Where and when:** after a gap; presumably Uruk (not stated). Time since S5.8 not given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh's architect; an unnamed "they," who are confused; the god Enki; Urlugal. No direct speech survives.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh's architect draws up his tomb, in a way now lost (M 235).
2. Because "they" are confused, Enki shows them how to read the dream (M 236-237).
3. Urlugal, and nobody else, solves the vision (M 238).

**Specifics:** the architect; the tomb; Urlugal, Gilgamesh's son (Glossary, p. 224). Who "they" are is not said (the counsellors of S5.8 are the nearest candidates).

**Short quotations:**
- "Urlugal solved it" (M 238)

**Gaps:** a gap comes before M 235 (from about N1 vi 23 to M 234); a word is lost in M 235. George: the break hides just how Enki got his message to Gilgamesh, but Urlugal seems to have had a part, and the message apparently settled where to put the tomb so that it could not be violated (p. 151).

**Repetitions:** none.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, where Gilgamesh has no named son and no tomb. Nearest: in Tablet VIII someone other than Gilgamesh speaks after a gap, mentioning a judge of the Anunnaki, and then Gilgamesh, hearing this, thinks of damming the river (VIII 208-212, p. 68), during Enkidu's funeral; George links Enkidu's funeral with this poem's (pp. 98-99, 151).

### S5.10. The Euphrates is drained and the stone tomb built in its bed (M 239-261, p. 160)
**Where and when:** Uruk and Kullab, then the Euphrates riverbed. The work takes less than half a month, not even five or ten days.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (the lord); a herald; the labor gangs of Uruk and Kullab. The herald speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The lord calls up workers in his city; a herald blows his horn across the lands (M 239-240).
2. Herald → Uruk and Kullab: they must rise up, cut through the Euphrates and drain it (M 241-242).
3. Uruk's gang comes like a Deluge, Kullab's like a dense fog (M 243-244).
4. Within half a month, not five days, not ten, they cut the river and drain it; its pebbles look up at the Sun God in surprise; the dry riverbed cracks (M 245-249).
5. He builds the tomb of stone: stone walls, stone doors at the entrance, bar, threshold and bolts of the hardest diorite, beams of cast gold (M 250-255).
6. He moves a heavy stone block into place (M 256; an indented line repeats the heavy block); he lays out things of every kind, now lost (M 257).
7. So that no one in future would discover it: anyone who searched would never find where it was (M 258-260).
8. For the young lord Gilgamesh (indented line), a secure room is set up in the middle of Uruk (M 261).

**Specifics:** the herald's horn; Uruk and Kullab; the work gangs compared to a Deluge and to fog. Time: under half a month ("not five days ... not ten days"). The pebbles and the Sun God. Materials: stone for tomb, walls and doors; hardest diorite for bar, threshold and bolts; gold for the beams; a heavy stone block. Aim: the site never to be found. A secure chamber in the middle of Uruk.

**Short quotations:**
- "O Uruk, arise!" (M 241)

**Gaps:** in italics (uncertain): "not even" (M 245), "chamber" (M 261). Words lost in M 256-260 (the items laid out, who would fail to find it). Restored: the purpose words in M 258-259 and the indented line's opening.

**Manuscript note:** two indented lines (the second heavy-block line, and the line naming the young lord Gilgamesh).

**Repetitions:** the calls to Uruk and Kullab are parallel; "hardest diorite" twice; the heavy block of stone twice.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart for Gilgamesh's tomb. Nearest:
- **Damming the river:** in Enkidu's funeral, after an unknown speaker, Gilgamesh thinks of damming the river (VIII 211-212, p. 68; "damming" is uncertain); the rest is lost. George says the Akkadian account of Enkidu's funeral recalls this poem's funeral of Gilgamesh (pp. 98-99).
- **Numbers / speed:** the epic's building feats are Uruk's wall (I 11-23, p. 2) and Uta-napishti's boat, whose hull is set up by the fifth day (XI 57, p. 87); here the river is drained in under half a month.
- **Workforce:** the nearest scene is the whole land gathering with tools at Atra-hasis's gate to build the boat (XI 48-56, p. 87). The epic never shows Gilgamesh calling up labor; George mentions forced labor only as one theory of his tyranny (Introduction, p. xlix).

### S5.11. The household is laid in the tomb (N3 1-7, about M 262-268, p. 160)
**Where and when:** inside the tomb in the riverbed, in the middle of Uruk.
**Who:** Gilgamesh's household: wife, child, senior and junior wives, minstrel, steward, barber, attendants, servants. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. His beloved wife and child, his beloved senior and junior wives, his beloved minstrel and steward and someone else, his beloved barber and someone else, his beloved attendants and servants, and his beloved possessions (N3 1-6).
2. All are laid in their places as if lined up for a palace inspection in the middle of Uruk (N3 7).

**Specifics:** each member is called "beloved." Members: wife; child; senior wife; junior wife; minstrel; steward; barber; attendants; servants; goods. George connects the passage with the group burials of entire elite households that Leonard Woolley excavated in the 1920s in the "Royal Cemetery" at Ur, early third millennium (p. 151); his synopsis calls them the royal harem and entourage, ready to accompany the king into the afterlife (p. 151).

**Short quotations:**
- "a palace-review" (N3 7)

**Gaps:** two household members are lost (after "steward" and after "barber"), and a word after "goods." Restored: "his beloved" (N3 4-5), "his" (N3 6), "attending" (N3 7). Whether the household is alive when laid down is not said.

**Repetitions:** "his beloved" opens each item.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. There Gilgamesh has no wife, child or entourage; Ishtar proposes marriage and he refuses (VI 7-79, pp. 47-49). His minstrel has no counterpart in the epic (in "Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven" the minstrel is Lugalgabangal, Glossary p. 223).

### S5.12. Gifts for the gods of the Netherworld; Gilgamesh lies down (N3 8-31, about M 267-290, p. 161)
**Where and when:** the tomb, right after S5.11.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, son of the goddess Ninsun; the gods who receive gifts are named. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh lays out audience-gifts for Ereshkigal, presents for Namtar, surprises for Dimpikug, and gifts for Bitti and for Ningishzida and Dumuzi (N3 8-13).
2. He lays out gifts for Enlil's dead forebears: the pairs Enki and Ninki, Enmul and Ninmul, Endukuga and Nindukuga, Endashurimma and Nindashurimma, and Enutila and Enmesharra, Enlil's mothers and fathers (N3 14-18).
3. For Shulpae (lord of the table), for Shakkan with Ninhursanga, and for the Anunna and Igigi gods, both groups called gods of the Holy Mound (N3 19-22).
4. For the dead priests: en, lagar, lumaḫ, nindingir, guda, linen-clad and others (N3 23-25).
5. He lays out further gifts and presents, objects now lost (N3 26-28).
6. He lies down on something covered with something (both lost) (N3 29).
7. Gilgamesh, son of Ninsun, pours a water offering at that spot (N3 30-31). A gap follows.

**Specifics:** recipients in order: Ereshkigal; Namtar; Dimpikug (a Netherworld deity); Bitti ("He opens!," the Netherworld's gatekeeper); Ningishzida and Dumuzi; Enlil's ancestors living in the Netherworld (Enki and Ninki, Enmul and Ninmul, Endukuga and Nindukuga, Endashurimma and Nindashurimma, Enutila, and Enmesharra, Enlil's uncle); Shulpae ("Manifest Hero," the Mother Goddess's husband); Shakkan (god of gazelles and wild animals) and Ninhursanga ("Lady of the Uplands," the Mother Goddess); the Anunna and Igigi gods; the dead priests (Glossary, pp. 221-224). Kinds of gift: audience-gifts, presents, surprises, gifts. A water offering (George's parenthesis). Note: this Enki is one of Enlil's ancestors, not the god of Eridu (Glossary, p. 222).

**Short quotations:**
- "audience-gifts for Ereshkigal" (N3 9)

**Gaps:** words lost in N3 25-29 (the last priests, what the gifts were, what he lay on and what covered it). Restored: "for the dead" (N3 23-25), "out" (N3 28). The gifts are "their" gifts; George's synopsis reads them as meant to win a kind welcome for Gilgamesh and his household (p. 151). The text does not narrate his death; George's synopsis says only that he lies down (p. 151).

**Repetitions:** the laying-out formula five times (N3 9-13); the list of dead priests repeats Enlil's list in the dream (M 104-107), with "dead" added.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the grave gifts for Enkidu in Tablet VIII (VIII 92-188, pp. 64-67), as George notes (p. 151).
- **Who:** there Gilgamesh gives gifts on his friend's behalf; here he gives them for himself and his household before lying down in his own tomb.
- **Recipients:** in both: Ereshkigal, Namtar, Dumuzi. Only in the epic: Ishtar; Belet-ili, Enlil's older sister; Hushbisha; Qassu-ṭabat; Ninshuluhhatumma; Bibbu; Dumuzi-abzu (VIII 134-183). Only here: Dimpikug, Bitti, Ningishzida, Enlil's ancestors, Shulpae, Shakkan, Ninhursanga, the Anunna and Igigi gods, the dead priests. (Shakkan does appear in the epic, among the dead in Enkidu's dream, VII 202, p. 60.)
- **Objects:** the epic names every gift (a mahogany throwstick for Ishtar, a lapis lazuli flask for Ereshkigal, a carnelian flute for Dumuzi, a lapis chair and staff for Namtar, and so on, VIII 135-183) and shows each to the Sun God with a formula asking the god to welcome Enkidu and walk beside him. Here the gifts are not described, and there is no Sun God and no formula.
- **Water:** the epic's last surviving funeral lines set out an elammaku-wood table, a carnelian bowl of syrup and a lapis bowl of ghee at dawn (VIII 213-218, p. 68). Here Gilgamesh pours water.

### S5.13. The tomb is sealed and the river returned (N1 viii 2-5, p. 161)
**Where and when:** the tomb in the riverbed, after a gap.
**Who:** an unnamed "they." No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. They carry something (lost) into the tomb and close up its door (N1 viii 2).
2. They let the Euphrates back in; its water floods over, and the water hides his resting place (N1 viii 3-5).

**Specifics:** the sealed door; the Euphrates reopened; the tomb hidden under water. George's synopsis: the door is closed with a large stone made for the job, and the river is put back in its bed so no one can find the tomb (pp. 151-152). In the surviving lines that stone is the heavy block of S5.10.

**Short quotations:**
- "the waters removed (from view)" (N1 viii 5)

**Gaps:** a gap precedes N1 viii 2. Lost: what they carried in. "Sealed" is restored; "resting place" is conjectural. Who "they" are is not said. The surviving text does not narrate Gilgamesh's death.

**Repetitions:** none.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, which narrates neither Gilgamesh's death nor his burial. Nearest: the uncertain damming of the river at Enkidu's funeral (VIII 212, p. 68), whose purpose is lost.

### S5.14. Uruk mourns (about M 290-297, p. 161)
**Where and when:** Gilgamesh's city (Uruk), after the tomb is hidden.
**Who:** the city's young men, young women, old men and old women; his people. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. For the young lord Gilgamesh, the city's young men claw their faces and the young women pull out their hair (about M 290-292).
2. The city's old men and women roll in the dust; a line about what they did not put on is broken (about M 293-294).
3. When the news is announced, his people roll in the dust (M 295).
4. For the young lord, spirits sink and hearts are struck (M 296-297).

**Specifics:** grief by group: young men, clawing their faces; young women, tearing hair; old men and women, rolling in the dust; the whole people, rolling in the dust.

**Short quotations:**
- "the heart was stricken" (about M 297)

**Gaps:** the opening of the first line and "lord" are restored (about M 290, 296); the end of about M 294 is lost.

**Repetitions:** the phrase introducing the young lord Gilgamesh twice; rolling in the dust twice. The despair line recalls the Mê-Turan ending of "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld," where the king's heart is struck and his mind despairs (p. 144).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the mourning for Enkidu (VII 144-147, p. 58; VIII 1-64, pp. 61-64).
- **Who mourns:** for Enkidu, Gilgamesh calls on the elders, the young men, ploughman, herdsman, shepherd boy, brewer, harlot and the wild itself to grieve (VIII 9-45), and he tears out his own hair and throws off his finery (VIII 63-64). Here the whole city mourns Gilgamesh, age group by age group.
- **Outcome:** Shamash predicts that Gilgamesh will have Uruk's people mourn Enkidu (VII 144-145); here the people mourn Gilgamesh.

### S5.15. First ending (the Mê-Turan text): names live on through statues and offspring (about M 298-305, p. 162)
**Where and when:** the narrator's closing reflection; no scene.
**Who:** the narrator; the goddess Aruru is named; the last line addresses Ereshkigal.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narration: statues have been made since ancient times for every man who bears a name and set up in temple chapels, so the saying of their names will never be forgotten (about M 298-301).
2. Narration: the goddess Aruru, Enlil's older sister, gave men children so that their names would last (about M 302-303).
3. Narration: their statues have been made since ancient times, and their names are still spoken in the land (about M 304).
4. Closing praise addressed to Ereshkigal, mother of Ninazu (M 305).

**Specifics:** funerary statues; chapels in the gods' temples; names spoken aloud; Aruru (another name of Belet-ili, the Mother Goddess, Glossary p. 221), Enlil's older sister; children. Ereshkigal as Ninazu's mother.

**Short quotations:**
- "sweet is your praise!" (M 305)

**Gaps:** none in these lines. "(men)" and "(their names still)" are George's parentheses.

**George's reading (p. 152):** this is the more didactic ending: the dead survive in the memory of the living, through votive statues in temples, which keep the name spoken and give a focus for the funerary cult, and through the families the gods made men have.

**Repetitions:** the phrase about all men who are given names (also M 182 and N1 vi 18); the statues made since ancient times, twice.

**Compared with the standard version:** partial parallels.
- **Statues:** Gilgamesh has a statue made of Enkidu (VIII 66-72, p. 64; promised at VII 82). Here statues of all named men, kept in temples.
- **Aruru:** in the epic Aruru makes Enkidu from clay (I 94-104, pp. 4-5), and Belet-ili, Enlil's older sister, receives a grave gift for Enkidu (VIII 139-140, p. 66). Here Aruru, also Enlil's older sister, gives men children.
- **Remembrance:** the epic ends with Gilgamesh showing Ur-shanabi the wall of Uruk (XI 323-328, pp. 96-97), which the prologue presents as his lasting work (I 11-23). Here memory lasts through statues and children.
- **Closing praise:** the epic has none; this ending praises Ereshkigal, not Gilgamesh.

### S5.16. Second ending (another manuscript): no king will ever match him (N3 39-42, p. 162)
**Where and when:** the last lines of a different manuscript (George introduces them as another manuscript's different conclusion, p. 162).
**Who:** the narrator; Nintu is named.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narration: never in all time to come will Nintu bear a king equal to Gilgamesh, son of the goddess Ninsun, one who could either stand against him or outdo him (N3 39-41).
2. Closing praise of Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab (N3 42).

**Specifics:** Nintu ("Lady who Gives Birth"), a name of the Mother Goddess (Glossary, p. 223). Lord of Kullab.

**Short quotations:**
- "sweet is your praise!" (N3 42)

**Gaps:** none in these lines. George calls this ending the less well preserved one (p. 152); the lines between N3 31 and N3 39 are not printed here.

**Repetitions:** the same closing praise ends "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" (Ur version), there addressed to Gilgamesh as warrior and son of Ninsun.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the prologue's praise, which says he surpasses all kings and asks who could rival his royal standing and claim kingship as he does (I 29, 45-46, p. 2). Difference: the epic's praise opens the poem; here it closes it, after his burial. Names: Nintu here; the epic's mother goddess is Aruru / Belet-ili (I 49, 94).

## Elements list

**Characters (with epithets as given)**
- Gilgamesh: the great wild bull; lord Gilgamesh; the one perfect in battle; the warrior with a shoulder-belt; the one perfect in strength; the one who brought the wicked low; the one who spoke wisely; the land's watchful one; the one who climbed the mountains; lord of Kullab (S5.1); "the young lord" (S5.2, S5.6, S5.10, S5.14); hero of the Shining Mountain, lord of Uruk, smith of the great gods (S5.7); son of the goddess Ninsun (S5.12, S5.16); after death, governor of the Netherworld and chief of the dead (S5.4, S5.5, S5.8).
- Enkidu: his dear friend, his little brother, his young companion (named only in Enlil's promise, S5.5, S5.7).
- Gilgamesh's family: mother Ninsun; father Lugalbanda, "the pure" (S5.7); father, grandfathers, mother, sisters and siblings in the Netherworld (S5.5); his son Urlugal (S5.9); his beloved wife and child, senior wife and junior wife (S5.11).
- Household: minstrel, steward, barber, attendants, servants (S5.11).
- His architect (S5.9); the herald (S5.10); the labor gangs of Uruk and Kullab (S5.10).
- The counsellors (restored, uncertain) (S5.7, S5.8).
- Ziusudra, the one human who survived the Deluge (S5.3, S5.4).
- His city's people: young men, young women, old men and women, his people (S5.14); the city's elders (S5.5).
- The dead priests and priestesses: en, lagar, lumaḫ, nindingir, guda, linen-clad, "the true one" (S5.5, S5.12); governors, kings, army chiefs, troop captains (S5.5).

**Places**
- The deathbed, called Namtar's bed and a bed of misery (S5.1, S5.2).
- The gods' assembly, their place of ceremony (S5.3).
- Uruk, Kullab (S5.1, S5.2, S5.10); the middle of Uruk (S5.10, S5.11).
- Huwawa's forest and the mountain of the unique cedar; Ziusudra's home; the settlements of all lands (S5.3).
- The Netherworld: the underworld, the place of darkness, the Great City, Aralli (S5.4, S5.5); the Holy Mound (S5.12).
- The Euphrates and its bed; the stone tomb and its doors (S5.10, S5.13).
- Chapels in the gods' temples (S5.15).

**Objects and materials**
- The shoulder-belt (S5.1); net, snare, throwing-net (S5.1, S5.8).
- The herald's horn (S5.10).
- The tomb: stone walls and doors; bar, threshold and bolts of hardest diorite; beams of cast gold; a heavy stone block (S5.10).
- Funerary statues (S5.4, S5.15).
- Audience-gifts, presents, surprises, gifts (S5.12); a water offering (S5.12).
- Palm fiber and onion (images, S5.5).

**Creatures**
- The wild bull (Gilgamesh's title, S5.1); a fish in a net; a gazelle in a snare (S5.1); a netted bird; a deep-sea fish (S5.8).

**Gods and what they do**
- Namtar: holds Gilgamesh with his lock; has no hands or feet; takes men at night; gores (S5.1, S5.7); receives presents (S5.12).
- Enki / Nudimmud: opens Gilgamesh's eyes in the dream (S5.2, S5.7); gives the verdict to An and Enlil (S5.4); shows how to read the dream so the tomb can be placed (S5.9); Gilgamesh swears by him as his god (S5.7).
- Enlil, Great Mountain, father of the gods: comforts Gilgamesh in the dream; gave him kingship but not eternal life (S5.5).
- An: addressed by Enki together with Enlil (S5.3, S5.4).
- The gods' assembly: reviews Gilgamesh's deeds; once sent the Deluge; swore that humans would not live forever (S5.3, S5.4).
- Sissig, the Dream God, Utu's son: to give Gilgamesh light below (S5.4).
- Utu: anger is to be undone before him (S5.5). The Sun God: the riverbed's pebbles look up at him (S5.10).
- Ningishzida and Dumuzi: judges of the dead and the measure of Gilgamesh's authority; receive gifts (S5.4, S5.5, S5.8, S5.12).
- The Anunna gods: hold the funeral feast; Gilgamesh will count among them (S5.5, S5.8); receive gifts, with the Igigi (S5.12).
- Ereshkigal, mother of Ninazu: receives audience-gifts (S5.12); praised in the first ending (S5.15).
- Dimpikug, Bitti, Enlil's ancestors (Enki and Ninki, Enmul and Ninmul, Endukuga and Nindukuga, Endashurimma and Nindashurimma, Enutila, Enmesharra), Shulpae, Shakkan, Ninhursanga: receive gifts (S5.12).
- The Mother Goddess: has never borne a man that death does not take (S5.8). As Aruru, Enlil's older sister: gives men children (S5.15). As Nintu: will never bear a king to equal Gilgamesh (S5.16).
- Ninsun: Gilgamesh's mother, the reason the gods hesitate, though not a reason to spare him (S5.4, S5.7).

**Numbers and time**
- Twelve lying-down lines (S5.1).
- Six days of illness (S5.2).
- Far-off days, nights and years (S5.4).
- Six things that overtook him (S5.5).
- Under half a month to drain the river, "not five days, not ten" (S5.10).
- The Month of Torches, the ghosts' festival (S5.4); George: the fifth month, about August, the ninth day (p. 151).
- Three "will come to you" lines from a fuller manuscript (S5.7).

**Dreams and omens**
- The deathbed dream sent by Enki-Nudimmud: the gods' assembly, Enki's verdict, Sissig and the festival, Enlil's comfort (S5.2-S5.5).
- The dream retold by Gilgamesh to his counsellors (S5.7), and their reply (S5.8).
- The dream's "solution," shown by Enki and worked out by Urlugal, which fixes the tomb's site (S5.9).

**Customs and rituals**
- Sumer's rites restored after the Deluge: washing of hands and mouth (S5.3).
- Funerary statues; wrestling and trials of strength by young warriors in a half-circle around a doorway; lights in the Month of Torches (S5.4).
- The gods' funeral feast (S5.5).
- An oath by the life of mother, father and god (S5.7); the gods' oath by heaven's life and earth's life (S5.4).
- A labor levy announced by herald and horn (S5.10).
- Burial of the household with the king, laid out as if for a palace inspection (S5.11).
- Gifts to the Netherworld's gods; a water offering (S5.12).
- Sealing the tomb and hiding it under the river (S5.13).
- Mourning: clawing faces, tearing hair, rolling in the dust (S5.14).
- Statues in temple chapels; names kept spoken; children given so that names last (S5.15).
- Closing praise: to Ereshkigal (S5.15) or to Gilgamesh (S5.16).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **Provisional translation:** George says the poem is still very hard in places and extends to it his warning that the Bull of Heaven rendering is not final (pp. 150, 122).
- ⚠️ **Manuscript sigla** (M, N1-N4) are not defined in this section; M is the Mê-Turan text (p. 154), and N1-N4 appear to be the Nippur tablets.
- ⚠️ **The gaps:** after M 19 (about M 20-39), after M 61 (about M 62-64), after M 174 (about M 175-179), after the counsellors' reply (to M 234), after N3 31, and before N1 viii 2. Most of the gods' debate over whether Gilgamesh must die (George's synopsis, p. 150) falls in the gaps; only the line saying he should not be taken (M 66) and Enki's answer survive.
- ⚠️ **Enki's and Enlil's speeches in the first telling** (end of S5.4, start of S5.5) are entirely restored from the retelling (George's note, p. 154). The text does not mark who speaks the Sissig and festival lines; George's synopsis treats the judgment as Enki's and has Enlil appear after it (pp. 150-151).
- ⚠️ **Whom Gilgamesh consults:** a lost word in M 132; the restored and uncertain "counsellors" in N1 vi 4.
- ⚠️ **How Enki got the tomb's site to Gilgamesh, and Urlugal's part:** lost in a break (George, p. 151).
- ⚠️ **The "they" who are confused** (M 236) and **the "they" who seal the tomb** (N1 viii 2) are not identified.
- ⚠️ **Gilgamesh's death is not narrated** in the surviving text: he lies down (N3 29), pours water (N3 31), a gap follows, then the sealing.
- ⚠️ **Whether the household is alive** when placed in the tomb is not said (S5.11).
- ⚠️ **Whose gifts:** the text says "their" gifts and presents (S5.12); George's synopsis reads them as meant to win a kind reception for Gilgamesh and his household (p. 151).
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings (italics):** "in combat" (M 3); "net," "held fast," "snatches" (conjectural, M 16-18); "he had a dream" (conjectural, M 47); "on that account," "spoke" (M 66-67); "conversed," "whatever life it has," "thus come," "siblings," "sibling's" (Enlil's speech); "lord" of Uruk and "my" eyes (M 139); "counsellors," and the conjectural openings of N1 vi 8-9, "throw," "fenced in," "rushes" (S5.8); "not even" (M 245); "chamber" (M 261); "resting place" (N1 viii 5).
- ⚠️ **Person shift in the retelling:** it begins with "my eyes" and then stays in the third person (George, p. 151).
- ⚠️ **Two endings:** George identifies the second only as another manuscript's different conclusion (p. 162).
- ⚠️ **Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), "Sumerian poems,"** says Gilgamesh is buried under the Euphrates once he has died; George 2020's surviving text does not narrate the death (he lies down and pours water, then a gap), and his synopsis says only that he lays himself down (p. 151).
- ⚠️ **Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), "Sumerian poems,"** notes that the Sumerian texts spell the hero's name Bilgames; George 2020's translation uses "Gilgamesh" throughout. Wikipedia's account of this poem (dream of the gods' decision, counsel, funeral preparations, gifts, burial under a diverted and restored Euphrates, rule and judgment over the dead) otherwise matches George 2020; its "Tablet eight" section also links the damming at Enkidu's funeral to this riverbed burial, as George does (pp. 98-99).

## Leads (observations on the text's own structure only)
- Lead: the dream is given twice, nearly word for word (M 48-125 and M 139-216), and each telling fills the other's gaps.
- Lead: the governor-and-judge formula (judging, deciding, a word as weighty as Ningishzida's and Dumuzi's) is stated four times: by Enki, by Enlil, in the retelling, and by the counsellors.
- Lead: the phrase about all men who are given names comes in the festival lines, the counsellors' reply and the first ending; each time it leads to statues or to fate.
- Lead: the dead priests are listed twice, first as the company waiting for Gilgamesh (Enlil's speech), then as receivers of his gifts (S5.12).
- Lead: the opening formula about far-off days is shared with "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" (its line 1), and so is the closing praise.
- Lead: water frames the ending: the river drained (S5.10), water poured as an offering (S5.12), the river let back over the tomb (S5.13).
