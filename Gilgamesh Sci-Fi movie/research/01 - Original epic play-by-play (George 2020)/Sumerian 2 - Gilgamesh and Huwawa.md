# Sumerian 2. Gilgamesh and Huwawa ("The lord to the Living One's Mountain" and "Ho, hurrah!"): play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 2 ("The Sumerian Poems of Gilgamesh"), pp. 105-122. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**Two poems.** George gives two related compositions: Version A, "The lord to the Living One's Mountain" (pp. 106-116), and Version B, "Ho, hurrah!" (pp. 117-122). Scenes are numbered S2A.1 and so on for Version A, S2B.1 and so on for Version B.

**Line numbers.** George's margin numbers follow the traditional count, which is based on a shorter form of the text. Lines he adds from fuller manuscripts are indented and left out of the count (chapter introduction, p. 99); their margin shows a manuscript label instead of a number (for example "NiO," "UrF," "UnC"). Here such lines are cited as "after line N" with the label. Ranges without a printed marker at one end are marked "about." Version A has 202 counted lines; Version B breaks off at about line 156. Speeches are summarized; George's wording appears only in a few short quotations.

## George's synopsis (pp. 105-106 and 117, paraphrased)
**Both poems (p. 105):**
- Another favorite school text. It covers the journey to the Forest of Cedar, Huwawa's death and the cutting of the cedar, so it is a forerunner of Tablets III-V of the Babylonian epic, but the details differ widely.
- The key difference: Huwawa is caught **through a trick, not overpowered**, and the trick is one the god Enki reveals to Gilgamesh.
- The two compositions are close relatives with many shared lines; both titles could be translated in other ways. More copies of Version A survive, so it was the more popular, and almost all of it can be restored. Version B has more gaps.

**Version A (pp. 105-106):**
- Afraid of death, Gilgamesh sets his mind on winning glory and plans a journey to the legendary Cedar Mountain, home of "the Living One," apparently a title of its guardian spirit, the demigod Huwawa. Early tradition put the mountain far to the east, where the sun comes up.
- Enkidu, Gilgamesh's servant, advises him to get the approval of the Sun God, Utu (Shamash in Akkadian). Gilgamesh asks, explaining that he has seen how fleeting human life is and wants to make his name last. Utu assigns him **seven constellations** as guides.
- Gilgamesh musters and arms Uruk's young men and leaves. The constellations lead him to the cedar country. He crosses seven ranges before he finds a tree that suits him, cuts it down without delay, and his men chop it into logs.
- Huwawa, the cedars' guardian, wakes and hurls one of his **auras** (George: the shining, numinous powers that protect him) at Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh and Enkidu are knocked senseless. Enkidu recovers first and finally manages to wake Gilgamesh, who swears to find out who attacked them. Enkidu describes the dreadful Huwawa; Gilgamesh replies that two together can do what one alone cannot.
- Near Huwawa's home Gilgamesh is frozen where he stands, and Huwawa's voice tells him not to fear but to get down on the ground. Gilgamesh pretends to want a marriage alliance and offers his sisters **Enmebaragesi and Peshtur** as brides. He also promises luxuries apparently unknown in that remote place: fine flour, water in leather bottles, sandals large and small, precious stones and more. For the sisters and for each gift Huwawa hands over one aura. The auras are imagined as great cedars, and Gilgamesh's men cut each one into logs to carry home.
- Once Huwawa has no auras left and cannot fight, Gilgamesh drops the pretense, hits him and takes him captive. Huwawa begs for his life and complains to Utu that Gilgamesh has cheated him. Gilgamesh is inclined to a lord's mercy, but Enkidu warns that letting Huwawa go means never getting home. When Huwawa rounds on Enkidu in anger, Enkidu slits his throat.
- The heroes carry the head to the god Enlil, who angrily demands why they killed Huwawa and says they should have treated him with every honor. Finally Enlil shares out Huwawa's auras (p. 106).

**Version B (p. 117):**
- Shorter, less popular and still incomplete; more compressed than Version A and different in many small ways.
- The big difference in plot comes when Gilgamesh wakes from the auras' sleep. Instead of putting on a brave show for a frightened Enkidu, he doubts he is strong enough for the monster and asks his own god, **Enki**, to "emerge" in his words. George takes this to mean that the trickster god should inspire him to win by clever speech.
- Enki does this through Enkidu, who seems to blurt out the words Gilgamesh should say, and the story goes straight on to the meeting with Huwawa and Gilgamesh's flattering diplomacy.

## Version A: "The lord to the Living One's Mountain" (pp. 106-116)

### S2A.1. Gilgamesh resolves to go; Enkidu says tell Utu (lines 1-12, pp. 106-107)
**Where and when:** presumably Uruk (not stated). Opening of the poem; no time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu, his servant. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh, called simply "the lord," sets his mind on the Living One's Mountain (1-2).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: because no one can avoid death, he will go into the mountain and make a name for himself; where other names already stand he will add his own, and where none stand yet he will put up the names of gods (3-7).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: if his lord is going into the mountain he must first tell Utu, the "Young Hero," because the mountain (Cut-Cedar Mountain) is Utu's business (8-12).

**Specifics:** the Living One's Mountain (1-2); Cut-Cedar Mountain (12). Utu's title, Young Hero (10, 12). Gilgamesh "the lord" (1-2). Enkidu his servant (3, 8).

**Short quotation:** "set up my name" (5): repeated in the same speech to Utu at 31.

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** 4-7 return word for word at 30-33. Enkidu's advice (9-12) recurs in Version B (S2B.2).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Gilgamesh's proposal of the expedition in Tablet II (Old Babylonian Yale tablet, about Y 97-101, pp. 17-18) and his reasons (II 234-236, p. 19; Y 184-187, p. 20).
- **Names:** Huwawa's home is the Living One's Mountain, also Cut-Cedar Mountain, not the Forest of Cedar; the Sun God is Utu, not Shamash; Huwawa, not Humbaba (and he is not yet named).
- **Motive:** in both, death and fame. In the standard version Gilgamesh tells Enkidu that a man's days are counted and all he does is wind (II 234-235; Thompson 1928 reads "mortals—their days are (all) number’d, / All that they do is (but) wind," Tablet III, col. iv, ll. 5-10, p. 21), and he vows to win a name that lasts forever (Y 187). The Sumerian promise to set up gods' names where there are none has no counterpart.
- **Who speaks and what Enkidu says:** in the standard version Enkidu replies with warnings against going (Y 105-115, II 216-229, pp. 18-19). Here he raises no objection and only advises telling Utu. In the standard version it is Ninsun, not Enkidu, who turns to the Sun God (III 35-116, pp. 23-26).
- **Roles:** Enkidu is a servant here, a friend in the standard version.

### S2A.2. Offering and prayer to Utu; Gilgamesh's lament (lines 13-35, p. 107)
**Where and when:** not stated; right after S2A.1. No time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Utu, who answers from heaven. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Utu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh takes a white kid in his hand and holds a brown kid against his chest as an animal offering (13-14).
2. He raises a pure staff to his nose and speaks to Utu in heaven (15-16).
3. Gilgamesh → Utu (prayer): he is about to go into the mountain, Cut-Cedar Mountain, and asks Utu to help him (17-18).
4. Utu → Gilgamesh, from heaven: at home he is a nobleman, but what would a young man like him amount to out in the mountain? (19-20).
5. Gilgamesh → Utu: he asks Utu to listen. In his city people die and it wounds the heart. Looking over the rampart, he once saw a dead body floating down the river, and he knew that he would end the same way. He cites a proverb: however tall, no man reaches the sky; however broad, no man spans a mountain. Since death cannot be escaped, he will go into the mountain and make his name, and set up gods' names where none stand (21-33). The corpse is a **memory he recounts**.
6. Utu takes his tears as if they were an offering and, like a merciful man, pities him (34-35).

**Specifics:** a **white kid** and a **brown kid** (13-14); the pure staff held at the nose (15); Utu addressed as Utu of Heaven (16). The rampart, the river, the floating corpse (25-26). Utu calls him a young man and a nobleman (20).

**Short quotation:** "I too shall become like that" (27)

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** 30-33 repeat 4-7 word for word. The plea for help (17-18) recurs in Version B (S2B.2); the lament recurs there in a changed form (S2B.1).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the appeals to the Sun God before the journey: Ninsun's prayer to Shamash from her roof (III 35-116, pp. 23-26) and the damaged rituals for a safe journey after III 130 (p. 26). The death theme parallels II 234-236 (p. 19).
- **Who prays:** here Gilgamesh prays for himself, with a kid offering. In Tablet III Ninsun prays for him, after bathing seven times, burning incense in a censer on the roof (III 37-45).
- **Who speaks:** here Utu answers directly. In Tablet III Shamash gives no answer in the surviving text; the standard version first has his voice speak in the forest (V 95-100, p. 39).
- **Events:** the floating corpse has no counterpart in Tablets II-V. A related image comes late in the standard version, in Uta-napishti's speech about the mayfly drifting on the river (X 312-313, p. 85).
- **Outcome:** here Utu pities him and helps at once (S2A.3). In Tablet III Ninsun asks Shamash to send the thirteen winds (III 88-93, pp. 24-25) and to have the nights keep watch over him.

### S2A.3. Utu gives the seven warriors (lines 36-47, pp. 107-108)
**Where and when:** not stated; right after S2A.2.
**Who:** Utu (called "the warrior," the Young Hero); Gilgamesh; the seven warriors (described, silent). Speaker: Utu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The narrator describes seven warriors, all born of one mother (36-43):
   - the eldest has a lion's paws and an eagle's talons;
   - the second is a cobra (uncertain) with gaping mouth (the word "open" is restored; more is lost);
   - the third is a mighty Dragon Serpent (end lost);
   - the fourth, whose name is lost, breathes fire;
   - the fifth is a serpent whose shriek splits the hills;
   - the sixth is like a flood torrent pounding the mountains;
   - the seventh strikes like lightning and no one can approach it.
2. Utu gives Gilgamesh these seven (44).
3. Utu → Gilgamesh (three uncounted lines after 44, from manuscript NiO, then 45): the goddess Nissaba has given him something more (the start is lost); the seven shine in the sky and know the roads on earth; they are to show him the way (perhaps "to the east," a conjecture) and lead him to the place in the hills where boats have to be pulled ashore.
4. With this Utu cheers Gilgamesh, "the Cedar Smiter" (46-47).

**Specifics:** seven, sons of one mother (36). Lion's paws and eagle's talons (37); cobra, Dragon Serpent, fire, shrieking serpent, flood torrent, lightning (38-43). The goddess Nissaba (after 44). The place where boats must be beached (45). Gilgamesh's title: Cedar Smiter (46). George identifies the seven as **constellations** (p. 105).

**Short quotation:** "the Cedar Smiter" (46)

**Gaps:** "open" (38) is restored; "cobra" (38) is uncertain; parts of 38-40 are lost. In the NiO lines the words for "in the heavens" and "on earth" are restored, "to the east" is conjectural (George prints it in italics inside brackets), and the opening word is lost.

**Repetitions:** the description of the seven is repeated, mostly in restored form, when they lead Gilgamesh (after 58, S2A.5), and again in Version B (S2B.3).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version** for the seven warriors or constellations.
- **Events and who helps:** the standard version's help from the Sun God is the thirteen winds, requested by Ninsun (III 88-93, pp. 24-25) and sent during the fight (V 157-165, p. 41). Its guide on the road is Enkidu: the elders tell Gilgamesh to let Enkidu walk ahead because he knows the way (III 6-7, p. 22).
- **Numbers:** in the standard version seven belongs to Humbaba, not to Gilgamesh's helpers: his seven cloaks, i.e. auras (V 99-100, p. 39), and his seven sons (about V 307-308, p. 46).
- **Names:** Nissaba and the title Cedar Smiter do not occur in Tablets II-V.

### S2A.4. Fifty men; the forge and the Dark Grove (lines 48-58, p. 108)
**Where and when:** Uruk, its forge, and the Dark Grove outside the walls. Right after S2A.3.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; the townsmen; fifty unattached men; smiths and woodcutters (implied: he "had them" cast and cut). Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh blows the horn in his city; the text says he sounds it as a single man but it carries like two together (48-49).
2. Gilgamesh → the city (proclamation): men with families should go to their families, men with mothers to their mothers; fifty men with no ties, like himself, should join him (50-51).
3. It is done as he says: family men go home, and fifty unattached men stand with him (52-53).
4. At the forge he has bronze war-hatchets and battle-axes cast, his weapons for heroic deeds (54-55).
5. In the Dark Grove outside the city he has axe handles cut from oak and from boxwood (56-57).
6. He gives these to the men of his city who are going with him (58; "he set them" is restored).

**Specifics:** the horn (48-49). **Fifty** unattached men (51, 53). **Bronze** hatchets and axes (55). The **Dark Grove**, outside the city (56). Handles of **oak** and **boxwood** (57). The men called "the sons of his city" (58).

**Short quotation:** "fifty of them" (51)

**Gaps:** the words "he set them" (58) are restored.

**Repetitions:** 52-53 repeat 50-51 as narrative. Version B has the same call-up with wives and children in place of families and mothers (S2B.4).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the forge scene (Old Babylonian Yale tablet, Y 162-171, pp. 18-19) and the departure (III 212-213, p. 27; Y 272-277, pp. 27-28).
- **Events:** the standard version has no horn call, no Dark Grove and no wood for handles. There Gilgamesh bars Uruk's seven gates and calls the assembly (Y 172-173, p. 19).
- **Numbers and materials:** fifty companions here; the standard version's numbers are weights: axes of three talents each, dagger blades of two talents, ten talents carried by each hero (Y 165-171). Bronze, oak and boxwood appear only here.
- **Who goes:** fifty men go with Gilgamesh and Enkidu here. In the standard version only the two heroes go; the young men chase after them as a crowd (III 213), and Enkidu tells Gilgamesh to send the crowd home (Y 277, p. 28).

### S2A.5. The journey over seven ranges; the first cedar felled (about lines 58-66, pp. 108-109)
**Where and when:** on the road to, then in, the cedar mountains. No time of day; no length of journey is given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu; the men of his city; the seven warriors as guides. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The seven warriors are described a second time (uncounted lines after 58, nearly all restored): the same seven forms; in the sky they shine and on earth they know the roads, and they show the way (perhaps east) (after 58, and 59).
2. They (the pronoun is uncertain) bring him to the place in the hills where boats are beached (60).
3. He crosses the first range of mountains without finding the cedar he is looking for (61), then the second through the sixth, each time without success (uncounted lines after 61, manuscript UrF). On the seventh range he finds it (62).
4. Without asking or searching any further, Gilgamesh cuts into the cedar (63-64).
5. Enkidu strips off its branches for him (a word or two lost), and the men of the city stack them up (65-66).

**Specifics:** **seven** mountain ranges (61-62). The refrain of the cedar he wanted (61-62). The beaching place for boats (60). George: early tradition located the Cedar Mountain far east, toward the sunrise (p. 105).

**Short quotation:** "the cedar he wanted" (61-62): the refrain of the search.

**Gaps:** the second description of the seven (after 58) is restored almost entirely from the first (36-45); plain text survives only in parts of its first line, in the line about the first warrior (59), and in a few words of the line on showing the path (whose "to the east" is again conjectural). "They" (60) is uncertain. The lines for ranges two to six are partly restored. A small loss in 65.

**Repetitions:** the description of the seven repeats S2A.3. The range line repeats six times with only the number changing, then turns on the seventh (61-62). Version B has the same sequence (S2B.4).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the journey in Tablet IV (IV 1-5 and its repeats, pp. 28-35) and the arrival at the forest (V 1-27, pp. 36-37); the felling parallels about V 300-323 (p. 46).
- **Events:** in the standard version the journey moves in stages, each with a well dug, flour offered on a hilltop, a shelter built for the dream god and a dream that Enkidu explains (Tablet IV, at least five dreams). None of that is here. There the heroes reach the forest and stand amazed at it (V 1-27); here Gilgamesh hunts range after range for one tree.
- **Numbers:** seven ranges here. The standard version measures the march in leagues: fifty in a day, a month and a half's journey covered by the third day (about IV 3-4); Humbaba later says Gilgamesh leveled ten mountains (V 169, p. 42).
- **Order of events and outcome:** here the cedar is cut **first**, and that wakes Huwawa. In the standard version the cedars are cut only **after** Humbaba's death (about V 300-323), and Enkidu chooses one tall cedar for a door for Enlil's temple.
- **Who does what:** here Gilgamesh strikes the tree and Enkidu trims the branches; in the standard version Gilgamesh does the felling while Enkidu looks for the best timber (about V 311-312).

### S2A.6. Huwawa's auras; the stupor; Enkidu wakes Gilgamesh (about lines 66-85, pp. 109-110)
**Where and when:** at the cut cedar on the mountain. It starts by day and runs to dusk: by the time Enkidu speaks, the evening light has gone (78). The stupor's length is not stated.
**Who:** Huwawa (unseen, acting from his lair); Gilgamesh; Enkidu; the men of the city. Speaker: Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The noise rouses Huwawa in his lair (uncounted line after 66).
2. Huwawa throws his terrifying auras at Gilgamesh (67).
3. Gilgamesh falls into a stupor like sleep, Enkidu into a daze, and the men of the city lie trembling at his feet like puppies (68-70; the words for stupor and torpor, and Enkidu's name, are restored).
4. Enkidu comes to. He shudders as if waking from a deep sleep or a dream, rubs his eyes, and finds everything silent and desolate (71-72). No dream content is given.
5. He touches Gilgamesh and speaks to him, but cannot wake him and gets no answer (73-74).
6. Enkidu → the sleeping Gilgamesh: he asks the young lord of Kullab how long he means to lie there. The mountain has gone dark and shadowed, the last evening rays are gone, and the Sun has gone proudly home to his mother Ningal. The men who came with him must not be left waiting at the foot of the mountain, and their mothers must not be reduced to spinning yarn in the city square (75-83).
7. He speaks this into Gilgamesh's right ear, and his heroic words wrap Gilgamesh like a garment (84-85; "this message" is restored). That Gilgamesh wakes is shown in S2A.7.

**Specifics:** the auras "of terror" (67). Puppies (70). "Young lord of Kullab" (76). Ningal, the Sun's mother (79). The foot of the mountain (82). Mothers spinning yarn in the city square (83). The right ear (84). A garment, as a simile (85).

**Short quotation:** "it had been a deep sleep" (71)

**Gaps:** the words for stupor and torpor and Enkidu's name (68-69), and "this message" (84), are restored. The line about disturbing Huwawa is uncounted.

**Repetitions:** Enkidu's waking lines come back in Version B, applied to Gilgamesh (S2B.5).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version** for the stupor before the meeting.
- **Events:** in the standard version terror grips Gilgamesh as the cedar's shadow falls on him, stiffening his arms and shaking his legs (V 27-30, p. 37), but he stays conscious. Humbaba's auras there are seven cloaks he must be kept from putting on (V 99-100, p. 39), which fill the forest during the fight (V 147-148, p. 41); he does not use them to knock the heroes out.
- **Sleep and waking:** the standard version's sleeps are the deliberate dream-sleeps of the journey, from which Gilgamesh wakes asking whether his friend called or touched him (IV 18-20 and repeats, pp. 28-35). Here Enkidu wakes first and has to rouse Gilgamesh.
- **Names:** Ningal does not occur in Tablets II-V.

### S2A.7. Gilgamesh's oaths; Enkidu's fear; "two men together will not die" (lines 86-119, pp. 110-111)
**Where and when:** on the mountain, at night, right after S2A.6.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh takes thirty shekels of oil and rubs it on his chest (86; George adds "(Gilgamesh)" to make the subject clear).
2. He sets himself down on the Great Earth, on all fours (restored) like an ox, bows his neck to the ground and speaks (87-88).
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (oath): he swears by the life of his mother, the goddess Ninsun, and of his father, pure Lugalbanda, and asks whether he should cower like a frightened child on his mother's knees (89-90).
4. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, a second time, with the same oath: he will not turn his feet homeward from the mountain until he learns whether the creature is human or divine (91-94).
5. Enkidu, whose epithet calls him the servant who makes life pleasant and sweet, → his master: Gilgamesh has not seen the creature and so is not afraid; Enkidu has seen it and is. He describes it: a warrior with a dragon's teeth and a lion's eye, a chest like a flood torrent and a brow that devours the reed thicket; no one can come near it; (uncounted line after 102) it is like a man-eating lion whose tongue never has enough blood. He asks to go home: Gilgamesh can climb on, and Enkidu will tell Gilgamesh's mother first that he is alive, to make her laugh with joy, and then that he is dead, to make her weep (95-105).
6. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: get moving; two men together do not die. A reed raft does not sink, a triple rope cannot be cut, a flood cannot wash a man off a wall, and nobody can put out a burning reed hut; if they stand together no one can harm them (106-110). He quotes a saying about ships: the boat from Magan sank, the barge and the cargo boat sank, but the reed raft stayed afloat and saved lives (111-114). They should hurry to go and look at the creature. He quotes another saying, that fear is answered by fear and cunning by cunning, and urges Enkidu on whatever he feels (115-119).

**Specifics:** **thirty shekels** of oil (86). The Great Earth; the ox (87). The oath by **Ninsun** and **Lugalbanda**, the latter called his father and pure (89, 92). Enkidu's epithet about making life pleasant and sweet (95). Huwawa's features: dragon teeth, lion eye, torrent chest, a brow that devours the canebrake, a man-eating lion's tongue (99-102 and after 102). The things that cannot fail: reed raft, triple rope, a man on a wall in a flood, a fire in a reed hut (106-109). The ships: the boat from Magan, a barge, a cargo boat, a reed raft (111-114).

**Short quotation:** "Two men together will not die" (106)

**Gaps:** "on all fours" (87) and "his tongue" (after 102) are restored; "sated" is uncertain. ⚠️ The poem never shows when Enkidu saw Huwawa (98).

**Repetitions:** the oath (89, 92) recurs throughout the ruse (S2A.9, S2A.10). In Version B Enkidu's description of Huwawa is spoken by Gilgamesh (S2B.6).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Enkidu's warnings (Y 105-115, p. 18; II 216-229, pp. 18-19), Gilgamesh's rebuke (II 232-233, p. 19) and the heroes' encouragement of each other in the forest (about V 31-50, and about V 73-80, pp. 37-39).
- **Who speaks the "two together" sayings:** in the standard version it is **Enkidu**: one friend is one, but two are two; two can climb a sloping rampart one cannot; a triple rope is hard to break; a strong dog's two pups can win (about V 73-80, p. 39). Here **Gilgamesh** says it, with other images (reed raft, flood, reed hut, the Magan boat).
- **How Enkidu knows Huwawa:** in the standard version Enkidu knew Humbaba from his days with the herd in the uplands (Y 106-107) and describes him by sound: a voice like the Deluge, fiery speech, deadly breath (Y 110-111). Here he describes teeth, eye, chest and brow, and asks to go home.
- **Events:** Enkidu's offer to go home and report to Gilgamesh's mother has no counterpart. In the standard version Gilgamesh calls Enkidu's talk cowardly (II 232), and later Enkidu says the same to him (V 130-131, p. 40).
- **Names:** Lugalbanda is Gilgamesh's **father** here; in the standard version he is Gilgamesh's personal god (V 141, p. 41; also IV, Ha1 15, p. 35), and Gilgamesh is called Lugalbanda's wild bull (I 35, p. 2). The Glossary records both traditions (p. 223). Magan does not occur in Tablets II-V.
- **Motive:** Gilgamesh wanting to know whether Huwawa is human or divine compares with his wish in the standard version to see the god people talk about (Y 182, p. 20).

### S2A.8. At Huwawa's house of cedar; Gilgamesh held fast; "Be not afraid" (lines 120-135, p. 111)
**Where and when:** close to Huwawa's dwelling on the mountain. No time of day; the previous scene was at night.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa; Enkidu is not mentioned. Speaker: Huwawa (see the note on 130-135).

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narration in the present tense: nobody comes closer than sixty pole-lengths, and Huwawa is already in his house of cedar (120-121).
2. Narration: his look is deadly; a shake of his head is a verdict of guilt; when he speaks he is blunt (122-124).
3. Huwawa → (whoever he addresses): capable as you are, you will never get back to your mother's city (125). ⚠️ Lines 122-125 speak of "one" in general; it is unclear whether 125 is addressed to Gilgamesh now or illustrates how Huwawa talks.
4. Terror floods Gilgamesh's muscles and feet; he cannot lift his foot from the ground (uncertain wording), his big toe holds it fast (uncertain), and the terror pours into his side and another part of him (lost) (126-129).
5. Huwawa → Gilgamesh: the "Ho, hurrah!" praise: tall young tree, noble favorite of the gods, angry ox ready to fight; his mother knew how to make a child and his nurse how to nurse one. Do not be afraid; put both hands on the ground (130-135).

**Specifics:** **sixty pole-lengths** (120). The house of cedar (121). The deadly eye (122). The guilty-verdict head-shake (123). The big toe (128). Gilgamesh's praise titles: tall-grown sapling, the gods' noble favorite, angry ox ready for combat (130-132).

**Short quotation:** "Ho, hurrah! Tall-grown sapling" (130): the formula that names Version B.

**Gaps:** "move" (127) and "held fast by" (128) are uncertain renderings; a loss in 129; "(Gilgamesh)" in 127 is George's added word. ⚠️ The speaker of 130-135 is not named; George's synopsis gives the words to Huwawa's voice, telling Gilgamesh not to fear and to kneel (p. 106).

**Repetitions:** the praise block (130-134) is repeated by Enkidu to Gilgamesh at 164-169 (S2A.13), opens Version B (S2B.1), and is spoken by Enkidu in "Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven" (Ma 120-125).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Humbaba's appearance and first speech in Tablet V (V 102-126, pp. 39-40).
- **Who speaks and what is said:** in the standard version Humbaba speaks with scorn, calling the visit fools' counsel and asking why Gilgamesh has come before him; he insults Enkidu as a fish's spawn and threatens to cut Gilgamesh's throat and feed him to the birds (about V 115-124). Here Huwawa flatters Gilgamesh and tells him not to fear and to kneel.
- **Events:** in the standard version Humbaba bellows like a wild bull and thunders like the storm god (V 102-106). Gilgamesh's terror comes earlier, at the forest's edge (V 27-30), and his reaction to Humbaba is that Humbaba's face has changed (V 126, p. 40). The foot held fast by its big toe has no counterpart.
- **Numbers:** sixty pole-lengths here; the standard version's sixty is in leagues: Humbaba hears the forest from sixty leagues away (II 223, p. 18), and the forest is sixty leagues of wilderness (Y 108).
- **Names:** a house of cedar here; in the standard version Humbaba has a lair and a bed (V 68, V 127).

### S2A.9. The ruse begins: two sisters for the first aura (lines 136-148, pp. 111-112)
**Where and when:** before Huwawa's dwelling, right after S2A.8.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa; the men of his city. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh puts both hands on the ground (136).
2. Gilgamesh → Huwawa: the oath by Ninsun and Lugalbanda; nobody knows where Huwawa lives on the mountain, and they ought to; he will send his **older sister Enmebaragesi** up to be Huwawa's wife (137-139).
3. Gilgamesh → Huwawa, again: the same oath and the same line about Huwawa's unknown home; he will send his **little sister Peshtur** to be Huwawa's concubine. In return Huwawa should give him one of his auras, and he will become Huwawa's kinsman (140-144).
4. Huwawa gives him his first aura (145).
5. The men of his city trim its branches, tie them in a bundle and lay it at the foot of the mountain (146-148). George: the auras are pictured as great cedars, cut into logs for the trip home (p. 106).

**Specifics:** Enmebaragesi, older sister, offered as wife (139); Peshtur, little sister, offered as concubine (143). Becoming Huwawa's kinsman (144). The foot of the mountain (148).

**Short quotation:** "I will become your kinsman" (144): the closing line of every round.

**Gaps:** none in these lines.

**Repetitions:** the oath, the line about Huwawa's unknown home, the request for an aura, the handing over, and the three lines of trimming, tying and laying down repeat for each of the seven auras (S2A.10).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** There is no trick, Gilgamesh has no sisters, and Humbaba is overpowered in a fight with the help of Shamash's thirteen winds (V 141-165, p. 41).
- **Auras:** in the standard version Humbaba's protection is seven cloaks; Shamash warns that he is wearing one and has taken off six (V 99-100, p. 39), so the heroes must strike before he puts them on. Here Huwawa gives them away one at a time.
- **Who offers what:** in the standard version it is Humbaba who makes offers: to serve Gilgamesh and keep timber for him, myrtle, cedar and cypress (V 171-177, p. 42). Here Gilgamesh makes the offers.

### S2A.10. Auras two to seven for gifts (uncounted lines after 148, pp. 112-114)
**Where and when:** before Huwawa's dwelling, continuing S2A.9.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa; the men of his city. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Note on the text:** the whole passage is indented and left out of the traditional count, which goes straight from 148 to 149. George takes it from fuller manuscripts (the label UnC appears beside the second round) and restores the second and seventh rounds in brackets (his policy, p. 99).

**Play-by-play:** every round follows one pattern: Gilgamesh speaks "a second time," "a third time," and so on; he swears by Ninsun and Lugalbanda; he repeats that Huwawa's home on the mountain is unknown and ought to be known; he names a gift he will bring up the mountain; he asks whether that will not make him one of Huwawa's family; he asks for an aura in exchange for kinship; Huwawa hands it over; and the men trim the branches, tie them up and lay the bundle at the foot of the mountain.
1. **Second aura:** the gift is lost; the speech is restored, with the gift left blank. The surviving plain text begins where Huwawa hands over the second aura and the men bundle it.
2. **Third aura:** the best flour, the kind the great gods eat, and flasks of cool water.
3. **Fourth aura:** large sandals for large feet.
4. **Fifth aura:** small sandals for small feet.
5. **Sixth aura:** rock crystal, chalcedony and lapis lazuli.
6. **Seventh aura:** the whole round is restored; the gift is lost.

**Specifics:** the gifts: top-quality flour fit for the great gods; cool water in flasks; big sandals; little sandals; rock crystal; chalcedony; lapis lazuli. George's synopsis sums them up as fine flour, water in leather bottles, sandals of two sizes, gemstones and other presents (p. 106).

**Short quotation:** "So may I not then join your family?" (refrain, after 148)

**Gaps:** the second and seventh rounds are restored except for the narrative lines of the second; the gifts of those two rounds are lost. In the fourth round the words "in the mountain" are restored.

**Repetitions:** six more rounds on the pattern of S2A.9. Version B shortens the passage (S2B.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** No one offers Humbaba gifts, sandals, flour or gems, and no auras change hands.

### S2A.11. The blow; Huwawa captured and bound (lines 149-152, and uncounted lines after 152, p. 114)
**Where and when:** at Huwawa's lair, right after the seventh aura.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa; "the two of them" (not named). Speakers: Huwawa; Gilgamesh (an order to sit).

**Play-by-play:**
1. With all seven auras taken from Huwawa, Gilgamesh comes up to his lair (149).
2. He creeps after Huwawa like a snake (the place in the simile, a "wine harbour," is an uncertain rendering), pretends he is about to kiss him, and punches him on the cheek (150-151).
3. Huwawa bares his teeth and furrows his brow (152).
4. (Uncounted lines after 152, manuscript SiA:) Gilgamesh throws a rope over him as one would over a captured wild bull, and ties his arms like a prisoner of war's.
5. (Manuscript UrG:) Huwawa → Gilgamesh: he calls him a deceitful warrior (the rest, perhaps an accusation of wrongdoing, is conjectural).
6. The two of them fit a nose-rope on him (a word lost). Dragging him out of his lair, one of them orders him to sit; the order is given twice, once of "the warrior" and once of Huwawa (after 152).
7. (Manuscript UrA:) Huwawa sits down weeping and sobbing; the text says this twice, once of "the warrior" and once of Huwawa. Huwawa begins to plead with Gilgamesh (after 152).

**Specifics:** seven auras (149). The snake simile; the "wine harbour" (150). The fake kiss; the fist; the cheek (151). A rope as for a captured wild bull; arms tied as for a prisoner; a nose-rope (after 152).

**Short quotation:** "O deceitful warrior" (after 152)

**Gaps:** "wine harbour" (150) is uncertain. In the uncounted lines the end of Huwawa's accusation and the word for dragging are conjectural; the verbs for fixing the nose-rope, saying and addressing are restored; a word after "nose-rope" is lost. ⚠️ "The two of them" is not explained, and Enkidu is not named in this scene.

**Repetitions:** the weeping line is said in parallel of "the warrior" and of Huwawa (after 152). The binding lines return in Version B as counted lines (126-127, S2B.8).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the fight with Humbaba (V 141-165, p. 41).
- **Events:** the standard version has a real battle: Gilgamesh strikes the bedrock nine times (uncertain), the two attack like a lion and a puma (uncertain), they seize Humbaba and grab his auras, the ground splits, Mounts Sirion and Lebanon break apart, and Shamash's thirteen winds pin Humbaba until Gilgamesh's weapons win (V 143-165). Here there is one punch after a false kiss, and a rope.
- **Who is called a deceiver:** here Huwawa calls Gilgamesh deceitful. In the standard version Humbaba accuses Enkidu of treacherously bringing Gilgamesh to him (V 121, p. 40).
- **Numbers:** here all seven auras are taken by trickery; in the standard version Humbaba has one cloak on and six off when the fight begins (V 99-100).

### S2A.12. Huwawa's plea to Utu; Gilgamesh takes pity (lines 153-162, pp. 114-115)
**Where and when:** outside the lair, with Huwawa bound and sitting (S2A.11).
**Who:** Huwawa; Gilgamesh; Enkidu; Utu (addressed, silent). Speakers: Huwawa, Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Huwawa → Gilgamesh: he begs to be set free and to be allowed a word with the Sun God (153-154).
2. Huwawa → Utu (**prayer**): he had no mother to bear him and no father to raise him; the mountains gave him birth and Utu brought him up. Gilgamesh, he says, swore by heaven, by earth and by the Netherworld (155-157).
3. Someone grasps someone's hand and falls prostrate (158). ⚠️ The text does not say who; George's synopsis has Huwawa begging for his life (p. 106).
4. Noble Gilgamesh feels pity for him (159).
5. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, his servant: let the caught bird fly home, and let the captured soldier go back to his mother's arms (160-162).

**Specifics:** "the Sun God" (154) and Utu (155). No mother, no father, born in the mountains, raised by Utu (155-156). The oath Huwawa says Gilgamesh swore: by heaven, earth and Netherworld (157). The release images: a captive bird, a captive soldier (161-162).

**Short quotation:** "it was you who reared me" (156)

**Gaps:** none in the counted lines. ⚠️ The oath by heaven, earth and Netherworld (157) does not appear earlier in the poem; there Gilgamesh swears by Ninsun and Lugalbanda (S2A.9, S2A.10).

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's two release lines (161-162) are turned into a condition by Enkidu at 172-173.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Humbaba's pleas (V 166-177, p. 42; V 185-195, pp. 42-43) and a Middle Babylonian fragment from Ugarit that George places after V 206 (p. 43).
- **Wording:** the Ugarit lines say much the same as 155-156: Humbaba, speaking to Shamash, says he knew no mother or father, that a mountain bore him and Shamash raised him (p. 43). That fragment was found after 1928 and is not in Thompson.
- **What Humbaba offers:** in the standard version he offers to serve Gilgamesh and supply timber worthy of his palace (V 171-177), and begs Enkidu to intercede (V 194-195). Here he asks only to speak to Utu and accuses Gilgamesh of breaking an oath.
- **Who speaks:** in George's standard version Gilgamesh gives no reply to the pleas in the surviving lines; Enkidu answers each one. Here Gilgamesh takes pity and proposes letting him go. (Wikipedia's synopsis differs on this point; see Open questions.)
- **Names:** Utu here, Shamash in the standard version.

### S2A.13. Enkidu's warning; Huwawa's retort; Enkidu cuts off his head (lines 163-179, with uncounted lines after 174, pp. 115-116)
**Where and when:** the same place, right after S2A.12.
**Who:** Enkidu; Gilgamesh; Huwawa. Speakers: Enkidu, Huwawa.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he addresses him with the "Ho, hurrah!" praise (tall young tree, noble favorite of the gods, angry ox ready to fight), adding a line that calls him the young lord honored in Uruk, and the lines about his mother and nurse (163-169).
2. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: even the tallest man, if he lacks good advice, is swallowed by Namtar without noticing. If the caught bird flies home and the captured soldier returns to his mother, Gilgamesh himself will never get back to his own mother's city (170-174).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (uncounted lines after 174, manuscript UnB): who ever heard of a captured warrior set free, a captured priestess sent back to her cloister, a captured priest given back his wig? Huwawa will tangle the mountain road and the mountain path in front of them.
4. Someone, called "the warrior," listens to these words (after 174; the phrase is restored). ⚠️ Who is meant is not stated.
5. Huwawa → Enkidu: Enkidu is slandering him to Gilgamesh; he calls Enkidu a hired man who serves the enemy for his bread (175-177).
6. As Huwawa is still speaking, Enkidu, in a rage, cuts his head off at the neck (178-179).

**Specifics:** Namtar (171), whom the Glossary explains as "Doom," Ereshkigal's minister and the angel of death (p. 223). The priestess and her cloister; the priest and his wig (after 174). Huwawa's insult: a mercenary (177). Beheading at the neck (179).

**Short quotation:** "Mercenary" (177)

**Gaps:** in the uncounted lines after 174, most of the second half of each line is restored, including the end of the "who ever saw" question, the road and path lines, and the words introducing the listener. ⚠️ George's synopsis says Enkidu cuts Huwawa's throat (p. 106); the translation says he cuts off his head at the neck (179).

**Repetitions:** 164-169 repeat Huwawa's praise address (130-134) with the extra Uruk line (167). 172-173 repeat 161-162. The priestess lines recur in Version B (140-146, S2B.9).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Enkidu's urging and the killing in Tablet V (V 178-183, p. 42; about V 196-204, p. 43, repeated about V 245-253 and V 265-270, pp. 43-44; the killing, V 285-290, p. 45).
- **Enkidu's argument:** in the standard version he warns that if Humbaba goes home they are as good as unborn, because Humbaba will trap them in the forest (V 181-182), and urges a quick kill before Enlil hears of it, to win lasting fame (about V 200-204). Here the argument is a proverb about freed captives and a warning that Huwawa will confuse the mountain road.
- **Huwawa's insult to Enkidu:** close in sense. In the standard version Humbaba says Enkidu sits before Gilgamesh like a shepherd or a hired hand doing his bidding (V 261-262, p. 44). Here he calls him a mercenary working for bread.
- **Events missing here:** the standard version's curse by Humbaba, that the two will not grow old and that only Gilgamesh will bury Enkidu (V 279-280, p. 45), has no counterpart.
- **Who kills and how:** here **Enkidu alone**, in anger, cuts off the head. In the standard version **Gilgamesh** stabs Humbaba in the neck and **Enkidu** pulls out heart and lungs; they take the tusks as booty (V 285-290).
- **Names:** Namtar does not occur in Tablets II-V.

### S2A.14. The head before Enlil; Enlil's anger (lines 180-192, p. 116)
**Where and when:** before the gods Enlil and Ninlil (the place is not named). How long after the killing is not stated.
**Who:** Gilgamesh and Enkidu ("they"); Enlil; Ninlil (present, silent). Speaker: Enlil.

**Play-by-play:**
1. They put the head into a leather bag and bring it before Enlil and Ninlil (180-181).
2. After kissing the ground before Enlil, one of them throws the bag down and empties out the head; they place it in front of Enlil (182-184). ⚠️ Who throws the bag (183) is not named.
3. Seeing Huwawa's head, Enlil is angered by what Gilgamesh has done (185-186).
4. Enlil → them: he asks why they did it and whether anyone ordered Huwawa's name blotted out; Huwawa should have sat with them and shared their bread and their water. Now, he says, Huwawa will decorate the houses of the great gods (187-192; the last words are restored).

**Specifics:** a **leather sack** (180). Enlil and Ninlil (181). Kissing the ground (182). Bread and water (190-191). The great gods' houses (192, partly restored).

**Short quotation:** "Why have you done this thing?" (187)

**Gaps:** the words for "the houses" and "gods" (192) are restored.

**Repetitions:** none.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the end of Tablet V (about V 302-306 and about V 315-323, p. 46) and the gods' council at the opening of Tablet VII (Hittite paraphrase, p. 54).
- **Events:** in the standard version the heroes do not appear before Enlil. Enkidu wonders how they will answer Enlil in Nippur for the ruined forest (about V 302-306), makes a great cedar door for Enlil's Nippur temple, and the two float a raft down the Euphrates with Gilgamesh holding Humbaba's head (about V 315-323).
- **Outcome:** here Enlil rebukes them face to face, and no punishment is named. In the standard version the gods hold a council: Anu rules that one of the two must die for killing the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba, Enlil chooses Enkidu and spares Gilgamesh, and Shamash protests that the killings were done at Enlil's own word (Tablet VII, Hittite paraphrase, p. 54).
- **Names:** Ninlil is present here; she does not appear in Tablets V or VII.
- **What the head becomes:** here Enlil says Huwawa will adorn the gods' houses; in the standard version the tusks are taken as booty (V 290) and the head goes on the raft.

### S2A.15. Enlil distributes the auras; closing praise (uncounted line before 193, and lines 193-202, p. 116)
**Where and when:** the same, right after Enlil's speech.
**Who:** Enlil; the goddess Nungal (receives an aura); the narrator. No speeches except the narrator's closing words.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Seated on his throne, Enlil shares out Huwawa's heavenly auras (uncounted line before 193, manuscript UnB).
2. In order, he gives one aura each to the field, the river, the reed thicket, the lion, the woods, the palace and the goddess Nungal, and keeps whatever auras are left for himself (193-200).
3. Narrator: glory to the mighty Gilgamesh, and praise to the goddess Nissaba (201-202).

**Specifics:** the seven recipients in order: field, river, canebrake, lion, woods, palace, Nungal (193-199). The leftover auras (200). Glossary: Nungal, patron goddess of prisons and Enlil's stewardess; Nissaba, a grain goddess and patron of writing and accounting (p. 224).

**Short quotation:** "the remaining auras" (200)

**Gaps:** none. ⚠️ Huwawa lost seven auras (149) and Enlil gives away seven, yet some are left over (200); their number is not stated.

**Repetitions:** the seven-step list mirrors the seven rounds of the ruse (S2A.9, S2A.10).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Humbaba's auras are not distributed after his death, and there is no closing praise of Nissaba.

## Version B: "Ho, hurrah!" (pp. 117-122)

### S2B.1. Opening praise; Gilgamesh resolves to climb the mountain (lines 1-21, pp. 117-118)
**Where and when:** Uruk (named at 5). Opening of the poem; no time of day.
**Who:** the opening voice (not named); Gilgamesh. Speakers: the opening voice (1-4), Gilgamesh (5-21, under a printed speaker label). The listener is not stated.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The poem opens by hailing Gilgamesh with the "Ho, hurrah!" praise (tall young tree, noble favorite of the gods, angry ox ready for battle), naming him young lord Gilgamesh, honored in Uruk (1-4).
2. Gilgamesh: when a man dies in Uruk, the mind and heart are wounded (5-6).
3. From the rampart he once saw a dead body floating on the water (7-8), a **memory he recounts**, as in Version A.
4. He despairs: death cannot be avoided, nor the grave and the crushing Netherworld that spares no one (9-11). He quotes the proverb that no man reaches over a mountain however high or spans one however wide (12-13).
5. Since no one escapes death, he swears by the lives of his mother Ninsun, his father Lugalbanda, his god Enki (also called Nudimmud), his little sister Enmebaragesi and his sister Peshtur that he will climb the mountain of the single, unique cedar (14-19).
6. He will build a temple to his god at the cedar's foot, take its branches for himself, and pass between its splendid gateposts (20-21).

**Specifics:** rampart, water, corpse (7-8). The grave and the Netherworld (11). The oath list: Ninsun; Lugalbanda (the epithet "pure" restored); Enki, the lord Nudimmud; Enmebaragesi, little sister; Peshtur, sister (15-17). The unique cedar (19). A temple to his god at its base (20). The cedar's gateposts, clad in splendor (21). Glossary: Nudimmud, "Man-fashioner," a name of Ea (p. 224).

**Short quotation:** "the unique cedar tree" (19)

**Gaps:** "the pure" (15) and "climb" (18, 19) are restored. ⚠️ Enmebaragesi is the little sister here (17) but the older sister in Version A (139).

**Repetitions:** 1-4 are Version A's praise block (130-134, 164-169). 5-13 are a variant of Version A 23-29.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Gilgamesh's reasons for the expedition (II 234-235, p. 19; Y 184-187, p. 20) and the use of the cedar for a god's house (about V 315-319, p. 46).
- **Motive:** death and fame in both. This version names the Netherworld; the standard version's Tablet II says only that whatever a man does is wind (II 235).
- **Names:** here Enki (Ea) is Gilgamesh's own god; in Tablets II-V Ea has no role, and Gilgamesh's god is Lugalbanda (V 141). The standard version gives Gilgamesh no sisters.
- **The temple:** here Gilgamesh plans a temple to his god at the foot of the cedar. In the standard version Enkidu makes a great cedar door for Enlil's temple at Nippur (about V 315-319). Gilgamesh's plan there to plant their weapons in Humbaba's gate (III 211, p. 27) compares with passing through the cedar's gateposts here.
- **Who speaks first:** Version B begins with the praise formula; Version A and the standard version do not.

### S2B.2. Enkidu says tell Utu; Utu appears; Gilgamesh's prayer (lines 22-33, p. 118)
**Where and when:** not stated; right after S2B.1. Utu comes out with his head held high (30).
**Who:** Enkidu; Gilgamesh; Utu. Speakers: Enkidu, Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu, his servant → Gilgamesh: before entering the mountain, Cut-Cedar Mountain, he must tell Utu, because the mountain belongs to Utu, the Young Hero (22-28).
2. Utu, the god of heaven, puts on his lapis-lazuli brilliance and comes forth proudly (29-30).
3. Gilgamesh, lord of Kullab, holds the pure staff to his nose (31).
4. Gilgamesh → Utu (**prayer**): he is about to enter the mountain, Cut-Cedar Mountain, and asks for Utu's help (32-33).
5. Lines 34-35 are lost (marked by George's row of asterisks).

**Specifics:** Utu's lapis-lazuli brilliance (29). The pure staff at the nose (31). "Lord of Kullab" (31). Cut-Cedar Mountain (25, 28, 33).

**Short quotation:** "lapis-lazuli lustre" (29)

**Gaps:** the opening words of Enkidu's speech and the word "mountain" (23, 25) are restored. Lines 34-35 are lost. In the surviving text there is no kid offering, no answer from Utu and no lament addressed to Utu (all present in Version A 13-35).

**Repetitions:** 22-28 repeat Version A 8-12; 32-33 repeat Version A 17-18.

**Compared with the standard version:** as for S2A.2: in the standard version Ninsun, not Gilgamesh, prays to Shamash (III 35-116, pp. 23-26). The Sun God coming out proudly in his brilliance has no counterpart in Tablets II-V.

### S2B.3. Utu's seven warriors as stars and guides (about lines 36-50, p. 118)
**Where and when:** not stated; right after the gap.
**Who:** Utu; Gilgamesh; the seven warriors (described). Speaker: Utu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Utu gives Gilgamesh seven warriors born of one mother (36-37, restored).
2. Utu → Gilgamesh, in the present tense: he describes the seven as in Version A (lion's paws and eagle's talons, cobra, Dragon Serpent, fire-breather, shrieking serpent, flood torrent, lightning); the first lines are restored (38-44).
3. Utu → Gilgamesh (continued): the seven shine in the sky as blazing stars and know the roads on earth, including the road to Aratta; like merchants they know the trade routes, and like pigeons every crevice of the mountains; they will guide him through the passes (45-50).

**Specifics:** seven; the same seven forms as Version A (38-44). The seven as stars (46). **Aratta** (47), which the Glossary places far off in the highlands of Iran as Uruk's traditional rival (p. 221). Merchants (48); pigeons (49); mountain passes (50).

**Short quotation:** "stars blazing on high" (46)

**Gaps:** lines 34-35 are lost before this scene; 36-41 are wholly restored, as are parts of 42-43 and 47-48. "Cobra" (39) is uncertain.

**Repetitions:** 38-45 repeat Version A 37-43 (and the restored lines after A 44), recast as Utu's speech in the present tense.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version** (see S2A.3). Differences from Version A: here the seven are called stars outright, and the guide images are new (Aratta, merchants, pigeons). Version A's Nissaba and the beaching place for boats do not occur.

### S2B.4. The task force; departure; seven ranges; the cedar felled (about lines 51-65, pp. 118-119)
**Where and when:** Uruk and Kullab, then the road to Cut-Cedar Mountain and the ranges. No duration is given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; the townsmen; Enkidu; the men of his city. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh raises a task force in his city (51) and blows the horn in Kullab (52; "the midst" of Kullab is a conjectural restoration).
2. Gilgamesh → the city (proclamation): married men should go to their wives and fathers to their children; the warriors, known and unknown, who have neither wife nor child should come with Gilgamesh (53-56).
3. The king leaves the city; Gilgamesh leaves Kullab (57-58).
4. On the road to Cut-Cedar Mountain he crosses the first range without finding the cedar he wants (59-60); likewise the second through sixth (uncounted lines after 60, manuscript B, mostly restored); on the seventh he finds it (61).
5. Gilgamesh cuts down the cedar, Enkidu his servant cuts it into logs, and the men of the city stack them (62-65).

**Specifics:** the task force (51); the horn (52); the warriors known and unknown (54); no number of men is given. "The king" (57). Seven ranges (60-61). Logs and a stack (63-65).

**Short quotation:** "warriors known ... warriors unknown" (54)

**Gaps:** "the midst" (52) is conjectural. The lines for ranges two to six are restored except for a few words in each (manuscript B).

**Repetitions:** the range lines repeat Version A 61-62; the felling repeats Version A 63-66 with small changes.

**Compared with the standard version:** as for S2A.4 and S2A.5 (the forge, Y 162-171, pp. 18-19; the journey, Tablet IV, pp. 28-35; the felling, about V 300-323, p. 46).
- **Differences from Version A:** wives and children instead of families and mothers; **no number** of men (Version A has fifty); no forge, no Dark Grove and no weapons in the surviving text; no guidance by the seven and no beaching place for boats. Enkidu cuts the trunk into logs instead of trimming branches.
- **Order of events:** as in Version A, the tree is cut before Huwawa is met; in the standard version, after his death.

### S2B.5. The aura unrolls; the stupor; Gilgamesh wakes (lines 66-80, pp. 119-120)
**Where and when:** at the cut cedar. When Enkidu speaks the evening light has gone (77).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa (approaching, unseen); Enkidu. Speaker: Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. As the two warriors come close to each other, Huwawa's aura spreads out like a carpet (66-67; Huwawa's name is restored).
2. Gilgamesh is overcome by a stupor like sleep (68; the subject and the word for stupor are partly conjectural).
3. Lines 69-72 are lost.
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (mostly restored): he asks the young lord how long he will go on sleeping; the mountain is dark and in shadow, and the evening rays are gone (73-77).
5. Gilgamesh gets up, shuddering as if out of a dream or a deep sleep, rubs his eyes and finds desolate silence (78-80).

**Specifics:** the aura spread like a carpet (67). "Young lord Gilgamesh" (75). Evening (77).

**Short quotation:** "unrolled like a carpet" (67)

**Gaps:** Huwawa's name (67), most of 68 and most of 73-77 are restored; 69-72 are lost. George's footnote 1 to line 78 says the tablet wrongly reads "Enkidu" there; George reads "Gilgamesh."

**Repetitions:** 74-77 repeat Version A 75-78 (with "sleep" for "lie"); 78-80 repeat Version A 71-72, which there describe Enkidu.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version** for the stupor (see S2A.6). Differences from Version A: only Gilgamesh is said to be overcome in what survives; the aura spreads like a carpet instead of being thrown; it comes as the two warriors approach, not in response to the noise of cutting.

### S2B.6. Gilgamesh calls on Enki and doubts himself; Enkidu supplies the words (lines 81-97, p. 120)
**Where and when:** at the mountain, at night, right after Gilgamesh wakes.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu; the god Enki (invoked). Speakers: Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh (oath and **invocation**): swearing by Ninsun and Lugalbanda, he prays that his god Enki, the lord Nudimmud, may "emerge" in his words (81-83; Enki's name and the prayer are restored).
2. Gilgamesh: he has seen the creature (restored) and understands what it is: a warrior with a lion's eye, a chest like a flood, a brow that devours the reed thicket, unapproachable, and a man-eating lion's tongue never sated with blood (84-88).
3. Gilgamesh: he lacks the strength to match such a warrior; who could have it? (89).
4. Enkidu, his servant, answers him (90). Lines 91-92 are lost.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: swearing by Gilgamesh's mother and father, he prays that Gilgamesh's god Enki-Nudimmud may emerge in his words, and gives him the words: the creature's mountain home should be known; little sandals should be made for its little feet and big sandals for its big feet (93-97). George: Enki speaks through Enkidu, who seems to blurt out what Gilgamesh should say (p. 117).

**Specifics:** Enki, also called Nudimmud and Enki-Nudimmud (82, 94). Huwawa's features: lion's eye, flood-torrent chest, brow that devours the canebrake, man-eating lion's tongue (85-88). The sandal words: small sandals for small feet, big for big (96-97).

**Short quotation:** "emerge in my words" (83)

**Gaps:** 82-84 are largely restored; 91-92 are lost; 93-97 are partly restored. "Match" (89) is George's supplied word; "sated" (88) is uncertain. George explains "emerge in my words" as the trickster god inspiring clever speech (p. 117).

**Repetitions:** 85-88 repeat Enkidu's description of Huwawa from Version A (99-102 and the line after 102), now in Gilgamesh's mouth. Gilgamesh repeats the sandal lines to Huwawa at 105-107 (S2B.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Gilgamesh's fear before Humbaba and Enkidu's encouragement (V 126-142, pp. 40-41).
- **Who is afraid:** Gilgamesh, both here and in the standard version, where he says a frightened heart does not calm down in a moment (V 128). In Version A it is Enkidu.
- **What Enkidu supplies:** in the standard version, a push to use force: strike hard like a lion, and remember your god Lugalbanda (V 136-141). Here, clever words inspired by Enki.
- **Names:** Enki (Ea) has no role in Tablets II-V; there Gilgamesh's god is Lugalbanda (V 141).
- **Differences from Version A:** the roles are reversed. In Version A Enkidu describes Huwawa and wants to go home, and Gilgamesh answers that two men together will not die. No such speech survives in Version B.

### S2B.7. Gilgamesh's words to Huwawa; the auras handed over (about lines 98-122, pp. 120-121)
**Where and when:** before Huwawa. How soon after S2B.6 is not stated.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa; the men of his city. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. A few lines are lost (98-102; George's note, p. 120).
2. Gilgamesh → Huwawa: swearing by his mother Ninsun and by Lugalbanda, the father who begot him, he prays that his god Enki-Nudimmud may emerge in his words (103-104).
3. Gilgamesh → Huwawa: he says Huwawa's mountain home should be known, and offers small sandals for his small feet and large ones for his large feet (105-107).
4. Lines 108 to about 113 are lost.
5. Restored lines (about 114-122): Huwawa gives him the first aura and the men stack it; then the second through the sixth.
6. The men heap them up in piles on the mountain (uncounted line, manuscript A).

**Specifics:** Lugalbanda as the father who begot him (103). Sandals, small and large (106-107). Piles on the mountain (uncounted line).

**Short quotation:** "little sandals" (106)

**Gaps:** 98-102 and about 108-113 are lost; about 114-122 are restored entirely. George's footnote 2 to line 104: the tablet wrongly reads "him" and "your" there. The offer of the sisters and Version A's other gifts are not in what survives of Version B; the sisters appear only in the opening oath (17).

**Repetitions:** 105-107 repeat Enkidu's words (95-97) as Gilgamesh's own. The aura count follows Version A's sequence in shortened form.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version** (see S2A.9, S2A.10). Differences from Version A: only sandals are named as a gift in what survives; the trick is credited to Enki speaking through Gilgamesh; the aura-trees are piled on the mountain rather than laid at its foot.

### S2B.8. The blow; Huwawa bound; "you deceived me" (lines 123-134, p. 121)
**Where and when:** at Huwawa's lair, after the seventh aura.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Huwawa. Speaker: Huwawa.

**Play-by-play:**
1. With all seven auras taken, Gilgamesh comes up to the lair and punches Huwawa **on the ear** (123-124).
2. Huwawa furrows his brow and bares his teeth at him (125).
3. Gilgamesh ropes him like a captured wild bull and ties his elbows behind him like a prisoner of war (126-127).
4. "The warrior" and Huwawa both start weeping and sobbing (128-129).
5. Huwawa → Gilgamesh: he cheated him and laid hands on him despite his oath; swearing by Gilgamesh's mother Ninsun and father Lugalbanda, he says that Gilgamesh's god Enki-Nudimmud spoke through him; and he repeats that Gilgamesh roped him like a wild bull and tied his elbows like a prisoner (130-134).

**Specifics:** seven auras (123). The ear; the fist (124). The rope; elbows tied behind (126-127, 133-134).

**Short quotation:** "you deceived me" (130)

**Gaps:** the words for Gilgamesh's mother (131), "your" and Enki's name (132) and the first halves of 133-134 are restored. "Behind him" and "behind me" are George's supplied words.

**Repetitions:** 133-134 repeat 126-127 in Huwawa's mouth. 128-129 parallel the uncounted lines after Version A 152.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the fight (V 141-165, p. 41), as for S2A.11. Differences from Version A: the punch lands on the ear, not the cheek; there is no false kiss and no snake simile; the elbows are tied; Huwawa names Enki as the source of the deception and accuses Gilgamesh of breaking an oath (Version A has the oath by heaven, earth and Netherworld, 157).

### S2B.9. Gilgamesh would free Huwawa as a guide; Enkidu objects (lines 135-146, with uncounted lines, p. 121)
**Where and when:** the same place.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enkidu; Huwawa (bound). Speakers: Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Noble Gilgamesh pities Huwawa (135; some words restored).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, his servant: he will set the warrior free (136-137); (uncounted lines, manuscript W) Huwawa can be their guide and scout the way, and carry his pack as a porter (the word "porter" is conjectural).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (138; uncounted line, manuscript W): even the tallest man can lack good sense.
4. Line 139 is lost.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: who has ever seen a captured warrior freed, a captured priestess returned to her cloister, a captured priest given back his wig? Huwawa will confuse the mountain road and path in front of them, and they will never get back to their mothers' city (140-146; "return to the city" is restored).
6. Lines 147-150 are lost.

**Specifics:** guide, scout, porter, pack (uncounted lines after 137). The priestess and cloister; the priest and wig (141-142). Their mothers' city (146).

**Short quotation:** "he shall be our guide" (after 137)

**Gaps:** "porter" is conjectural; "return to the city" (146) is restored. Lines 139 and 147-150 are lost.

**Repetitions:** 140-145 repeat Version A's uncounted lines after 174, here as counted lines and without restorations.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Enkidu's case against mercy (V 178-183, p. 42), as for S2A.13. Gilgamesh's reason for sparing Huwawa (to use him as guide and porter) has no counterpart; in the standard version it is Humbaba who offers to stay and serve (V 174, p. 42). Differences from Version A: no captive-bird or Namtar lines; Enkidu says "we," not "you," will not get home.

### S2B.10. Huwawa's reply; the text breaks off (lines 151-156, p. 122)
**Where and when:** the same place.
**Who:** Huwawa; Gilgamesh. Speakers: Huwawa, Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Huwawa → Gilgamesh (the name is a conjectural restoration): a mountain cave was his mother and an upland cave his father, and Utu made him live alone on the mountain (151-154).
2. Gilgamesh → Huwawa: he begins, "Come," and the line breaks off (155-156).
3. The rest is lost (George, p. 122). Huwawa's death and the scene before Enlil do not survive in Version B.

**Specifics:** two caves as mother and father; mountains and uplands (152-153). Huwawa alone on the mountain (154).

**Short quotation:** "a cave in the mountains" (152)

**Gaps:** the word for answering is restored and Gilgamesh's name (151) is conjectural. The poem breaks off after 156.

**Repetitions:** repeats in other words Version A's motif of no mother and no father (155-156).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Humbaba's plea in the Ugarit fragment that George places after V 206, where a mountain bore Humbaba and Shamash raised him (p. 43). Differences: here caves are mother and father, and Utu is blamed for Huwawa's solitude.

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **Titles:** George notes that both titles could be translated differently (p. 105).
- ⚠️ **"The Living One":** George says the title apparently means Huwawa (p. 105).
- ⚠️ **The seven warriors as constellations** is George's identification (p. 105). Version A does not call them stars; Version B does (B 46).
- ⚠️ **Speaker of A 130-135** (the praise and "Be not afraid") is not named; George's synopsis gives the words to Huwawa (p. 106). Enkidu speaks the same block at A 164-169.
- ⚠️ **A 122-125** describe how Huwawa looks at and speaks to "one"; it is unclear whether 125 is addressed to Gilgamesh at that moment.
- ⚠️ **Enkidu's sighting of Huwawa:** Enkidu says he has seen him (A 98), but no earlier sighting is told. In Version B the same claim is Gilgamesh's (B 84, restored).
- ⚠️ **Enmebaragesi** is the older sister in A 139 and the little sister in B 17. The Glossary adds that historically Enmebaragesi was an early ruler of Kish, assumed male (p. 222); in "Gilgamesh and Akka" the name belongs to Akka's parent.
- ⚠️ **Lost gifts:** the offers for the second and seventh auras are lost (A, after 148).
- ⚠️ **The oath by heaven, earth and Netherworld** that Huwawa says Gilgamesh swore (A 157) is not shown earlier.
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings (italics):** "cobra" (A 38, B 39); "They" (A 60); "move" and "held fast by" (A 127-128); "wine harbour" (A 150); "sated" (after A 102, B 88).
- ⚠️ **Conjectural restorations (italics in brackets):** "to the east" (after A 44, after A 58); the end of Huwawa's accusation and the word for dragging (after A 152); "the midst" (B 52); "porter" (after B 137); Gilgamesh's name (B 151); the subject and stupor of B 68.
- ⚠️ **Unnamed persons:** who grasps whose hand at A 158; "the two of them" (after A 152); the one who throws down the sack (A 183); the listening "warrior" (after A 174); the opening voice of Version B (B 1-4).
- ⚠️ **How Huwawa dies:** George's synopsis says Enkidu cuts his throat (p. 106); the translation says he cuts off the head at the neck (A 179).
- ⚠️ **The leftover auras** (A 200): seven are taken and seven given out, yet Enlil keeps the rest; how many is not stated.
- ⚠️ **The beaching place for boats** (A 45, 60) is not explained by George.
- ⚠️ **Version B text problems:** George's footnote 1 (B 78): the tablet wrongly reads "Enkidu"; footnote 2 (B 104): the tablet wrongly reads "him" and "your." Lines 34-35, 69-72, 91-92, 98-102, about 108-113, 139 and 147-150 are lost, and the end is missing. The reading of "emerge in my words" as Enki inspiring cunning speech is George's (p. 117).
- **Uncounted lines:** the second to seventh aura rounds (A, after 148) and several other passages lie outside the traditional count; George adds them from fuller manuscripts to give the fullest form (p. 99).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) gives Version A's opening as "The lord went to the Living One's Mountain"; George 2020 has no verb ("The lord to the Living One's Mountain").
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the Huwawa poems correspond to Tablets II-V; George 2020 matches them to Tablets III-V (pp. 98, 105).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), in its Ishchali tablet section, says the auras appear in one of the Sumerian poems as "sons"; in George 2020 both Huwawa poems call them auras throughout, and the seven sons of Humbaba belong to the standard version (about V 307-308).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), Tablet five, says Gilgamesh pities the pleading Humbaba; George 2020's Tablet V shows no reaction from Gilgamesh in the surviving lines (about V 166-206), only Enkidu's replies. Pity is explicit in the Sumerian Version A (A 159) and Version B (B 135).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says the Sumerian poems spell the hero's name "Bilgames"; George 2020's translation uses "Gilgamesh" throughout.

## Leads (observations on the text's structure only)
- **Lead:** sevens organize Version A: seven warriors, seven ranges, seven auras traded, seven recipients of the auras.
- **Lead:** the "Ho, hurrah!" praise block passes between speakers: Huwawa to Gilgamesh (A 130, per George), Enkidu to Gilgamesh (A 164), the opening voice (B 1), and Enkidu in "Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven" (Ma 120).
- **Lead:** Version A gives Gilgamesh's speech about death twice, first to Enkidu (A 4-7), then to Utu, framed by the corpse on the river (A 30-33).
- **Lead:** the captive-bird and captive-soldier lines are first Gilgamesh's plea for mercy (A 161-162) and then Enkidu's warning (A 172-173).
- **Lead:** the two versions give the fear to different heroes: Enkidu in Version A (A 95-105), Gilgamesh in Version B (B 84-89), and the description of Huwawa goes with the fear.
- **Lead:** every aura round ends with the same three lines (trim, tie, lay down at the mountain's foot), so the cut trees pile up as the auras are given away.

| S2A.8 Huwawa's house; Gilgamesh held fast (A 120-135) | Humbaba's appearance and speech, V 102-126 (pp. 39-40) | Huwawa praises Gilgamesh and tells him to kneel; in the standard version Humbaba insults both heroes and threatens Gilgamesh. Sixty pole-lengths against sixty leagues. |
| S2A.9-S2A.10 Sisters and gifts for seven auras (A 136-148 and uncounted lines) | No counterpart in the standard version (compare Humbaba's offer of timber, V 171-177) | The whole trick; Gilgamesh's sisters Enmebaragesi and Peshtur; flour, water, sandals, gems. |
| S2A.11 The blow; binding (A 149-152 and uncounted lines) | The fight, V 141-165 (p. 41) | A false kiss and one punch, then a rope; no battle and no thirteen winds. Huwawa calls Gilgamesh deceitful; in the standard version Humbaba accuses Enkidu of treachery (V 121). |
| S2A.12 Huwawa's plea; Gilgamesh's pity (A 153-162) | Pleas V 166-177, V 185-195; Ugarit fragment (p. 43) | The no-mother, no-father plea is close to the Ugarit lines. Gilgamesh pities him here; in George's standard version he gives no answer and Humbaba offers service and timber. |
| S2A.13 Enkidu's warning; beheading (A 163-179 and uncounted lines) | V 178-183, about V 196-204, V 261-262, V 279-280, V 285-290 (pp. 42-45) | Enkidu alone cuts off the head in anger; in the standard version Gilgamesh stabs the neck and Enkidu removes heart and lungs. Huwawa utters no curse. |
