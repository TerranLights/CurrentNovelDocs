# Sumerian 4. Gilgamesh and the Netherworld ("In those days, in those far-off days"), and the Akkadian Tablet XII: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 2 ("The Sumerian Poems of Gilgamesh"), pp. 131-150; George's note on Tablet XII at the end of chapter 1, p. 97. Public-domain wording for Tablet XII: Thompson 1928, Tablet 12. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How this file is organized.** Part A: the Sumerian poem, scenes S4.1 to S4.18. Part B: Tablet XII, the Akkadian rendering of the poem's second half as George translates it, scenes XII.1 to XII.6; each of those scenes is compared both with the Sumerian poem and with the standard version. The elements list, open questions, leads and Differences table at the end cover both parts.

## George's synopsis (pp. 131-134, paraphrased)
- Ancient readers named the poem after its opening words. It was a popular copying text in the Old Babylonian schools at Nippur and Ur. It is the only Sumerian Gilgamesh poem that starts with a myth of origins (p. 131).
- The myth: shortly after the gods shared out the cosmos, a great storm broke. Enki was on his way by boat to the Netherworld, George thinks to settle in his own realm, the freshwater deep (Abzu); hail filled his boat and the waves beat at it. The same storm uprooted a willow on the Euphrates bank (p. 131).
- Inanna found the willow, carried it home to Uruk, planted it and waited, hoping for furniture from its wood. Evil creatures moved into it, and she grieved. Her brother Utu, the Sun God, heard her story and did nothing; Gilgamesh heard it, took his weapons, cleared out the creatures, cut the tree down, gave her the timber and made two toys from what was left. Scholars disagree about the toys; George's best guess is a ball and a mallet (p. 131).
- Gilgamesh and Uruk's young men play all day until the men are exhausted, and their women spend the day carrying them food and drink. Next day, as the game starts again, the women complain (George assumes to the gods, comparing Tablet I 73ff.), and the toys drop through a hole into the Netherworld. Gilgamesh weeps. Enkidu, his servant, offers to fetch them (p. 132).
- Gilgamesh warns him about Ereshkigal's dark realm: to survive among the dead he must treat them with respect, keep a low profile and attract no notice. He will see Ereshkigal herself, lying stretched out in endless grief for her son Ninazu, stripped, clawing her skin and tearing her hair (p. 132).
- Enkidu ignores all of it and is held captive. Enlil at Nippur refuses Gilgamesh; Enki at Eridu agrees, and has Utu bring Enkidu's shade up as the Sun rises out of the Netherworld at daybreak. The friends embrace, and Gilgamesh questions Enkidu at length about life below; George calls this exchange the poem's high point (p. 132).
- George's reading of the dialogue: a dead man's thirst is eased by the fresh water his family pours for him, so the more sons, the better his lot; those with no children fare worst. Next come those who cannot be buried with their bodies whole (lepers, the sick, the violently killed); one recension may add a moral note about sons who dishonored their parents. Stillborn infants are compensated with comfort. The burnt vanish as smoke and never arrive below, so their ghosts could never be appeased and were the most dreaded; burning was the worst end of all and caps Enkidu's report (pp. 132-133).
- One manuscript tradition stops there. Tablets from Ur go on, and their references to history point to a later addition from Girsu. Enkidu reports that the dead of Sumer and Akkad, Girsu's above all, are crowded out by Amorites, who keep them from the spots where fresh-water offerings come down and leave them foul water. George connects this to the end of the third millennium BC, when Ur's Third Dynasty fell to Amorite pressure and an Elamite invasion and rulers of nomadic Amorite origin took over (p. 133).
- Learning that his own parents share this fate, Gilgamesh turns to the duties a son owes the dead: he makes statues of his ancestors, sets up mourning rites for them and teaches the same rites to Girsu's people, so that the dead are satisfied and leave the living alone. Early administrative records name a place called the "Bank of Gilgamesh" where Girsu's rulers made offerings to their dead kin (p. 133).
- The poem's second half, from line 172 on, was translated into Akkadian in antiquity and became Tablet XII of the Babylonian Gilgamesh series. In that version Gilgamesh also appeals to the Moon God, besides Enlil and Ea (= Enki) (p. 134).

## Part A. The Sumerian poem

### S4.1. Prologue: the primeval age and Enki's stormy voyage (lines 1-26, p. 134)
**Where and when:** mythic time, just after creation and the sharing-out of the universe; the opening formula sets it in the distant past. Enki is on the water, sailing toward the Netherworld. No human setting yet.
**Who:** narration only. Named: An, Enlil, Ereshkigal, Enki. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. A three-part time formula: far-off days, far-off nights, far-off years (1-3).
2. The world's first things had appeared and been put in order; the sanctuaries had eaten bread and the land's ovens had been lit (4-7).
3. Sky and earth had been separated, each from the other, and mankind had received its name (8-10).
4. An claimed the sky, Enlil the earth, and the Netherworld was handed to Ereshkigal as her dowry (11-13).
5. Enki, called "the father," set out by boat for the Netherworld; the sailing line is said twice (14-16).
6. Hail struck him: small stones like hammer-stones, large ones like stones for crushing reeds (17-20).
7. The hail piled up inside the hull of his boat; the poet compares the heaps to turtles (21-22).
8. The water at the bow snapped at him like wolves; the water at the stern lashed like lions that kill (23-26).

**Specifics:** time words: days, nights, years. Sanctuaries and ovens. Ereshkigal's share is a dowry. Enki's titles: father, lord. Two sizes of hail, likened to hammer-stones and reed-crushing stones. Animal images: turtles (heaped hail), wolves (bow water), lions (stern water). Bow and stern of the boat.

**Short quotations:**
- "In those days, in those far-off days" (1): the opening formula and ancient title. It also opens Enki's speech in "The Death of Gilgamesh" (M 69-71, p. 153).

**Gaps:** none. The word for "crushing" (20) is in italics (uncertain).

**Repetitions:** lines 11-26 (the three gods' shares, Enki's voyage, the hail, wolves and lions) come back almost unchanged in Inanna's two retellings, at about 54-69 (to Utu) and 98-113 (to Gilgamesh). There, lines 1-10 are replaced by a short clause about the time when fates were fixed and the land was rich, and the gods "made off with" their shares instead of taking them.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. Its prologue (I 1-46, pp. 1-2) praises Gilgamesh and Uruk's wall; there is no creation myth and no voyage of Ea (Enki). Names: An and Enki here, Anu and Ea there.

### S4.2. The willow in Inanna's garden (lines 27-46, pp. 134-135)
**Where and when:** the Euphrates bank, then Uruk, in Inanna's sacred garden. Same moment as the storm in S4.1. The scene then covers five, then ten years of growth.
**Who:** an unnamed woman (in her own retelling she names herself Inanna, 80); three creatures. The woman speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. A willow grows on the bank of the sacred Euphrates and drinks from the river (27-29).
2. The south wind rips it out by the roots and breaks its branches; the river floods over it (30-31).
3. A woman who heeds what An and Enlil have said lifts the tree and carries it into Uruk, to Inanna's sacred garden (32-35).
4. She plants it and waters it using her foot rather than her hand (36-37).
5. The woman (to herself) asks how long she must wait for a sacred chair to sit on and a sacred bed to lie in (38-39).
6. Five years pass, then ten: the trunk grows thick and the bark cannot be split (40-41).
7. Three creatures settle in it: at the roots a snake immune to spells nests; in the branches the Lion-Bird raises its young; in the trunk a demon-girl makes her dwelling (42-44).
8. Inanna, usually the laughing, glad-hearted maiden, weeps (45-46).

**Specifics:** tree: willow. Place: bank of the Euphrates (called holy). Wind: south. Destination: Uruk, Inanna's garden (called pure). Planted and watered with the foot. Furniture hoped for: a throne and a bed (both called pure). Growth: 5, then 10 years. Bark unsplittable. Creatures by part of the tree: roots, the Snake-that-Knows-no-Charm; branches, the Lion-Bird and brood; trunk, the Demon-Maiden. Inanna's epithet: the laughing maiden, "holy." The Lion-Bird is the Babylonian Anzû, pictured as an eagle with a lion's head or as a winged horse, living in the mountains (Glossary, p. 223).

**Short quotations:**
- "a pure throne" (38): her wish, repeated in both retellings (81, 125).

**Gaps:** the second half of line 40 (the ten-year phrase) is restored, matching the wording of the two retellings (83, 127).

**Repetitions:** 27-46 recur, in the first person, inside Inanna's retellings at about 70-89 and 114-133.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version.
- **Names:** Inanna is the Sumerian name of Ishtar, Uruk's goddess in the standard version (Glossary, p. 223).
- **Creatures:** the Lion-Bird occurs in the standard version only incidentally: Enkidu says he would have set one as a door guardian at Ebabbara (VII 53, p. 55), and the figure who drags him below in his dream has a face like a Lion-Bird's (VII 169, p. 58). A snake steals the plant of youth at XI 305-307 (p. 96); it is not this tree's snake.
- **Events:** the standard version's tree-felling is the cedar cut in the Forest of Cedar to make a door for Enlil's temple (V 307-323, p. 46), not a willow cut for Inanna's furniture.

### S4.3. Dawn: Inanna tells Utu, who does not help (lines 47-90, pp. 135-136)
**Where and when:** daybreak: the sky lightens, birds sing together, and the Sun God steps out of his chamber (47-49). Some unstated time after the tree's ten years of growth. Place not given.
**Who:** Inanna; Utu, the Sun God, her brother, called "Young Hero." Inanna speaks; Utu is silent.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Daybreak; the Sun God comes out of his chamber (47-49).
2. Inanna → Utu (addressing him as brother): she tells the whole story again in the first person, from the age when fates were fixed and the gods divided the world, through Enki's voyage and the hail, to the willow, her planting it, her wish for chair and bed, and the three creatures (52-87).
3. Narration: Inanna weeps (88-89).
4. Utu gives her no help (90).

**Specifics:** dawn details: lightening horizon, chorus of birds, the Sun leaving his chamber. Inanna is Utu's sister and is called "holy" (50); Utu is "Young Hero Utu" (51, 90). In her retelling she calls herself a woman who heeds An (75) and names herself Inanna (80).

**Short quotations:**
- "did not help her" (90): the refusal formula. Its other forms: Gilgamesh helps her (135), Enlil does not help Gilgamesh (230), Enki helps him (238).

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** the dawn passage (47-50) recurs at 91-94. Inanna's speech (52-87) repeats 11-44 with the changes noted under S4.1; her speech in S4.4 repeats this one almost exactly.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version.
- **Names:** Utu is the Sumerian name of Shamash (Glossary, p. 224).
- **Who speaks / outcome:** in the standard version the Sun God is Gilgamesh's protector throughout (for example, Ninsun prays to him on Gilgamesh's behalf, III 45-106, pp. 23-25). When Ishtar is insulted in Tablet VI she goes to her parents, Anu and Antu, not to Shamash (VI 81-86, p. 49).

### S4.4. Dawn again: Inanna tells Gilgamesh, who agrees to help (lines 91-135, pp. 136-138)
**Where and when:** daybreak, with the same dawn passage as in S4.3. Whether this is the next morning or later is not said. Place not given.
**Who:** Inanna; Gilgamesh, called "the warrior." Inanna speaks; Gilgamesh answers with action, not words.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Daybreak, birdsong, the Sun God leaves his chamber (91-93).
2. Inanna → Gilgamesh (addressing him as brother): the same first-person account, from the fixing of fates and Enki's voyage to the creatures in the tree (96-131).
3. Narration: Inanna weeps (132-133).
4. Once his sister has told him this, Gilgamesh, her brother, helps her (134-135).

**Specifics:** Gilgamesh's title here is "the warrior" (95). She calls him brother (96), and the narration calls them sister and brother (134-135), the same kinship words used for Inanna and Utu in S4.3.

**Short quotations:**
- "helped her in this matter" (135): the positive form of Utu's refusal at 90.

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** 91-94 repeat the dawn passage of 47-50. 96-131 repeat Inanna's speech to Utu (52-87) almost exactly; the only visible change is the punctuation before the two "how long" questions (125-126).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version.
- **Who speaks / outcome:** the standard version's encounter runs the other way. Ishtar asks Gilgamesh to marry her; he refuses and insults her by listing her past lovers; she gets the Bull of Heaven from Anu (VI 1-114, pp. 47-50). Here Inanna asks her "brother" for help and gets it.
- **Names:** Inanna here, Ishtar there.

### S4.5. Gilgamesh clears the tree and makes the ball and mallet (lines 136-150, p. 138)
**Where and when:** at the tree in Inanna's garden in Uruk, straight after S4.4. No time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; the men of Uruk who came with him; the three creatures. Inanna receives the wood. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh straps on a belt of fifty pounds, which to him weighs no more than thirty shekels (136-137).
2. He picks up his bronze campaign axe, which weighs seven talents and seven pounds (138-139).
3. He strikes the snake at the roots (140).
4. The Lion-Bird collects its young and leaves for the mountains (141).
5. The Demon-Maiden deserts her home in the trunk (142). All of them escape to the wastelands (143).
6. Gilgamesh pulls the tree up by the roots and breaks off its branches (144).
7. The townsmen with him trim the branches and tie them into bundles (145-146).
8. He gives Inanna, his sister, timber for her throne and her bed (147-148).
9. For himself he makes a ball from the root and a mallet from a branch (149-150).

**Specifics:** belt: 50 pounds, carried as if it were 30 shekels. Axe: bronze, "for expeditions," 7 talents 7 pounds. Where the creatures go: the Lion-Bird to the mountains; all three to the wastelands. Timber: throne and bed for Inanna. Root → ball; branch → mallet.

**Short quotations:**
- "its root he made into his ball" (149)

**Gaps:** none in the text. "Belt" (136) is in italics (uncertain). The snake is struck; whether it dies is not said. On the toys, George reports no agreement among scholars and calls a ball and mallet the likeliest reading (p. 131).

**Repetitions:** the uprooting and branch-breaking (144) repeat what the south wind did at 30.

**Compared with the standard version:** no direct counterpart in the standard version. Nearest parallels:
- **Felling a tree to furnish a god's house:** after Humbaba's death Gilgamesh fells cedars, Enkidu picks the timber and makes a door for Enlil's temple at Nippur, and they tie the logs into a raft (V 307-323, p. 46; Enkidu remembers the door at VII 37-46, pp. 54-55). Here the tree is a willow in Uruk, Gilgamesh works with Uruk's men (Enkidu is not there), and the wood becomes Inanna's throne and bed.
- **Weapon weights:** the Old Babylonian Yale lines that George uses to fill a gap in Tablet II give axes of three talents each, with each hero carrying ten talents (Y 165-171, p. 19). Here: one axe of seven talents seven pounds, and a fifty-pound belt.
- **Creatures driven off:** no counterpart; in the standard version the forest's guardian, Humbaba, is killed (Tablet V, pp. 36-46).
- **Ball and mallet:** no counterpart.

### S4.6. The game in the city square; ball and mallet fall into the Netherworld (lines 151-164, pp. 138-139)
**Where and when:** Uruk's main square. A whole day of play until evening, then the next dawn.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (called the boaster); the city's young men; a group of sons of widows; mothers and sisters; the widows; the young girls. Speakers: the widows' sons (a groan).

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh, eager for a ball, starts playing in the square and shows off (151-152).
2. The city's young men long for the ball too (153; George indents this line).
3. Gilgamesh rides on the backs of a band of widows' sons (154).
4. The widows' sons groan about their necks and hips (155).
5. Mothers bring their sons bread; sisters bring their brothers water (156-157).
6. Toward evening Gilgamesh marks the spot where the ball stopped, picks it up and takes it home (158-160).
7. At dawn he climbs onto the men's backs again at the mark (161).
8. Because of the widows' complaint and the young girls' outcry (162-163), both ball and mallet drop to the bottom of the Netherworld (164).

**Specifics:** the city square. Mounts: sons of widows. Groaned-over body parts: neck, hips. Bread from mothers, water from sisters. Evening; a drawn mark; the ball taken to his house. Dawn. Complainers: widows, young girls. The toys fall to the Netherworld's bottom.

**Short quotations:**
- "O my neck! O my hips!" (155)

**Gaps:** none in the text. The poem does not say whom the widows and girls complain to, or how their complaint makes the toys fall. George assumes the gods, comparing Tablet I 73ff., and has the toys fall through a hole (p. 132).

**Repetitions:** none within the scene.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the oppression of Uruk in Tablet I (I 63-93, pp. 3-4), as George points out (p. 132).
- **Events:** in Tablet I Gilgamesh harasses the young men with no cause, wears out his companions with his contests, and keeps sons from fathers, daughters from mothers and brides from bridegrooms (I 65-76). The complaint reaches heaven; Ishtar and then Anu listen, and the gods have Aruru make Enkidu to match him (I 74-104, pp. 4-5). Here the complaint ends with the toys sinking into the Netherworld; no new companion is made, since Enkidu is already Gilgamesh's servant.
- **Who complains:** Tablet I names the warrior's daughter and the young man's bride (I 77, 92); here, widows and young girls.
- **George's comment (Introduction, p. xlix):** among the explanations offered for Gilgamesh's tyranny in Tablet I, the exhausting game of this poem is probably the closest; the Akkadian line about his weapons suggests the games became more warlike in the Babylonian tradition.
- **No counterpart:** riding on the widows' sons; the ball and mallet.

### S4.7. At the Gate of Ganzir: Gilgamesh weeps for his ball and mallet (lines 164-176, p. 139)
**Where and when:** Ganzir, the gate that leads into the Netherworld, right after the fall, the same morning.
**Who:** Gilgamesh alone. He speaks a lament with no named listener.

**Play-by-play:**
1. He reaches for the toys with something (the word is lost), then with his hand, then with his foot, and fails each time (the indented "With …" line, then 165-166).
2. He sits down at the gate (167).
3. He weeps and sobs (168).
4. Gilgamesh (lament) cries out to the ball and mallet: he had not yet enjoyed the ball or played the game enough (169-171).
5. He wishes he had left the ball that day at the carpenter's workshop, and calls on the carpenter's wife, whom he treats as a mother, and the carpenter's daughter, whom he treats as a little sister (172-174).
6. He asks who will fetch the ball back from the Netherworld and the mallet back from Ganzir (175-176).

**Specifics:** Ganzir, called the entrance to the Netherworld (167); in George's Glossary, the first of its seven gates (p. 222). Three attempts: a lost word, hand, foot. The carpenter's workshop, wife and daughter. The word "game" (171) is in italics (uncertain).

**Short quotations:**
- "who will bring it up for me?" (175)

**Gaps:** one word lost in the indented line before 165.

**Repetitions:** the pairing "ball … Netherworld" with "mallet … Ganzir" runs through the poem (175-176, 179-180, 225, 233).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, which never names Ganzir. Tablet XII starts at the equivalent of line 172 (XII.1); George says the Akkadian covers the poem from line 172 on (p. 134).

### S4.8. Enkidu volunteers; Gilgamesh's rules for the Netherworld (lines 177-205, pp. 139-140)
**Where and when:** the Gate of Ganzir, continuing S4.7, the same day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; his servant Enkidu. Both speak.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (addressing him as "my lord"): asks why he weeps and is heartsick, and promises to bring the ball and mallet back up that very day (177-180).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: if he is going down today, he must listen and obey (181-184). Then six rules, each with its reason:
   - no clean clothes, or the dead will mark him as a stranger (185-186);
   - no scented oil from a flask, or the smell will draw them around him (187-188);
   - no throwing-stick, or those it hits will crowd around him (189-190);
   - no cornel-wood rod in his hand, or the shades will tremble at him (191-192);
   - no sandals, since he must make no noise down there (193-194);
   - no kissing the wife or son he loved and no striking the wife or son he hated, or the Netherworld's outcry will catch him (195-199).
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu, describing who he will find: the goddess called the Mother of Ninazu, lying down, her shining shoulders and breast bare, scraping with her nails and pulling out her hair (200-205).

**Specifics:** forbidden things: clean garment, sweet oil in a flask, throwstick, cornel rod, sandals. Family below: a loved and a hated wife, a loved and a hated son. The Mother of Ninazu: bare shoulders and breast, nails used like a rake, hair torn out like leeks. George identifies her as Ereshkigal, grieving forever for her son Ninazu (p. 132); Ninazu means "Lord Doctor" (Glossary, p. 223). Those who would surround Enkidu are not named; only "the shades" are (192).

**Short quotations:**
- "the outcry of the Netherworld" (199): the danger that strikes at 221.

**Gaps:** none. "Rake" (204) is in italics (uncertain). ⚠️ George indents 204-205 (the nails and the hair) like lines from fuller manuscripts, but the margin number 205 falls on the second of them, so it is unclear whether they belong to the traditional count. Tablet XII has nothing for these two lines (XII.2).

**Repetitions:** the six rules are repeated as narrative, one by one, in S4.9 (206-221).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version: there Enkidu never enters the Netherworld alive. Nearest passage: Enkidu's dream of the dead (VII 165-208, pp. 58-60).
- **Events:** in the dream he is dragged down as a captive and sees the House of Dust, where the dead eat dust and clay and wear feathers like birds (VII 187-190). Here he goes down by choice, after careful instructions.
- **Who:** in the dream Ereshkigal sits with her scribe Belet-ṣeri reading a tablet to her, and she lifts her head to ask who brought him (VII 203-208). Here she is the Mother of Ninazu, lying bare and tearing her hair; Belet-ṣeri is absent.
- **Names:** "Mother of Ninazu" here; queen of the Netherworld, Ereshkigal by name, in Tablet VII (VII 203).

### S4.9. Enkidu goes down and breaks every rule; the Netherworld seizes him (lines 206-221, p. 140)
**Where and when:** the Netherworld, right after S4.8.
**Who:** Enkidu; the unnamed "they"; the shades; the wives and sons he loved and hated. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu ignores his master's instructions (206).
2. He wears clean clothes and is marked as a stranger; he anoints himself with oil and the smell draws them around him (207-210).
3. He throws a throwstick and those it hits surround him; he carries a cornel rod and the shades tremble (211-214).
4. He wears sandals and makes noise (215-216).
5. He kisses the wife and son he loved and strikes the wife and son he hated (217-220).
6. The Netherworld's outcry seizes him (221).

**Specifics:** as in S4.8, item by item. Enkidu is called the servant of "his master" (206).

**Short quotations:**
- "paid no attention" (206)

**Gaps:** none. "Noise" (216) is in italics (uncertain).

**Repetitions:** 207-221 turn the rules of 185-199 into actions, in the same order. The description of the Mother of Ninazu (200-205) is not repeated here; Tablet XII does repeat it (XII.3).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels, in outcome only, Enkidu's death in Tablets VII-VIII.
- **Events and cause:** in the standard version the gods in council decide that Enkidu must die because the pair killed the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba (the Hittite paraphrase George prints for the lost opening of Tablet VII, p. 54); he sickens and dies (VII 252-267, pp. 60-61). Here he is alive, goes down by choice, and is held for breaking the Netherworld's etiquette.
- **Who seizes him:** in his death-dream a man with Lion-Bird features grabs him by the hair, turns him into a dove and leads him to the house of darkness (VII 168-184, pp. 58-59); George's synopsis calls this figure the Angel of Death (p. 54). Here the Netherworld's outcry takes him.

### S4.10. Seven days: Gilgamesh laments (Mê-Turan manuscript only) (Mt1 10-16, p. 140)
**Where and when:** a week after Enkidu went down ("the seventh day" after that "wicked day"). Place not given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the king. He speaks; no listener named.

**Play-by-play:**
1. For seven days Enkidu does not come back up (Mt1 10-11).
2. The king cries out and weeps bitterly (Mt1 12).
3. Gilgamesh (lament): the Netherworld has taken his favorite servant, his loyal companion, his adviser (Mt1 13). Not Namtar, not Azag, not the merciless officer of Nergal took him, and he did not die in battle; the Netherworld took him (Mt1 14-16).

**Specifics:** time: the seventh day. Enkidu's titles: favorite servant, steadfast companion, counselor. The powers that did not take him: Namtar ("Doom," Ereshkigal's minister and the angel of Death, Glossary p. 223), Azag (a demon, p. 221), Nergal's sheriff (Nergal: god of plague and war, later Ereshkigal's husband, p. 223), and battle, "the field of men."

**Short quotations:**
- "Namtar did not seize him" (Mt1 14): opens the refrain repeated in each petition (S4.11, S4.12) and in Tablet XII.

**Gaps:** in Mt1 13-14 a possessive word and the closing "seized him" are restored. "Pitiless" (Mt1 15) and "men" (Mt1 16) are in italics (uncertain).

**Manuscript note:** all seven lines are indented and numbered by the Mê-Turan manuscript (Mt1), not by the traditional count, so they occur only in that manuscript (chapter convention, p. 99).

**Book illustration:** drawing 16 stands here, captioned with the battle line. The list of illustrations identifies it as a detail of the "Royal Standard" mosaic panel from Ur, twenty-seventh century BC, British Museum. It shows war chariots rolling over fallen men.

**Repetitions:** Mt1 13-16 recur unchanged inside both petitions (S4.11, S4.12).

**Compared with the standard version:** a loose parallel to Enkidu's last days and Gilgamesh's mourning (VII 252-267, pp. 60-61; VIII 1-56, pp. 61-63).
- **Numbers:** a seven-day wait here. In Tablet VII Enkidu's illness is counted day by day for twelve days (VII 255-260); in Tablet X Gilgamesh says he wept for him six days and seven nights (X 235, p. 82, mostly restored).
- **Who speaks / content:** in Tablet VII the dying Enkidu himself contrasts his end with death in battle, which wins a man his name (VII 264-267, partly restored). Here Gilgamesh is the one who says Enkidu did not die in battle.
- **Names and roles:** Enkidu is servant, companion and adviser here; in the standard version, friend and brother. There Namtar is the Netherworld's vizier, who receives a grave gift for Enkidu (VIII 152-156, p. 66), not a power that seizes.

### S4.11. Gilgamesh petitions Enlil at Ekur; Enlil does not help (lines 222-230, p. 141)
**Where and when:** Ekur, Enlil's temple at Nippur (Glossary, pp. 222-223). After S4.9 (or S4.10); no time given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the warrior and son of the goddess Ninsun; Enlil. Gilgamesh speaks; Enlil gives no answer in words.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh goes by himself to Ekur and weeps in front of Enlil (222-224).
2. Gilgamesh → Enlil (addressing him as Father): his ball and mallet fell below, into the Netherworld and Ganzir; he sent Enkidu after them and the Netherworld took him (225-226). He adds the lament's lines: his favorite servant, loyal companion and adviser (an indented Mê-Turan line, Mt1 22); not Namtar, not Azag, not Nergal's officer; not battle; the Netherworld took him (227-229).
3. Enlil does not help him (230).
4. Gilgamesh sets out for Eridu (230).

**Specifics:** Gilgamesh's epithets: warrior, son of Ninsun. He goes alone. Enlil is addressed as "Father." Ekur means "Mountain House" (Glossary, p. 222).

**Short quotations:**
- "Father Enlil did not help him" (230)

**Gaps:** in the Mt1 22 line, the word "servant" and the phrase naming the Netherworld are restored. "Pitiless" (228) is in italics (uncertain).

**Repetitions:** 227-229 repeat the lament of S4.10. The whole plea (225-229) is said again to Enki in S4.12.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, where nobody asks the gods to return Enkidu. Nearest passage: at Enkidu's sickbed Gilgamesh says he will pray to the great gods (Shamash, Anu, Enlil, Ea) and have a gold statue made; Enkidu replies that what Enlil has said cannot be taken back (VII 75-89, p. 56). Here Gilgamesh really goes to Enlil.
- **Who / outcome:** in the standard version Enlil is the god who decides Enkidu must die (Hittite paraphrase, p. 54); here he simply refuses to help.
- **Names:** Ninsun is Gilgamesh's mother in both.

### S4.12. Gilgamesh petitions Enki at Eridu; Utu brings up Enkidu's phantom (lines 231-243, pp. 141-142)
**Where and when:** Eridu, Enki's temple; then the opening into the Netherworld. The opening is made when the Sun God passes through, which George's synopsis places at sunrise, as the Sun comes up out of the Netherworld (p. 132).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Enki; Utu, "Young Hero," son of Ningal; Enkidu as a phantom. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Enki.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh goes alone to Eridu and weeps in front of Enki (231-232).
2. Gilgamesh → Enki (as Father): the same plea he made to Enlil, word for word (233-237; the Mê-Turan "favorite servant" line is restored here).
3. Enki helps him (238).
4. Enki → Utu: when he opens a way into the Netherworld in his course as Sun God, he is to bring Gilgamesh's servant up to him (239-241).
5. Utu opens the Netherworld and brings the servant up to Gilgamesh as a phantom (242-243).

**Specifics:** Eridu: Enki-Ea's cult city in the far south of Babylonia (Glossary, p. 222). Ningal: the Moon's wife and the Sun's mother (Glossary, p. 223). The means: an opening into the Netherworld. Enkidu comes up in phantom form. George adds "(as the Sun God)" as a parenthesis in 240.

**Short quotations:**
- "in the form of his phantom" (243)

**Gaps:** the Mê-Turan "favorite servant" line is wholly restored in this plea. "Pitiless" (236) is in italics (uncertain).

**Repetitions:** 233-237 repeat 225-229. The help formula ties together Utu (90), Gilgamesh (135), Enlil (230) and Enki (238).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version: Enkidu's shade never returns there. After Enkidu's death Gilgamesh goes looking for Uta-napishti instead (IX 1-8, pp. 68-69).
- **Names:** Enki and Utu here; Ea and Shamash there. In the standard version Ea is the god who finds a way out (he warns Uta-napishti of the Deluge by speaking to a reed fence, XI 19-31, pp. 86-87); here Enki finds the way to bring Enkidu up.
- **Who:** here Enlil refuses and Enki helps; in the Flood story Enlil sends the destruction and Ea saves the survivor, then defends what he did before Enlil (XI 172-196, pp. 91-92).

### S4.13. Reunion: "sit down and weep"; the body in the Netherworld (lines 244-254, p. 142)
**Where and when:** wherever the phantom comes up (not stated), right after S4.12.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the lord; Enkidu's phantom. Both speak.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh embraces and kisses him; the two tire themselves out with questions and answers (244-245).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (as "my friend"): asks whether he saw how the Netherworld is ordered, and wishes he could hear about it (246-247).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: if he tells, Gilgamesh should sit down and weep. Gilgamesh agrees to sit and weep (248-249).
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the penis that once gave Gilgamesh's heart such joy is now like an old beam eaten by grubs; it longed to reach the vagina, which is now like a dusty crack in the earth (250-253).
5. Gilgamesh cries "Ah, woe!" and sits down in the dust (254).

**Specifics:** the wording makes the embrace one-sided: Gilgamesh hugs and kisses Enkidu (244). Gilgamesh calls Enkidu "my friend" (247), although the narration has just called him servant (243). Images: an old beam full of grubs (251); a crack in the ground full of dust (253). Gilgamesh sits in the dust (254).

**Short quotations:**
- "sit you down and weep" (249)

**Gaps:** none.

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's question "Did you see …?" starts the formula used throughout S4.14 and S4.15.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (no meeting with Enkidu's shade). Partial parallels:
- **Dust and clay:** in Enkidu's dream the dead live on dust and eat clay (VII 188, p. 59); Gilgamesh says his beloved friend has turned to clay (X 245, p. 82).
- **Grief:** in Tablet VIII Gilgamesh tears out his hair and strips off his finery (VIII 63-64, p. 64); here he sits in the dust.
- **Names:** "friend" in both; "servant" (243) has no standard-version counterpart.

### S4.14. Questions, part 1: fathers of sons, the heirless, the childless and the unmarried (lines 255-278, pp. 142-143)
**Where and when:** the dialogue of S4.13 continues.
**Who:** Gilgamesh asks; Enkidu's phantom answers. Each exchange has four steps: Gilgamesh asks whether Enkidu saw a certain person; Enkidu says he did; Gilgamesh asks how that person is doing; Enkidu describes it.

**Play-by-play (Gilgamesh's question, Enkidu's report of what he saw):**
1. One son: the father weeps bitterly over a peg fixed in his wall (255-256).
2. Two sons: he sits on a pair of bricks eating a loaf (257-258).
3. Three sons: he drinks from a waterskin hung on a saddle (259-260).
4. Four sons: he is as glad as a man who owns a four-donkey team (261-262).
5. Five sons: like a good scribe, he is nimble-handed and walks into the palace freely (263-264).
6. Six sons: he is as glad as a man with a plough team in harness (265-266).
7. Seven sons: he sits on a throne just below the gods and hears the court's business (267-268).
8. No heir: his loaf is as hard as a fired brick (269-270).
9. The palace eunuch: he leans in a corner like a discarded stick of some kind (271-272).
10. The woman who never gave birth: thrown aside like a flawed pot; no man wants her (273-274).
11. The young man who never undressed his wife: he weeps over a rope he finished making by hand (275-276).
12. The young woman who never undressed her husband: she weeps over a reed mat she finished weaving by hand (277-278).

**Specifics:** sons counted one to seven. Objects: a wall peg; two bricks; a loaf; a waterskin on a saddle; a team of four donkeys; a scribe and the palace; a plough in harness; a throne; a kiln-fired brick; an alala-stick; a pot; a hand-made rope; a hand-made reed mat.

**Short quotations:**
- "How does he fare?" (255): the third step of the question formula.

**Gaps:** none. In italics (uncertain): "kiln-fired" (270), "eunuch" (271), "scratched alala" (272), "defective" (274).

**George's reading (p. 132):** each son adds to the fresh water poured for a dead father, easing his thirst; the childless have no one above to pour for them and suffer most.

**Repetitions:** the question formula repeats for every category here and in S4.15 and S4.17.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version. Enkidu's dream there sorts the dead by rank, not by family: crowned kings who once served Anu and Enlil; en-, lagar-, lustration-, lumahhu- and gudapsû-priests; Etana; Shakkan; Ereshkigal and her scribe (VII 194-205, p. 60). Sons and offerings for the dead are not mentioned.

### S4.15. Questions, part 2: violent deaths, offenders, the forgotten, the fortunate, and the man burnt to death (about lines 279-303, pp. 143-144)
**Where and when:** the dialogue continues. The Nippur version ends with this scene (p. 144).
**Who:** Gilgamesh asks; Enkidu answers, in the same formula.

**Play-by-play (Gilgamesh's question, Enkidu's report):**
1. Someone who fell off a roof: his bones cannot be mended.
2. A man a lion ate: he keeps crying out for his hand and his foot.
3. A man the Storm God struck dead: he jerks like an ox while maggots eat him.
4. The leper (287): he is given food and drink apart from others, eats bitter food and drinks brackish water, and lives outside the town; he too jerks like an ox under the maggots (288-290).
5. A man hit by a mooring-pole (295): an obscure answer about calling for one's mother or crying out in grief as a mooring-pole is drawn up, and something about his daily bread, with a word lost.
6. A man who ignored what his parents told him: he screams without pause about his self and his limbs.
7. A man his parents cursed: he has no heir, and his ghost keeps wandering.
8. A man who treated his god's name lightly: his ghost has bitter food and brackish water.
9. A man killed in battle: his parents cannot hold his head in their laps; his wife weeps.
10. A shade with nobody to make funerary offerings: he eats pot scrapings and bread crusts thrown into the street.
11. Stillborn infants who never had names: they play at gold and silver tables laden with syrup and ghee.
12. A man who died "the death of his god" (300), an expression George leaves unexplained: he rests on a divine bed (301).
13. A man burnt to death: Enkidu reports that the man was absent: no ghost of his is below, since his smoke rose to the sky (302-303).

**Specifics:** causes of death: a fall from a roof, a lion, the Storm God, leprosy, a mooring-pole, battle, fire. Food and drink: bitter food and brackish water (the leper, the man who slighted his god); scrapings and thrown-away crusts (the forgotten); syrup, ghee, tables of silver and gold (the stillborn). The leper lives outside the town. The battle-dead man's wife weeps.

**Short quotations:**
- "his smoke went up to the heavens" (303): the last line of the Nippur version.

**Gaps:** one word lost in the mooring-pole answer, and Enkidu's "I saw him" in that question is restored. "Twitches" (Storm God answer) is in italics (uncertain). In the battle answer, "father" is George's emendation (his footnote 5).

**Line numbers ⚠️:** the margin numbers jump here: 278, then a "V" beside the lion question, then 287 (leper question), 290 (end of the leper answer), 295 (mooring-pole question), 300 ("death of his god" question). More lines stand between 295 and 300 than the count allows. George does not explain the "V" or the jumps in this section; they look like lines merged from more than one manuscript tradition.

**George's reading (pp. 132-133):** these are people who could not be buried whole, because of leprosy, illness or a violent death. One recension may add a moral strand with the men who wronged their parents. Stillborn babies, taken too early, are made up for with luxury. The burnt go up in smoke and never reach the Netherworld; no rite could quiet their ghosts, so they were feared most, and burning was the worst possible end.

**Repetitions:** the question formula. The ox-and-maggots answer comes twice (Storm God, leper); bitter food with brackish water comes twice (leper, the man who slighted his god).

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart for the catalogue in the standard version. Partial parallels:
- **Death in battle:** the dying Enkidu sets his own death against death in combat, which makes a man's name (VII 264-267, p. 61, partly restored). Here the man killed in battle does badly: his parents cannot cradle his head.
- **Maggots:** Gilgamesh says he would not release Enkidu's body for burial until a maggot fell from its nose (X 236-237, p. 82, restored).
- **Lions:** Gilgamesh kills lions while wandering (IX 9-18, p. 69); no one is eaten by one in the standard version.
- **Offerings to the dead:** the nearest standard-version material is Enkidu's funeral, with gifts for the Netherworld's gods and a final table of offerings (VIII 92-218, pp. 64-68); the hunger of the neglected dead is not described.

### S4.16. The Mê-Turan ending: the king turns his mind to the Living One's Mountain (3 unnumbered lines, p. 144)
**Where and when:** right after the dialogue, in the Mê-Turan version only.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called king and lord. No speeches.

**George's note (p. 144):** the Nippur version stops suddenly at 303. A second version, from the provincial town of Mê-Turan, has three extra lines that join this poem to the start of "Gilgamesh and Huwawa."

**Play-by-play:**
1. His heart is struck and his mind falls into despair.
2. The king goes looking for life.
3. The lord sets his mind on the Living One's Mountain.

**Specifics:** "the Living One's Mountain." In "Gilgamesh and Huwawa" the Living One seems to be Huwawa, the spirit guarding the Cedar Mountain (George, p. 105). Version A of that poem opens with almost the same line about the lord and the Living One's Mountain (p. 106).

**Short quotations:**
- "The king searched for life" (second line)

**Gaps:** the possessive "his" is restored in the third line.

**Repetitions:** the third line is nearly the first line of "Gilgamesh and Huwawa," version A (p. 106).

**Compared with the standard version:** in wording, parallels Gilgamesh's quest after Enkidu's death (IX 1-8, pp. 68-69) and the prologue's line about his roaming the world in search of life (I 41, p. 2).
- **Order of events:** in the standard version the expedition to Humbaba's Forest of Cedar (Tablets II-V) comes before Enkidu's death, and the later quest is for Uta-napishti (IX 3-8). This link sends Gilgamesh toward Huwawa's mountain after Enkidu's report from below.
- **Names:** Huwawa (the Living One) here; Humbaba there.

### S4.17. The Ur version continues: the oath-breaker, Girsu and the Amorites, Gilgamesh's own parents (UET VI 58, reverse, about lines 7-16, pp. 144-145)
**Where and when:** the same dialogue, carried on after the burnt man in a third recension known from Ur tablets (p. 144).
**Who:** Gilgamesh asks; Enkidu answers, in the same formula.

**Play-by-play (Gilgamesh's question, Enkidu's report):**
1. A man who swore an oath and cheated a god: at the Netherworld's upper end, where the poured water arrives, he drinks and is still thirsty.
2. A Girsu citizen at the place where his parents mourn (uncertain rendering): each such man faces a thousand Amorites; his shade can neither shove them away with its hands or charge through them with its chest; at the place of libations the Amorite drinks first.
3. The sons of Sumer and Akkad: they drink dirty water from a place of slaughter.
4. Gilgamesh asks where his own father and mother live and how they are: Enkidu saw them; both drink the same dirty water from the place of slaughter.
5. A gap follows ("* * *").

**Specifics:** the top of the Netherworld, where water offerings are received (twice). Number: a thousand Amorites per man. Peoples and places: Girsu, the Amorites, the sons of Sumer and Akkad. Gilgamesh's parents are not named here; elsewhere in the poem he is son of the goddess Ninsun (222 and the last line).

**Short quotations:**
- "a thousand Amorites" (rev. 11, about)

**Gaps:** in the last exchange, "How do they fare?", the words for "the two" and the closing "dirty water" are restored. In italics (uncertain): "but stays thirsty," "at the place of sighs," "precedence." The gap after this passage hides what comes between the report and the conclusion; George's synopsis says Gilgamesh, prompted by filial duty, makes statues of his ancestors (p. 133), but the making is not in the surviving text.

**Manuscript note:** these lines are indented and use the Ur tablet's own numbering (UET VI 58, reverse 9-10 and 15 in the margin), not the poem's.

**George's reading (p. 133):** probably a later addition from Girsu. The Amorites keep the dead of Sumer and Akkad, and Girsu's especially, away from the spots where fresh-water offerings arrive. He links it to the fall of the Third Dynasty of Ur to Amorite raids and an Elamite invasion at the end of the third millennium BC.

**Repetitions:** the question formula; the dirty-water answer twice (Sumer and Akkad; Gilgamesh's parents); the place of libations twice.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version, which never names Girsu, the Amorites, or Sumer and Akkad. There Gilgamesh's mother is the goddess Ninsun and Lugalbanda is named as a father figure ("Wild bull of Lugalbanda," I 35, p. 2); neither is placed in the Netherworld.

### S4.18. The Ur version's conclusion: statues, mourning rites, clear water (UET VI 60, reverse, lines about 1-17, p. 145)
**Where and when:** Uruk and Gilgamesh's palace; then a morning, as the Sun God comes out; then nine days of rites. Time since S4.17 unknown (a gap intervenes).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Uruk's young men and women; Kullab's elders and matrons; Girsu's citizens. Gilgamesh speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. He brings "them" back to Uruk, his city (rev. 1-2); two lines later "them" turns out to be statues (rev. 6).
2. Equipped with gear, a hatchet and a spear, he goes in and sets them happily in his palace (rev. 3-4).
3. Uruk's young men and women and Kullab's elders and matrons look at the statues and are glad (rev. 5-6).
4. Facing the rising Sun God, he gives orders (rev. 7-8).
5. Gilgamesh (to his dead parents): he promises them clear water to drink (rev. 9).
6. Before midday, with something lost about the weaving of their crowns, Gilgamesh carries out the mourning rites (rev. 10-11).
7. He keeps the rites for nine days; Uruk's young men and women and Kullab's elders and matrons weep (rev. 12-13).
8. Things went as he had foretold: he pushed back (uncertain) the citizens of Girsu (rev. 14-15), and he repeats his promise of clear water to his parents (rev. 16).
9. Closing praise of Gilgamesh, warrior and son of Ninsun (rev. 17).

**Specifics:** statues (how many is not said); hatchet and spear; the palace; the rising Sun God; clear water (against the dirty water of S4.17); nine days of rites; crowns being woven; mourners: Uruk's young men and women, Kullab's elders and matrons (Kullab is a district of Uruk, Glossary p. 223).

**Short quotations:**
- "sweet is your praise!" (rev. 17): the closing doxology of this version.

**Gaps:** "Uruk" (rev. 1) and "inside" (rev. 3) are restored. A word is lost before the crown-weaving (rev. 10). "Pushed back" (rev. 15) is in italics (uncertain). ⚠️ George's synopsis says Gilgamesh teaches Girsu's people the same rites (p. 133); the translated line has him pushing back Girsu's citizens, marked uncertain.

**Manuscript note:** indented lines numbered by the Ur tablet (UET VI 60, reverse 5, 10, 15 in the margin).

**George's note (p. 133):** early administrative records mention the "Bank of Gilgamesh," where Girsu's rulers made offerings to their dead relatives.

**Repetitions:** the clear-water promise twice (rev. 9, 16); the list of mourners twice (rev. 5, 13).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Enkidu's funeral in Tablet VIII.
- **Statues:** Gilgamesh promises Enkidu a gold statue (VII 82, p. 56) and has craftsmen make one, with lapis lazuli eyebrows and a gold chest (VIII 66-72, p. 64). Here the statues are of his parents.
- **Sun God:** at Enkidu's funeral every grave gift is shown to the Sun God (VIII 132-188, pp. 65-67), and the last surviving lines lay out an offering table at dawn (VIII 213-218, p. 68). Here Gilgamesh gives his orders facing the rising Sun.
- **Numbers:** nine days of rites here; in Tablet X Gilgamesh wept for Enkidu six days and seven nights (X 235, p. 82).
- **Mourners:** in the standard version Gilgamesh calls on Uruk's young men and elders to mourn Enkidu (VIII 9, 21, 42-43, pp. 61-63). Kullab and Girsu are not named there.
- **Outcome:** no counterpart for the parents' water or Girsu's citizens.

## Part B. Tablet XII (the Akkadian version)

**What George says Tablet XII is:**
- In his view Tablet XII, the last tablet of the "Series of Gilgamesh," does not belong to the epic. It is an Akkadian prose rendering of the later part of "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld," probably added because its subject fits: it describes the Netherworld, where Gilgamesh after his death ruled over the dead (chapter 1, p. 97; this is George's whole Tablet XII entry there).
- The series has twelve tablets; the twelfth translates the second half of a Sumerian poem line by line in prose. It lasted into the first millennium BC, although the Sumerian original did not. A few scholars have argued it belongs in the epic, but most, George says, think it was attached only because the material is related (Introduction, p. xxx).
- It gives a version of lines 172-303 that differs slightly from the Sumerian (p. 145), and in it Gilgamesh also petitions the Moon God (p. 134).
- He describes it as an appendix that says more about the fate of the dead (Introduction, p. lii).

**Thompson 1928.** Thompson's public-domain translation of Tablet 12 (pp. 57-60) was made long before George's and reads several passages very differently; each scene below notes where.

### XII.1. Gilgamesh laments the lost ball and mallet (XII 1-5, p. 146)
**Where and when:** not stated; the tablet opens in the middle of the lament (= Sumerian 172-176). The Gate of Ganzir is not mentioned.
**Who:** Gilgamesh. He speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh (lament): today he wishes he had left his ball at the carpenter's workshop; he calls on the carpenter's wife, like a mother to him, and daughter, like a little sister, wishing he had left it there (XII 1-3).
2. Today ball and mallet have both fallen into the Netherworld (XII 4-5).

**Specifics:** carpenter's workshop, wife and daughter; ball and mallet.

**Short quotations:** none; Thompson's wording does not match this passage (see below).

**Thompson 1928 reads** the opening as a seine and a net that have passed through "the Architect's dwelling," with a speaker asking what he can do (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, ll. 1-5, p. 57); in his note he takes this as a veiled reference to Enkidu's death (note 57:1). George reads the lost playthings and the carpenter's household.

**Gaps:** restored: most of the carpenter's wife's line (XII 2), most of the daughter's line (XII 3), "my" (XII 4) and "down" (XII 5).

**Repetitions:** none within the scene.

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (172-176):** same content, slightly reworded ("today" for "on this day"; "had I left it" for "had it stayed"). Missing here: the question who will bring the toys up; the mallet falls into the Netherworld, not into Ganzir. Nothing before Sumerian line 172 (prologue, willow, game, fall, Gate of Ganzir) has an equivalent in Tablet XII.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.7).

### XII.2. Enkidu volunteers; Gilgamesh's rules (XII 6-30, p. 146)
**Where and when:** continuing XII.1, the same day.
**Who:** Enkidu; Gilgamesh. Both speak.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (as "master"): asks why he wept so heartsick and promises to bring the ball and mallet back up from the Netherworld today (XII 6-9).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: if he goes down he must obey these instructions (XII 10-12). The rules and reasons (XII 13-27):
   - no clean clothing, or he will be recognized as a stranger;
   - no sweet oil from a flask, or the smell will draw a crowd;
   - no throwstick, or those it hits will surround him;
   - no staff in his hand, or the shades will tremble;
   - no sandals, so as to make no noise;
   - no kissing the wife or son he loves, no striking the wife or son he hates, or the Netherworld's outcry will catch him.
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: the Mother of Ninazu lies there, her shining shoulders uncovered, her breasts bare like stone flasks (XII 28-30).

**Specifics:** clean garment, flask of sweet oil, throwstick, staff, sandals; the Mother of Ninazu's bare shoulders, and breasts compared to stone flasks.

**Short quotations (Thompson 1928):**
- "She who dead lieth, / She who dead lieth, / Mother of Ninazu" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, about ll. 28-30, p. 58).

**Thompson 1928 reads** this as "The Mourner's Duty": advice to a mourner, not to Enkidu, on how to avoid being haunted (heading and note 57:1, p. 57). His items: no clean raiment, no sweet oil "from the cruse," no bow set to the earth (for George's throwstick), no stick in the hand, no shoe on the foot, no kissing or chastising of wife and child, or "the mourning of earth" will hold the mourner (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, ll. 15-27, p. 57). He reads the last image as a bosom "drawn, like the lard cruse" (p. 58), where George has breasts bare like stone flasks.

**Gaps:** restored: Enkidu's verb of answering (XII 6), "sick" (XII 7), the promise verbs (XII 8-9), Gilgamesh's verb of answering (XII 10), the "if you go down" clause (XII 11), the "pay heed" clause (XII 12), the opening of the garment rule (XII 13), "you" (XII 14).

**Repetitions:** the rules come back as narrative in XII.3.

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (177-205):**
- **Wording:** "master" for "lord"; a staff for the cornel rod; prohibitions phrased as "must not"; the wives and sons are those "you love" and "you hate," in the present tense.
- **Events missing:** Gilgamesh's extra line asking Enkidu to listen to his word (184); the Mother of Ninazu's raking nails and torn-out hair (204-205, the lines George indents in the Sumerian).
- **Changed image:** breasts bare like stone flasks, where the Sumerian says no linen covers her breast.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.8; the nearest is Enkidu's dream of the House of Dust, VII 165-208, pp. 58-60).

### XII.3. Enkidu goes down, breaks every rule, and sees the Mother of Ninazu (XII 31-50, pp. 146-147)
**Where and when:** the Netherworld, right after XII.2.
**Who:** Enkidu; the unnamed "they"; the shades; his wives and sons; the Mother of Ninazu. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Going down, Enkidu disregards Gilgamesh's instructions (XII 31-32).
2. Clean clothes: he is recognized as a stranger. Oil: the smell draws them around him (XII 33-36).
3. Throwstick: the shades tremble (an extra, indented line) and those hit surround him (XII 37-38).
4. Staff: the shades tremble (XII 39-40).
5. Sandals: he makes noise (XII 41-42).
6. He kisses the wife and son he loved and strikes the wife and son he hated (XII 43-46).
7. The Netherworld's outcry seizes him (XII 47).
8. The Mother of Ninazu lies there, shoulders uncovered, breasts bare like stone flasks (XII 48-50).

**Specifics:** as in XII.2, now in the past tense.

**Short quotations (Thompson 1928):**
- "the mourning of earth which did hold him enthralléd" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, l. 46, p. 58).

**Thompson 1928 reads** this passage with Gilgamesh, not Enkidu, as the one who breaks the rules, deliberately, so that Enkidu's ghost will rise and haunt him (his heading and note 57:1, pp. 57-58). He too repeats the Mother of Ninazu lament after the broken rules (p. 58).

**Gaps:** heavily restored. In brackets: the opening "As Enkidu" and "to the Netherworld" (XII 31); the reference to Gilgamesh's instructions (XII 32); "a clean garment" (XII 33); "him as a" (XII 34); "of it" (XII 36); "Netherworld" and "the shades" (XII 37 and the extra line); "struck" (XII 38); "his" (XII 39); "the shades did" (XII 40); most of XII 41-46; part of the repeated "the one who lies" (XII 48); "her" (XII 49). Fully preserved: XII 35, XII 47, XII 50.

**Manuscript note:** the extra trembling line after the throwstick is indented, and the margin count (XII 40 at the staff's trembling) leaves it out.

**Repetitions:** XII 33-47 turn the rules of XII 13-27 into actions; XII 48-50 repeat XII 28-30 in the past tense.

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (206-221):**
- **Events added:** the Mother of Ninazu is described again as narrative after the capture (XII 48-50), where the Sumerian has her only in Gilgamesh's warning; and the extra trembling of the shades after the throwstick.
- **Wording:** in Tablet XII they "identified" him, where the Sumerian has them read the clean garment as a stranger's mark; a staff for the cornel rod.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.9: there Enkidu dies of illness after the gods' sentence, Tablet VII, pp. 54-61).

### XII.4. Enkidu does not return; Gilgamesh petitions Enlil, Sîn and Ea (XII 51-78, pp. 147-148)
**Where and when:** Ekur (Enlil's temple), then Ur (Sîn's), then Eridu (Ea's). No time marker.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the king and son of Ninsun; Enlil; Sîn; Ea. Only Gilgamesh speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narration: Enkidu does not come back up to the world above. Not Namtar, not Asakku, not Nergal's merciless officer took him, and he did not fall in battle; the Netherworld took him (XII 51-54).
2. The king, weeping for his servant Enkidu, goes alone to Ekur (XII 55-56).
3. Gilgamesh → Enlil (as Father): his ball and mallet dropped into the Netherworld today; Enkidu went down for them and the Netherworld took him; then the refrain (not Namtar, Asakku or Nergal's officer; not battle) (XII 57-62).
4. Enlil does not say a word in reply (XII 63).
5. Gilgamesh goes alone to Ur, Sîn's temple, and repeats the plea to Sîn as Father (XII 64-70).
6. Sîn, too, says nothing (XII 71).
7. Gilgamesh goes alone to Eridu, Ea's temple, and repeats the plea to Ea as Father (XII 72-78).

**Specifics:** three gods and three temples: Enlil at Ekur, Sîn at Ur (the Moon God's cult city, Glossary p. 224), Ea at Eridu. Gilgamesh: king, son of Ninsun (XII 55). Enkidu: "his servant" (XII 55).

**Short quotations (Thompson 1928):**
- "(But) no answer did Enlil, the father vouchsafe." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, about l. 62, p. 59)

**Thompson 1928 reads** the same three visits (Enlil at Ekur, the Moon-god, Ea), with the Moon-god's silence restored (ll. 55-77, p. 59). Differences in his reading: the plea begins with the image of the seine and net striking Gilgamesh "down to the earth," not the fallen ball and mallet; the refrain names "Namtar ... nor fever ... nor the Croucher, Nergal, the ruthless" (ll. 51-53, p. 58), where George has Asakku and Nergal's officer; and the first refrain is a cry by Gilgamesh for Enkidu to rise (l. 50), not narration.

**Gaps:** restored: the verb in XII 51; parts of the refrain in XII 52-53, including Nergal's name; Enlil's name and the closing of Gilgamesh's statement in XII 57-59; "battle" (XII 62); the whole journey to Ur (XII 64); the end of XII 66; small words in XII 67, 69 and 70; all of XII 71-72 (Sîn's silence and the journey to Eridu); most of XII 73-78. Ur and Sîn's temple are restored; only the vocative to Sîn (XII 65) names him in plain text.

**Repetitions:** the plea three times, nearly identical (XII 57-62, 65-70, 73-78); the refrain four times, counting the narrator's (XII 52-54).

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (Mt1 10-16, 222-237):**
- **Events added:** a third petition, to Sîn, the Moon God, at Ur (George, p. 134). The refrain is spoken first by the narrator (XII 51-54), not by Gilgamesh in a lament.
- **Events missing:** the seven-day wait and the lament naming Enkidu servant, companion and adviser (the Mê-Turan lines). The Sumerian's weeping before each god becomes a single mention of Gilgamesh weeping for his servant at the start (XII 55).
- **Names:** Asakku for Azag; Ea for Enki; Sîn added. Ganzir is absent; both toys fall into the Netherworld.
- **Who speaks / wording:** Enlil and Sîn give no reply, where the Sumerian says Enlil gave no help; "where men do battle" for the Sumerian's "battle, the field of men."

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.11). Sîn appears there only in passing; for example, Gilgamesh prays to Sîn and Ishtar for protection at a mountain pass (IX 12, p. 69, restored).

### XII.5. Ea has Shamash open a chink; Enkidu's shade comes up (XII 79-87, p. 148)
**Where and when:** Eridu, then the opening into the Netherworld, as Shamash rises into the sky (a conjectural restoration).
**Who:** Ea; Shamash, "Young Hero," son of Ningal; Enkidu's shade. Ea speaks.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Ea helps Gilgamesh (XII 79).
2. Ea → Shamash: as he climbs into the sky, he might perhaps open a small crack in the Netherworld and bring Enkidu's shade up as a phantom (XII 80-83).
3. At Ea's word … (the rest of the line is lost) (XII 84).
4. Shamash rises, opens a crack in the Netherworld and brings Enkidu's shade up as a phantom (XII 85-87).

**Specifics:** a "chink," a small crack (XII 82, 86); Enkidu's shade in phantom form (XII 83, 87); Shamash, son of Ningal (XII 85).

**Short quotations (Thompson 1928):**
- "Oped, then, a hole in the earth, and the spirit of Enkidu issued / Forth from the earth like a wind." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, ll. 85-86, p. 59)

**Thompson 1928 reads** Ea's order as given to Nergal, "the warrior-hero," who opens the hole, so that Enkidu's spirit may come up and speak with his brother (ll. 78-85, p. 59). George reads Shamash, the Sun God, as the one who opens the crack; Thompson's spirit comes out "like a wind," a detail not in George's rendering.

**Gaps:** restored: Ea's helping (XII 79); "he spoke" and Shamash's name (XII 80); the line on Ningal and the rising, with the rising conjectural (XII 81); "you can open" and "in the Netherworld" (XII 82); the purpose clause and phantom phrase (XII 83); Ea's name (XII 84); the rising again, conjectural (XII 85). Lost: the end of XII 84. "Perhaps" (XII 82) is in italics (uncertain). Fully preserved: XII 86-87.

**Repetitions:** XII 86-87 carry out what Ea asked in XII 82-83.

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (238-243):**
- **Who speaks / wording:** Ea's request is tentative (perhaps a crack can be opened), where Enki gives a direct order to bring the servant up; "a chink" for "an opening"; "the shade of Enkidu" for "his servant"; "as a phantom" for the Sumerian's phantom form.
- **Names:** Ea and Shamash for Enki and Utu; Ningal is the Sun God's mother in both.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.12).

### XII.6. Reunion and the questions; the tablet's text ends with the forgotten dead (XII 88-119, lacuna, XII 144-153, pp. 148-150)
**Where and when:** wherever the shade appears, right after XII.5.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the lord; Enkidu's shade. Both speak. The question formula is shorter than in the Sumerian: Gilgamesh asks whether Enkidu saw someone, Enkidu says he did and describes him; the step "How does he fare?" is not printed.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The two embrace and kiss each other, sharing thoughts and trading questions (XII 88-89).
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (as "my friend"): begs him, twice, to tell what he saw of the Netherworld's rules (XII 90-91).
3. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he cannot tell; if he did, Gilgamesh should sit down and weep. Gilgamesh says he will sit down and weep (XII 92-95).
4. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: grubs eat the penis that once delighted him, like an old beam; the vagina he once touched with joy is full of dust, like a dusty crack in the earth (XII 96-99).
5. Gilgamesh cries "Ah, woe!" and throws himself into the dust; the line is said twice, once of "the lord," once of Gilgamesh (XII 100-101).
6. One son: a peg stands in his wall, and he weeps bitterly over it (XII 102-103).
7. Two to seven sons: two, he sits on two bricks with a loaf; three, he drinks from a waterskin on a saddle; four, glad as a man who owns a donkey team; five, deft as a fine scribe and at ease in the palace; six, glad as a ploughman; seven, enthroned just below the gods, hearing the court's business (XII 104-116).
8. No heir: his round loaf is like a fired brick (about XII 117).
9. The palace eunuch: propped in the corner like a fine standard, and something more, now lost (about XII 118-119).
10. Gap ("* * *"), XII 120-143.
11. Someone struck by a mooring-pole: an exclamation of pity for his parents; he roams when pegs are drawn out (about XII 144-145).
12. A man who died his god's death: he rests on a bed among the gods and drinks clean water (about XII 146-147).
13. A man killed in battle: his parents honor his memory while his wife mourns him (about XII 148-149).
14. A man whose corpse was left lying in the open country: his shade has no rest in the Netherworld (XII 150-151).
15. A shade with nobody to bring funerary offerings: he eats pot scrapings and bread crusts thrown into the street (XII 152-153). George's translation of the tablet ends here.

**Specifics:** the Netherworld's "rules" (XII 91); the old beam and grubs; the dusty crack in the ground (XII 96-99); sons one to seven; bricks, a loaf, a waterskin, a donkey team, a scribe, the palace, a ploughman, a throne; the round loaf like a fired brick; the eunuch like a standard; pegs pulled out; clean water; the corpse on the plain; pot scrapings and street crusts.

**Short quotations (Thompson 1928):**
- "I will not tell thee, (yea,) I will not tell thee" (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, l. 90, p. 60)
- "Lees of cup, and broken bread / Thrown into the street he eateth." (Thompson 1928, Tablet 12, last lines, p. 60)

**Thompson 1928 reads** the body lines as about a "friend" and a "bride" whom Gilgamesh once fondled, now entered by the worm and filled with dust (ll. 93-96, p. 60); the eunuch's answer as "As a pillar beautiful / [Props?] an inner portico" (l. 118); the mooring-pole question as about one who falls from a pole, with a plug removed (l. 145); the battle-dead man's parents as raising his head while his wife grieves (ll. 149-151); and the unburied corpse as lying "in desert" (p. 60). His gaps fall in about the same places as George's (about 17 and 25 lines).

**Gaps:** much restoration in XII 96-118: most of the body lines and of the sons' questions and answers, and all of the seven-sons, no-heir and eunuch questions, are in brackets. The rest of the eunuch's answer is lost. A gap of about 24 lines follows (XII 120-143; the margin goes from XII 119 to XII 145). After it, restored: Enkidu's "I saw him" (mooring-pole), the parents' words and "he" (XII 145), "died" and "I saw him" (XII 146), "with the gods" (XII 147), "saw him," "weeps" and "him" (XII 148-149). XII 150-153 survive whole. In italics (uncertain): "kiln-fired" (XII 118), "he is propped in" (XII 120).

**Line numbers ⚠️:** between the markers XII 115 and XII 119 the book prints seven lines for five numbers, so the numbers for the no-heir and eunuch exchanges are approximate.

**Repetitions:** the question formula; the cry of woe and the fall into the dust twice (XII 100-101).

**Compared with the Sumerian poem (244-303):**
- **Events added:** Enkidu first refuses to tell (XII 92). Both men embrace each other. Gilgamesh throws himself into the dust twice instead of sitting down once. A new category: the man whose corpse lies unburied in the open (XII 150-151).
- **Events missing (in the surviving text):** the childless woman, the unmarried young man and woman, the fall from a roof, the lion, the Storm God, the leper, the offenders against parents and against a god, the stillborn and the burnt man. Some may have been in the gap (XII 120-143); the text does not show which.
- **Outcomes changed:** the man killed in battle: his parents honor his memory and his wife weeps, where the Sumerian says his parents cannot cradle his head (with "father" emended). The man who died his god's death also drinks clean water. The mooring-pole answer is different (pegs pulled out; he wanders).
- **Order:** Tablet XII goes mooring-pole, death of his god, battle, unburied corpse, no offerings, and stops; the Sumerian goes mooring-pole, parents, god's name, battle, no offerings, stillborn, death of his god, burnt man.
- **Wording:** "rules" of the Netherworld for "the way things are ordered"; both body lines address things Gilgamesh once touched (the Sumerian has the penis asking to reach the vagina); "owner of a donkey team" for "a team of four donkeys"; "ploughman" for "plough in harness"; a round loaf for a loaf; the eunuch like a standard instead of like an alala-stick.
- **Ending:** neither the Mê-Turan link to the Living One's Mountain nor the Ur conclusion (statues, rites, Girsu) appears.

**Compared with the standard version:** no counterpart in the standard version (see S4.13-S4.15). As a plain narrative fact, Tablet XII has Enkidu alive and going down for the toys, although in Tablets VII-VIII he has already died and been mourned; George holds that Tablet XII is not part of the epic (p. 97; Introduction, p. xxx).

- Five years, ten years of growth (S4.2).
- 50 pounds, 30 shekels, 7 talents 7 pounds (S4.5).
- One day of play, an evening, the next dawn (S4.6); two daybreaks (S4.3, S4.4).
- Seven days' wait before the lament (S4.10, Mê-Turan only).
- One to seven sons (S4.14, XII.6).
- A thousand Amorites per man (S4.17).
- Nine days of mourning rites; "before midday" (S4.18).
- Two gods petitioned in the Sumerian, three in Tablet XII (S4.11-S4.12, XII.4).

**Dreams and omens**
- No dreams in this poem. Enkidu's account of the Netherworld is an eyewitness report from a shade, given in dialogue (S4.13-S4.17, XII.6).
- The toys fall after the widows complain and the girls cry out; how is not said (S4.6).

**Customs and rituals**
- Rules for behaving among the dead: no clean clothes, no scented oil, no throwstick, no rod, no sandals, no kissing or striking of family (S4.8).
- Fresh-water offerings by families for their dead; the place where the water offerings arrive (S4.14, S4.17; George p. 132).
- Funerary offerings, and the hunger of shades who get none (S4.15, XII.6).
- Whole burial versus not; burning as the worst end (S4.15; George pp. 132-133).
- Ancestors' statues set up in the palace; nine days of mourning rites; clear water promised to dead parents (S4.18).
- Grief: sitting or throwing oneself in the dust (S4.13, XII.6); weeping before a god (S4.11, S4.12).
- Women bringing bread and water to exhausted men (S4.6).
- The closing praise formula (S4.18).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **What the toys are.** George reports no scholarly agreement and calls a ball and mallet the likeliest reading (p. 131).
- ⚠️ **How the toys fall.** The poem mentions only the widows' complaint and the girls' outcry; George assumes the complaint went to the gods (p. 132).
- ⚠️ **Who "they" are** who would crowd around Enkidu (S4.8, S4.9): not said; only the shades are named.
- ⚠️ **Lines 204-205** (Ereshkigal's nails and hair) are indented like lines from fuller manuscripts, yet carry the margin number 205; Tablet XII lacks them (S4.8).
- ⚠️ **Line numbers in the dialogue** jump (278, "V," 287, 290, 295, 300); George does not explain the "V" (S4.15).
- ⚠️ **The mooring-pole answers** are obscure in both versions, with a word lost in the Sumerian (S4.15, XII.6).
- ⚠️ **The man killed in battle:** "father" is an emendation (footnote 5), and Tablet XII gives the opposite outcome (his parents honor him) (S4.15, XII.6).
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings (italics):** "crushing" (20), "belt" (136), "game" (171), "rake" (204), "noise" (216), "pitiless" and "men" in the refrain, "twitches," "kiln-fired," "eunuch," "scratched alala," "defective," "but stays thirsty," "at the place of sighs," "precedence," "pushed back"; Tablet XII: "perhaps" (XII 82), "kiln-fired," "he is propped in."
- ⚠️ **The Ur conclusion:** the making of the statues falls in a gap, and "them" in the first line is identified only by the mention of statues two lines later. George's synopsis has Gilgamesh teach Girsu's people the rites; the translated line has him push back Girsu's citizens, marked uncertain (S4.18).
- ⚠️ **Gilgamesh's parents in the Netherworld** (S4.17-S4.18) are not named, though the same poem calls him son of the goddess Ninsun. George does not comment.
- ⚠️ **The lost lines of Tablet XII** (XII 120-143) may have held more kinds of dead; which ones is unknown (XII.6).
- ⚠️ **Tablet XII is heavily restored:** George's restorations in XII 1-83 and 96-118 mostly follow the Sumerian wording, and how much of each line survives varies (XII.1-XII.6).
- ⚠️ **Sigla:** "Mt1," "UET VI 58," "UET VI 60" and "rev." are manuscript references that George does not define in this section.
- ⚠️ **Thompson 1928 reads Tablet XII very differently from George 2020:** a seine and net in "the Architect's dwelling" instead of the toys (p. 57); the rules as advice to a mourner, which Gilgamesh himself breaks to raise Enkidu's ghost (pp. 57-58); a bow for the throwstick; "fever" and Nergal himself in the refrain where George has Asakku and Nergal's officer (p. 58); Nergal, not Shamash, opening the hole (p. 59). These are the readings of 1928 scholarship; George's is the base text here.
- ⚠️ **Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), "Tablet twelve,"** says the lost possessions are unclear and that translations differ, naming a drum and a ball among them; George 2020 has a ball and a mallet (and calls this the probable reading, p. 131).
- ⚠️ **Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), "Tablet twelve,"** mentions a suggestion that the tablet comes from an unknown version of the Sumerian story; George 2020 calls it a line-by-line prose translation of a slightly different version of lines 172-303 (pp. 145, xxx). Wikipedia also reports scholars' differing views of the tablet's purpose (an unrelated appendix versus a deliberate closing); George 2020 says most scholars think it does not belong to the epic, while some have argued it does (p. xxx).
- ⚠️ **Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), "Sumerian poems,"** notes that the Sumerian texts write the hero's name as Bilgames; George 2020's translation uses "Gilgamesh" throughout. It also gives the alternative title "Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld," and says the poem is the main source for the Eridu Genesis and for the story of "Inanna and the Huluppu Tree"; George 2020 does not mention either in this section.

## Leads (observations on the text's own structure only)
- Lead: the help/refusal formula organizes the poem in two rounds: Utu refuses and Gilgamesh helps (Inanna's round); Enlil refuses and Enki helps (Gilgamesh's round).
- Lead: Inanna's story is told three times (narration, to Utu, to Gilgamesh), the last two each at a daybreak.
- Lead: Gilgamesh's six rules are given and then broken one by one in the same order; Tablet XII adds the Mother of Ninazu to the breaking sequence.
- Lead: the Sun God appears at each turn: he will not help Inanna, he brings Enkidu up, and in the Ur version Gilgamesh faces him when promising his parents clear water.
- Lead: the dialogue is a graded catalogue: sons from one to seven, then the childless, then those not buried whole or killed by violence, then offenders, then the fortunate (stillborn, death of his god), then the worst (burnt). The Ur version widens it to a city, a people and finally Gilgamesh's own parents (Girsu, Sumer and Akkad, father and mother), and its conclusion answers the dirty water of the last report with a promise of clear water.
- Lead: the poem has three endings: an abrupt stop (Nippur), a link to "Gilgamesh and Huwawa" (Mê-Turan), and Gilgamesh's rites for his parents (Ur).
- Lead: death in battle comes up twice: in the refrain about Enkidu (not killed in battle; taken by the Netherworld) and in the later question about the battle-dead man, which the two versions answer differently.
