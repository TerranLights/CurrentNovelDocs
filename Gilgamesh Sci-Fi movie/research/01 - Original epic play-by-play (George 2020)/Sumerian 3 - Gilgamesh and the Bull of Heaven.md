# Sumerian 3. Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven ("Hero in battle"): play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 2 ("The Sumerian Poems of Gilgamesh"), pp. 122-131. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**Line references.** This poem has no single running line count. George's margin cites the manuscript that supplies each passage: "Ma" (counted lines, for example Ma 5, Ma 140), "A" (column and line, for example A ii 15´), and several "N" sources (for example Nb obv. 4, Nj iii 5´, Nn 31´). The prime mark (´) is George's. These references are given here exactly as printed. Where the lines between two printed Ma markers count out exactly (Ma 1-6, Ma 20-41, Ma 86-113 leaving out the indented lines, Ma 125-130, Ma 135-140), the lines in between are cited by their Ma number. Elsewhere the count between markers does not fit, because the passage is taken from other manuscripts, so lines are cited by the nearest printed reference ("after A i 15´," "between Ma 40 and A i 15´"). Indented lines, which George adds from fuller manuscripts (chapter introduction, p. 99), are noted as such. Scenes are numbered S3.1 and so on.

## George's synopsis (pp. 122-123, paraphrased)
- Ancient title: "Hero in battle." Subject: Gilgamesh's feud with Inanna (Ishtar in Akkadian) and the two heroes' fight with the Bull of Heaven. George treats it as the Sumerian analogue of epic Tablet VI, though the openings of the two stories have little in common (p. 122).
- **Sources and reliability (p. 122):** fewer copies survive than of the Akka and Huwawa poems, so it was less popular. Its two best witnesses are not from Nippur, the usual source of good copies: one comes from the provincial town of Mê-Turan on the Diyala (modern Tell Haddad, northeast of Baghdad), the other is said to come from Dilbat (modern Dailem, south of Babylon). Four extract tablets, three still unpublished, are of unknown origin. The manuscripts follow different versions, use unusual spellings and contain many errors, so parts cannot be translated at all. George stresses that his version is far from definitive.
- **Plot (pp. 122-123):** a hymn opens the poem. Ninsun, Gilgamesh's divine mother, then instructs him in his duties, sheep-shearing and fishing among them, and the instructions are retold as narrative. Inanna then tries to hold Gilgamesh in Eanna, her temple, as master of the house and ruler of the people; George likens this to her marriage proposal in Tablet VI.
- Gilgamesh reports her advances to Ninsun and mentions that Inanna waylaid him by the gate under the wall, the traditional haunt of prostitutes (George cites epic Tablet VII 117). Ninsun tells him to refuse Inanna's gifts, evidently so they do not make him soft.
- On a later errand, rounding up animals for the goddess's pens, he runs into Inanna again and brusquely orders her aside. After a break in the text, Ninsun likens Inanna to a bloodthirsty beast of prey.
- If George reads the text correctly, Inanna is then weeping in her room in heaven. An, her father, asks the reason; she seems to say she cannot give herself to the one she loves. She calls Gilgamesh a wild bull on the loose, which recalls epic Tablet I 63-64. She asks An for the Bull of Heaven to kill him. An says the Bull grazes in the sky (it is Taurus) and would starve on earth. Inanna screams in a tantrum until An yields. She brings the Bull down to Uruk, where it eats every plant and drains the river.
- **The broken passage (p. 123):** George reconstructs it from versions that plainly differ. Lugalgabangal, Gilgamesh's minstrel, sees the Bull amid the damage and goes in to tell his master, who is at a feast and is not put off his enjoyment. After eating, Gilgamesh arms for battle, sends his mother and sister to offer sacrifices in Enki's temple, and swears to cut the Bull up and feed its meat to the poor.
- **The fight (p. 123):** with Inanna watching from the wall, the two heroes attack. Enkidu finds the Bull's weak point and Gilgamesh finishes it off. Gilgamesh throws part of a leg at Inanna, who dodges, and the leg smashes the wall; he wishes he could do the same to her. The Bull is butchered as he swore and the meat goes to the poor, but the horns are given to Inanna in Eanna.

## Scenes

### S3.1. Hymnic prologue (Ma 1-6, with indented lines Nb obv. 4 and 6, p. 124)
**Where and when:** the singer's opening, outside the story.
**Who:** the singer, who announces his song. Speaker: the singer.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The singer announces a song about Gilgamesh, ending every line with the same refrain about the hero in battle. Line by line he praises him as lord, as a lord with a black beard, as fair-limbed, as the mightiest young lord and as a master (restored) of wrestling and contests of strength (these two are indented lines from manuscript Nb), as a merry one (uncertain), and as one who storms against evildoers (Ma 1-6).

**Specifics:** a black beard (Ma 3). Wrestling and trials of strength (Nb obv. 6). Raging against wrongdoers (Ma 6).

**Short quotation:** "let me sing his song" (Ma 1): the refrain. The ancient title, "Hero in battle," comes from it (p. 122).

**Gaps:** the word "Expert" and the second half of Nb obv. 6 are restored. "Merry one" (Ma 5) is uncertain.

**Repetitions:** the refrain closes all eight lines.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in Tablet VI**, which opens with Gilgamesh cleaning himself up after the expedition (VI 1-5, p. 47). The nearest passage is the praise of Gilgamesh in the prologue of Tablet I (I 29-62, pp. 2-3), where his bearded cheeks are as dark as lapis lazuli (I 59, p. 3). Here the beard is simply black.

### S3.2. Ninsun's instructions for Gilgamesh's duties (from Nc 4´ to just before Ma 14, including Na 1´, p. 124)
**Where and when:** not stated (Uruk). No time of day.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called king and lord; his mother, called the mother who bore him (named Ninsun only later). Speaker: his mother.

**Play-by-play:**
1. His mother speaks to the king (Nc 4´).
2. Mother → Gilgamesh: go down to the river, take dust in your hand (the verb is a conjectural restoration) and wash in the river (after Nc 4´).
3. Mother → Gilgamesh: go into the juniper grove and learn the work; a line about an axe, "the weapon," is broken (before Na 1´).
4. Mother → Gilgamesh: go into the Gipar house and shear the lordly sheep, then sit (uncertain) on the plank at the front (Na 1´ and the next line).
5. Mother → Gilgamesh: out in the marsh, bend the oars into the water, and let them dip for him as thickly as a dense reed-bed; a last line, with a simile, is broken (the lines before Ma 14).

**Specifics:** the river; dust in the hand for washing; the juniper grove; an axe, called "the weapon"; the **Gipar** house, which the Glossary explains as Inanna's private rooms in her temple Eanna (p. 222); the lordly sheep; the plank at the front; the marsh; oars; a reed-bed. George sums the duties up as including sheep-shearing and fishing (p. 122).

**Short quotation:** "shear the lordly sheep" (Na 1´)

**Gaps:** the first "take" (after Nc 4´) is a conjectural restoration; the second "take," before the seat, is uncertain; the axe line and the last line have losses.

**Repetitions:** the whole set of instructions is repeated as narrative in S3.3 (George, p. 122).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Ninsun gives no such instructions in Tablet VI. The only shared point is washing: in Tablet VI Gilgamesh washes his matted hair, cleans his gear and puts on his crown (VI 1-5, p. 47), and that is when Ishtar notices him (VI 6).

### S3.3. Gilgamesh carries out the tasks (from Ma 14 to Nedg c, including Na 10´, p. 124)
**Where and when:** the broad courtyard of Inanna's house, the juniper grove, the Gipar house, the marsh, the courtyard and the Grand Court.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, called the king and the crusher of heads; his mother (first line only). No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. He gives his mother something (Ma 14, an indented line). ⚠️ What he gives is lost.
2. In the broad courtyard of Inanna's house Gilgamesh takes up a scythe (uncertain; "took his" is restored) (after Ma 14).
3. The king goes into the juniper grove and learns (uncertain) the work (Na 10´).
4. In the Gipar house he shears a lordly sheep (after Na 10´).
5. He sits on the front plank and spreads his net in the marsh (George supplies "his net"); the king bends the oars into the water and they dip for him like a dense reed-bed; one more simile line is broken (after Na 10´; "in the water for him" is restored).
6. The crusher of heads enters the courtyard, the Grand Court, without a fight; the rest of the line is lost (to Nedg c).

**Specifics:** the broad courtyard of Inanna's house; a scythe (uncertain); the juniper grove; the Gipar house; a lordly sheep; the front plank; a net (George's supplied word); oars; the Grand Court (Nedg c). Epithet: crusher of heads.

**Short quotation:** "The crusher of heads" (the line before Nedg c)

**Gaps:** "took his" and "in the water for him" are restored; "scythe" and "he learnt" are uncertain; "(his net)" is George's supplied word; Ma 14 and Nedg c end in losses.

**Repetitions:** repeats S3.2 as narrative, in the same order (grove, Gipar, plank, marsh, oars), but without the washing.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.**

### S3.4. Inanna's proposal (the line before Ma 20, then Ma 20-27; Ma 24 is also A i 5´; p. 125)
**Where and when:** Inanna looks out from the palace of Abzu; Gilgamesh is apparently in the courtyard (S3.3). The scene opens "At that time," right after S3.3.
**Who:** holy Inanna; Gilgamesh. Speaker: Inanna.

**Play-by-play:**
1. At that moment holy Inanna turns her gaze, from the palace of Abzu, on something whose name is lost (the line before Ma 20, and Ma 20-21; "cast her" is restored twice).
2. Inanna → Gilgamesh: she calls him her wild bull and declares, in a refrain, that he will be her man (uncertain rendering) and she will not let him go; he will sit in judgment in Eanna, give verdicts in the holy Gipar, and judge in Eanna, the temple An loves (Ma 22-26).
3. Inanna → Gilgamesh: he should be the house's lord and she its lady (Ma 27).

**Specifics:** the palace of Abzu (Ma 21); the Abzu is the Ocean Below (Glossary, p. 221). Gilgamesh as her wild bull. The offices: judging in Eanna, verdicts in the holy Gipar, Eanna as An's beloved temple. The refrain that she will not let him go.

**Short quotation:** "I shall not let you go" (Ma 22-26): the refrain of her proposal.

**Gaps:** "cast her" is restored twice; the object of her gaze is lost all three times. "You shall be" is uncertain.

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh repeats the six lines of the proposal word for word to his mother (S3.5), and part of them again later (S3.7).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Ishtar's proposal (VI 6-21, p. 47).
- **What is offered:** in the standard version Ishtar asks him to be her bridegroom and husband (VI 7-9; Thompson 1928 reads "my husband shalt be and I'll be thy consort," Tablet VI, col. i, l. 9, p. 32) and promises a chariot of lapis lazuli and gold with amber horns, a team of lions and huge mules, kings and nobles kneeling to him, tribute, and flocks that bear twins and triplets (VI 10-21). Here she offers him office in her temple, lord to her lady. George compares the two as near equivalents (pp. 122-123).
- **Why she looks:** in the standard version she is struck by his beauty after he has washed and crowned himself (VI 6). Here the object of her gaze is lost.
- **Names:** Inanna, An, Abzu and the Gipar here; Ishtar and Anu in the standard version. Gilgamesh as a wild bull appears in the standard version's prologue (I 30, p. 2), not in Ishtar's proposal.

### S3.5. Gilgamesh tells his mother; Ninsun's counsel (Ma 28-41, indented; then A i 9´-10´ and the lines after them; p. 125)
**Where and when:** before his mother. Gilgamesh says Inanna spoke to him at the city gate, by the foot of the wall.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Ninsun. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Ninsun.

**Note on the text:** the first part of the scene, from Gilgamesh hearing Inanna's words to his mother's reply, is indented, i.e. outside the traditional count; its margin references come from manuscript Ma (Ma 30, 35, 40).

**Play-by-play:**
1. The king hears Inanna and goes to speak to his mother, the goddess Ninsun (Ma 28-30; the verbs are restored).
2. Gilgamesh → Ninsun: three broken lines say that something happened "at the door of the city gate" and by the base of the city wall (Ma 31-33). He then **repeats Inanna's proposal** word for word (Ma 34-39).
3. When he has finished, his mother answers (Ma 40-41).
4. Ninsun → Gilgamesh: Inanna's gifts must not come into his room; the divine Palace Lady must not smother a warrior's strength under cloth (A i 9´-10´).
5. Ninsun → Gilgamesh: she gives him a speech to make to Inanna: Inanna must not stand in his way; he will catch (uncertain) wild bulls and sheep in the mountains to fill her folds and pens, and pile silver, carnelian, lapis lazuli and trinkets into leather bags to fill her house. That, she says, is what he must tell her (the four lines after A i 10´, and an indented closing line).

**Specifics:** the city gate and the base of the wall. Inanna's title, the divine Palace Lady. Cloth smothering a warrior's might. Goods: wild bulls and sheep from the mountains; folds and pens; silver, carnelian, lapis lazuli, trinkets, leather bags.

**Short quotation:** "must not enter your chamber" (A i 9´): said of Inanna's gifts.

**Gaps:** the ends of three lines of Gilgamesh's report are lost; the verbs for hearing, speaking and replying, and the words "O lord," are restored. "Catch" is uncertain. George's synopsis notes that the gate under the wall was where prostitutes worked, comparing Tablet VII 117 (p. 123), and explains Ninsun's ban as a guard against his growing soft (p. 123).

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's report repeats S3.4 word for word. Ninsun's model speech is repeated by Gilgamesh to Inanna in S3.6.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** In Tablet VI Gilgamesh does not consult his mother; he answers Ishtar himself at once (VI 22-79, pp. 48-49). Refusing her gifts compares in substance with his questions there about what he would have to give her and how she would feed and clothe him (VI 24-28). In the standard version the shade of the wall as a prostitute's post appears in Enkidu's curse on Shamhat (Tablet VII; George's reference VII 117, p. 123).

### S3.6. Gilgamesh rebuffs Inanna; her retort (from "Gilgamesh speaks to Inanna" to after A i 15´, p. 126)
**Where and when:** at Gilgamesh's next meeting with Inanna. George's synopsis: as he sets out to round up animals for her pens (p. 123).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Inanna, called the queen. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Inanna.

**Play-by-play:**
1. A printed heading announces Gilgamesh's speech to Inanna.
2. Gilgamesh → Inanna: she must not block his road; he will catch wild bulls and sheep in the mountains to fill her folds and pens and pile treasure in leather bags to fill her house (four lines, exactly as Ninsun gave them).
3. The queen, Inanna, gasps (A i 15´ and the next line).
4. Inanna → Gilgamesh: who told him she lacked wild bulls or sheep from the mountains, or silver, carnelian, lapis lazuli and trinkets? If he catches the animals and piles up the treasure, he should give it to his sisters and his mother.

**Specifics:** the same goods as in S3.5. His "sisters" and mother as the people to receive them (end of her speech).

**Short quotation:** "Who said to you, Gilgamesh" (after A i 15´): the opening of each of her three questions.

**Gaps:** the goods in the first two "if" lines, "silver," and "your" are restored; "catch" is uncertain.

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's speech repeats Ninsun's model (S3.5) word for word. Inanna's three questions throw the same three kinds of goods back at him.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Gilgamesh's refusal of Ishtar (VI 22-79, pp. 48-49).
- **What Gilgamesh says:** in the standard version he rejects her with insults, comparing her to useless or harmful things (a frost that makes no ice, a door that lets in drafts, a shoe that bites its wearer, VI 32-41), and lists the lovers she ruined: Dumuzi, the hoopoe, the lion, the horse, the shepherd turned wolf, and Ishullanu turned into a dwarf (uncertain) (VI 42-79). None of this is here; here he offers to stock her pens and fill her house, and asks her to step aside.
- **Who speaks next:** here Inanna retorts on the spot. In the standard version she says nothing to him and storms up to heaven (VI 80-81).

### S3.7. Gilgamesh reports again; Ninsun's scream (between A i 15´ and A ii 5´, p. 126)
**Where and when:** before his mother. George places this after a break in the text (p. 123).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Ninsun. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Ninsun.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The king goes in to his mother (the two lines saying so are restored) and begins to speak to Ninsun; only his first word, "Mother," survives.
2. Four lines are broken (George's note).
3. Gilgamesh → Ninsun (**retelling**): Inanna told him he would be her man (uncertain), her wild bull, and that she would not let him go.
4. His mother cries out, and her scream brings the sky close to the earth (said twice, once naming Ninsun). The scream spreads over Uruk and Kullab like a blanket or a cloth.
5. Ninsun: she pictures a wolf with a yearling lamb and a raven with a yearling lamb, and asks who mourned for it; "it" catches them, skins them, drinks their blood and pours it out like water from a jug (to A ii 5´).

**Specifics:** a wolf; a one-year-old lamb; a raven; flayed skins; blood poured as from a jug (A ii 5´). Uruk and Kullab covered as with a blanket and cloth.

**Short quotation:** "as if from a jug" (A ii 5´)

**Gaps:** the two lines on his entering and the words "his mother" are restored; four lines are broken; the wolf line has a loss. "Shall be" is uncertain. ⚠️ The text does not say who "it" is; George's synopsis takes Ninsun to be comparing Inanna to a predator (p. 123).

**Repetitions:** Gilgamesh's report repeats the first line of Inanna's proposal (S3.4). The scream formulas (sky brought close to earth; covering like a blanket or cloth) return for Inanna's scream in S3.8.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Ninsun has no part in Tablet VI.

### S3.8. Inanna weeps to An; she screams; An gives her the Bull (A ii 6´ to A iii 25´, with indented lines Np 15´ to Nk obv. 5´, pp. 126-127)
**Where and when:** "in the chamber" (A ii 8´). George: Inanna's chamber in heaven, if he has read the text correctly (p. 123).
**Who:** Inanna (holy Inanna, maiden Inanna); An (Great An). Speakers: An, Inanna.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Inanna weeps and sobs (A ii 6´).
2. An, to the daughter he loves, says something lost; sitting in the room, An (uncertain) speaks to her (A ii 7´-8´; "spoke to her" is conjectural).
3. An → Inanna: he asks his child why she is crying (A ii 9´).
4. Inanna → An: the great bull Gilgamesh runs loose and lives in Uruk; she weeps because she cannot give herself to him; she asks her father for the Bull of Heaven so she can kill the lord Gilgamesh, a wish she repeats three times (A ii 10´-16´).
5. Great An → Inanna: the Bull of Heaven would find nothing to eat, for its pasture is at the horizon, where the sun rises; he will not give it to her (A ii 17´-20´).
6. Inanna → An: then she will scream until the sky comes down to the earth (the two lines after A ii 20´).
7. She screams, and the sky comes close to the earth (said twice). Her scream is terrible. (Indented lines, Np 15´ to Nk obv. 5´:) the scream reaches heaven and earth and covers them like a blanket and a cloth; who could wail like holy Inanna?
8. Great An answers her and gives her the Bull of Heaven (A ii 24´, A iii 25´).

**Specifics:** the chamber (A ii 8´). Gilgamesh as a great bull running loose (A ii 10´-11´). The Bull's pasture at the horizon, where the sun rises (A ii 18´-19´); George: the Bull grazes in the sky because it is the constellation Taurus (p. 123). Inanna's threat: to scream until sky meets earth.

**Short quotation:** "it grazes where the sun rises" (A ii 19´)

**Gaps:** a loss after the first words of A ii 7´; "An" in A ii 8´ is uncertain and "spoke to her" conjectural. In the indented lines the subjects of the scream lines, "heaven and earth," "blanket," and the ends of the two wailing questions are restored. ⚠️ George says Inanna's reason, that she cannot give herself to Gilgamesh, is only what she "apparently" says (p. 123). ⚠️ The A count between A ii 20´ and A ii 24´ does not fit the printed lines, because the scream passage is drawn from several manuscripts.

**Repetitions:** her wish to kill the lord is said three times (A ii 15´-16´). The scream formulas repeat Ninsun's from S3.7.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels Ishtar's appeal to Anu (VI 80-114, pp. 49-50).
- **Who is present:** in the standard version Ishtar weeps before her father Anu and her mother Antu (VI 82-83). Antu is not here.
- **Her reason:** in the standard version she complains that Gilgamesh has insulted and slandered her (VI 84-86; Thompson 1928 has her say Gilgamesh "doth ... load me with insult," Tablet VI, l. 85, p. 34), and Anu answers that she brought it on herself (VI 89-91). Here she weeps because she cannot give herself to him, and An does not scold her.
- **Her threat:** in the standard version she threatens to break open the Netherworld and bring up the dead to eat the living (VI 96-100). Here she threatens to scream, and does. (Wikipedia's synopsis differs on the standard version's threat; see Open questions.)
- **An's objection:** both are about food. Here the Bull would have nothing to eat on earth because it grazes at the horizon. In the standard version Anu first makes Ishtar provide for Uruk, seven years of chaff from the widows and seven years of hay from the farmers (VI 103-105), which she says she has done (VI 110-111).
- **How the Bull is handed over:** in the standard version Anu puts the Bull's nose-rope in her hands (VI 114). Here An gives her the Bull and she takes hold of its tether (S3.9).
- **Names:** An, not Anu; Inanna, not Ishtar.

### S3.9. The Bull in Uruk (A iii 26´ to just before A iii 35´, pp. 127-128)
**Where and when:** from the sky down to Uruk and Kullab. Told in the present tense.
**Who:** Inanna; the Bull of Heaven. No speeches.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Maiden Inanna grips the tether "in manly fashion" and leads the Bull of Heaven down from the sky (A iii 26´-27´).
2. In Uruk the Bull eats the grass and drinks from the Engilua canal; the canal is a full league long, yet the Bull's thirst is not satisfied (A iii 28´-30´).
3. It eats the grass down to bare earth and bends the date-palms of Uruk to its mouth, snapping them (after A iii 30´).
4. Standing there, the Bull fills the whole of Uruk, and by itself the whole of Kullab (before A iii 35´).

**Specifics:** the tether (A iii 26´). The **Engilua canal**, a league long (A iii 29´-30´); the Glossary: also called Idengilua, a waterway of Uruk, perhaps the same as the Idurungal, the main eastern branch of the Euphrates (p. 222). Grass; bare ground; **date-palms**. The Bull's size: it fills Uruk and Kullab.

**Short quotation:** "In manly fashion" (A iii 26´)

**Gaps:** none in these lines. "(All of)" is George's supplied word. ⚠️ The A count does not fit between A iii 30´ and A iii 35´.

**Repetitions:** the minstrel repeats the six lines of damage (S3.10).

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the Bull's arrival and rampage (VI 115-124, pp. 50-51).
- **Events:** in the standard version Ishtar brings the Bull down and it dries up the woods, reed-beds and marshes and lowers the river by seven cubits (VI 117-118). Each snort opens a pit: first a hundred men of Uruk fall in, then two hundred, then Enkidu up to his waist (VI 119-124). Thompson 1928 reads this differently: a hundred, then two hundred, then three hundred men attack the Bull and are destroyed by its breath (Tablet VI, editorial summary before l. 130, p. 35). Here there are no pits and no one falls; the Bull eats grass and palms and drinks a canal.
- **Numbers:** a league-long canal here; seven cubits, one hundred and two hundred men in George's standard version.
- **Who is present:** in the standard version Enkidu is already at the Bull when it snorts (VI 123-125). Here neither hero is there; Gilgamesh is drinking (S3.10).

### S3.10. The minstrel's news; Gilgamesh goes on drinking (two lines before A iii 35´, to Ma 90, p. 128)
**Where and when:** the house of Gilgamesh's god, where he is drinking beer, at the same time as the Bull's rampage.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; his minstrel Lugalgabangal. Speakers: Lugalgabangal, Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Meanwhile the lord Gilgamesh is drinking beer in the house of his god (said twice; the two lines before A iii 35´).
2. His minstrel Lugalgabangal, who has stepped outside to vomit bile, looks up and sees the Bull of Heaven; he ducks down low and goes back in (A iii 35´-37´).
3. Lugalgabangal → Gilgamesh: he asks his lord how long he means to keep drinking, and tells him holy Inanna has brought the Bull of Heaven down from the sky (A iii 38´-40´). He then repeats the six lines on the damage: the grass, the league-long Engilua canal, the bare ground, the date-palms, the Bull filling Uruk and Kullab (after A iii 40´, through Nj iii 5´ to Nj iii 10´).
4. Gilgamesh → Lugalgabangal: he tells the minstrel to sing and play his strings, and calls for his tankard to be refilled with beer (Ma 85-87).
5. Lugalgabangal → Gilgamesh: he wishes his lord good eating and drinking (Ma 88-89) and (indented line, Nk rev. 5´) asks whether this business is not his lord's concern.
6. (Indented line) Gilgamesh answers his minstrel: why should such a thing frighten him? (Ma 90; "frighten me" is restored).

**Specifics:** beer; the house of his god (the god is not named); the minstrel vomiting bile; a stringed instrument; a tankard. Glossary: Lugalgabangal, "Chest-endowed Lord," Gilgamesh's minstrel (p. 223).

**Short quotation:** "how long will you drink?" (A iii 38´)

**Gaps:** in the repeated damage lines, the verbs for devouring, drinking, laying bare and bending, and the canal's unquenched thirst, are restored; so are the verb for answering, "may you," Gilgamesh's name and "frighten me." ⚠️ George says the passage is broken and is pieced together from evidently different versions of the text (p. 123); the margin switches between manuscripts A, Nj, Ma and Nk.

**Repetitions:** item 3 repeats S3.9 word for word, as reported speech; the minstrel's question is asked twice.

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** It has no minstrel, and Gilgamesh feasts only after the fight, celebrating in his palace (VI 179, p. 53).

### S3.11. Gilgamesh arms; his mother and sister sent to sacrifice (Ma 91-107, with indented lines Nn 7´ and after, pp. 128-129)
**Where and when:** after the meal (George: he gets ready for battle once he has eaten, p. 123).
**Who:** Gilgamesh; his mother Ninsun; his sister Peshtur. Speakers: Gilgamesh, Ninsun.

**Play-by-play:**
1. To strike the Bull, Gilgamesh takes up his weapons (Ma 91-92; said twice, the verb restored).
2. He ties on a belt (uncertain) weighing fifty pounds, hangs a dagger of seven and a half talents at his side, and takes his bronze expedition axe in hand (Ma 93-95; the verbs and some nouns restored).
3. Four broken lines mention his mother, his sister, his mother Ninsun and his sister Peshtur (Ma 96-99).
4. Gilgamesh → mother and sister (the verb is conjectural): his mother should go into Enki's temple, and his little sister Peshtur into the same temple; there oxen and sheep are to be slaughtered (Ma 100-104) and (indented line, Nn 7´) beer poured.
5. (Indented line) Ninsun, his mother, replies: only fragments survive, about the Bull of Heaven and him, the two of them, and herself (Ma 105-107).

**Specifics:** a belt (uncertain) of **fifty pounds**; a dagger of **seven and a half talents**; a bronze axe for expeditions (Ma 93-95). The **temple of Enki** (Ma 101). Oxen, sheep, beer (Ma 103-104, Nn 7´). Peshtur, his little sister (Ma 102).

**Short quotation:** "the temple of Enki" (Ma 101)

**Gaps:** the verbs for taking up, girding, hanging and holding, and the words for bronze and expeditions, are restored, as are Ninsun's name, "his" and "slaughter"; the verbs "spoke to them" and "go into" are conjectural; "belt" is uncertain. Ma 96-99 and Ninsun's reply (Ma 105-107) are mostly lost.

**Repetitions:** the line about striking the Bull is doubled (Ma 91-92); mother and sister are each named twice (Ma 96-99).

**Compared with the standard version:** **no counterpart in the standard version.** Tablet VI has no arming scene, no sacrifice before the fight and no sister; the heroes' weapons were made for the Cedar Forest (Y 162-171, pp. 18-19: axes of three talents, dagger blades of two). After the kill in Tablet VI they offer the Bull's heart to Shamash (VI 147-150, p. 52).

### S3.12. Gilgamesh's vow over the Bull (indented line Nn 11´, Ma 108-113, and another version to A iv 10, p. 129)
**Where and when:** right after Ninsun's reply.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Ninsun (listening); in the other version, the Bull of Heaven (addressed). Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. (Indented line, Nn 11´) Gilgamesh answers Ninsun (her name restored).
2. Gilgamesh → Ninsun (**vow**): he will strike the Bull down at once (uncertain rendering); its body is to be thrown into the narrow streets and its guts into the broad streets; the city's orphans are to get its meat by the basketful; its hide is to go to the tanner; and Inanna in Eanna is to pour sweet oil from flasks made of its two horns (Ma 108-113).
3. George notes that another version of the text has Gilgamesh say almost the same thing to the Bull itself: he will throw its body and guts into the streets, give its hide to the tanner and its meat to the orphans, and present its horns to Inanna in Eanna as oil flasks (the five lines to A iv 10).

**Specifics:** narrow streets; broad streets; orphans; meat by the basket; the tanner; **the two horns** as flasks for sweet oil, for Inanna in Eanna.

**Short quotation:** "by the basket-load" (Ma 111)

**Gaps:** most verbs of the vow, the Bull's name, and the words for the orphans' share and the pouring of oil are restored; "I shall smite" is uncertain. George's footnote 3 (Ma 111): an emendation; the tablet has "your" where George prints "its" meat. In the other version "hand over" is restored.

**Repetitions:** the vow comes in two versions, and S3.15 repeats it as fulfilled.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels what happens to the horns in Tablet VI (VI 160-166, pp. 52-53).
- **Events:** the standard version has no vow beforehand; after the kill Gilgamesh summons the craftsmen, who admire the size of the horns.
- **Numbers and materials:** in George's standard version each horn is a solid block of lapis lazuli weighing thirty pounds, with two-pound rims, and together they hold sixty bushels of oil (VI 162-164). Thompson 1928 reads thirty minas of azure each and "six measures of oil" (Tablet VI, ll. 172-175, p. 36). This poem gives no measures.
- **Outcome:** in the standard version Gilgamesh dedicates the horns to his god Lugalbanda as holders for anointing oil and hangs them in his room (VI 165-166). Here they go to **Inanna** in Eanna. The orphans' meat, the tanner and the streets have no counterpart; in the standard version the heart goes to Shamash (VI 148).

### S3.13. The fight with the Bull (from after A iv 10 to Ma 129, with A iv 20 and the indented line Nn 31´, pp. 129-130)
**Where and when:** in Uruk, below the city wall; Inanna watches from the rampart.
**Who:** Inanna (watching); the Bull of Heaven; Gilgamesh; Enkidu; the men of his city. Speaker: Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Inanna watches from the rampart while the Bull bellows in the dust (the two lines before Ma 115).
2. Gilgamesh takes his place (uncertain) at the Bull's head; Enkidu climbs (uncertain) onto some part of it whose name is lost (Ma 115 and the next line).
3. The Bull smothers the men of his city in dust, the way an unbroken calf does (the two lines after).
4. Enkidu gets behind the Bull, grabs its tail and calls out to his master Gilgamesh (the two lines before Ma 120).
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the "Ho, hurrah!" praise (tall young tree, noble favorite of the gods, angry ox ready for battle), naming him great lord Gilgamesh, honored in Uruk, with the lines on his mother and his nurse (Ma 120 to before Nn 31´; A iv 20 is the angry-ox line). (Indented line, Nn 31´:) he hails him as noble lord, and the line breaks off. He tells him not to be afraid, though he is a warrior without enough strength on his own (uncertain); four more lines, about a sure path, his hand and "the people," are mostly lost (to Ma 125).
6. When Enkidu has finished, Gilgamesh (name restored) strikes the top of the Bull's head with his seven-talent axe (Ma 126-127).
7. The Bull rears its head and crashes down from its height, a formless heap like a lump of clay or a pile of harvested grain (Ma 128-129).

**Specifics:** the rampart; dust; an untrained calf; the tail; the **axe of seven talents**; the **crown** of the Bull's head (Ma 127); a lump of clay; a harvested crop. Enkidu calls Gilgamesh his master (before Ma 120).

**Short quotation:** "smote its crown" (Ma 127)

**Gaps:** "stood at its," "went up on its" and "on his own" are uncertain; "(enough)" is George's supplied word; Gilgamesh's name (Ma 127) is restored; the last four lines of Enkidu's speech are mostly lost. ⚠️ The Ma count does not fit the printed lines between Ma 113 and Ma 125 (the speech combines Ma, A and Nn), so lines there are cited by the nearest marker. George's synopsis says Enkidu finds the Bull's weak spot before Gilgamesh kills it (p. 123); no weak spot is named in the text.

**Repetitions:** Enkidu's praise repeats the formula of "Gilgamesh and Huwawa" (Version A 130-134, 164-169; Version B 1-4), with "great lord" in place of "young lord."

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the killing of the Bull (VI 125-153, pp. 51-52).
- **How it is done:** in the standard version Enkidu grabs the Bull by the horns, it slobbers in his face and flicks dung at him (VI 125-127), and he explains his plan: he will go behind it, hold its tail and plant his foot on the back of its leg, and Gilgamesh, like a skilled butcher, will drive his knife in between the horns and the killing spot (VI 132-146). Here Enkidu also holds the tail, but his speech is praise and encouragement, and Gilgamesh kills the Bull with **an axe** blow to the top of its head, not a knife.
- **Who speaks:** in both, Enkidu speaks to Gilgamesh before the kill.
- **Where Inanna is:** here she watches from the rampart **during** the fight. In the standard version Ishtar climbs the wall **after** the kill, jumping and stamping, and laments that Gilgamesh has killed the Bull (VI 151-153).
- **Numbers:** the seven-talent axe here; Tablet VI gives no weapon weights.
- **Missing here:** the pits and the men who fall into them (VI 119-124); the heart offered to Shamash (VI 147-150).

### S3.14. The shoulder hurled; the wall demolished; Gilgamesh's threat (Ma 130 and the lines after it, before Ma 135, pp. 130-131)
**Where and when:** at the Bull's carcass, below the rampart where Inanna stands.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, the king; Inanna. Speaker: Gilgamesh.

**Play-by-play:**
1. With no butcher at hand, the king takes a knife himself and hacks off a shoulder to throw at Inanna (Ma 130 and the next line; "to throw" is George's supplied word).
2. The shoulder sends her fluttering off like a dove and knocks down the rampart (the line after).
3. The king stands at the Bull's head and weeps bitterly (the next line).
4. Gilgamesh → (Inanna, as George supplies it): what he can do to the wall he can do to her (the line before the fulfillment formula; "the wall?" and "with you?" are George's supplied words, with question marks).

**Specifics:** a knife; no butcher; a **shoulder**; a dove; the rampart knocked down; bitter tears.

**Short quotation:** "like a dove" (after Ma 130)

**Gaps:** "(to throw)," "(the wall?)" and "(with you?)" are George's supplied words, the last two marked as doubtful. ⚠️ The Ma count between Ma 130 and Ma 135 is off by one line. ⚠️ George's synopsis speaks of a piece of the leg, a limb, knocking down the wall (p. 123); the translation says a shoulder and the rampart. Why Gilgamesh weeps is not stated.

**Repetitions:** none.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the throwing of the shoulder (VI 154-159, p. 52).
- **Who throws:** in the standard version **Enkidu** rips off the Bull's shoulder and flings it at Ishtar (VI 154-155). Here **Gilgamesh** cuts it off with a knife and throws it. Thompson 1928 reads "the member" for what Enkidu throws (Tablet VI, l. 163, p. 35).
- **What it hits:** here it drives Inanna off and knocks down the rampart; in the standard version nothing is said to be destroyed.
- **The threat:** close in sense. In the standard version Enkidu says he would have done the same to Ishtar and hung the guts on her arms (VI 156-157; Thompson 1928: "I’d ha’ served thee the same way," Tablet VI, l. 165, p. 35). Here Gilgamesh says what he can do to the wall he can do to her.
- **Events missing here:** Ishtar gathering her temple women and harlots to mourn over the shoulder (VI 158-159).
- **Events missing there:** Gilgamesh's tears.

### S3.15. The vow fulfilled; closing praise (the line before Ma 135, and Ma 135-140, p. 131)
**Where and when:** Uruk, after the kill.
**Who:** Gilgamesh; Inanna (receives the horns); the orphans; the tanner; the narrator. Speaker: the narrator (closing line).

**Play-by-play:**
1. Everything happens as he said (the line before Ma 135).
2. He throws the Bull's body into the streets and its guts into the broad streets, gives its meat by the basketful to the orphans of his city, and hands its hide to the tanner; Inanna in Eanna pours sweet oil from flasks made of its two horns (Ma 135-139).
3. Narrator: with the Bull of Heaven slain, the narrator praises holy Inanna (Ma 140).

**Specifics:** streets and broad streets; orphans; baskets of meat; the tanner; the two horns as oil flasks; sweet oil; Eanna.

**Short quotation:** "sweet is your praise" (Ma 140)

**Gaps:** none. George's footnote 4 (Ma 137): an emendation; the tablet has "your meat" and "my city."

**Repetitions:** Ma 135-139 repeat the vow (S3.12, Ma 109-113) as narrative, in the order of its first version (the other version puts the hide before the meat). The same "sweet is your praise" ending closes "Gilgamesh and Akka" (113-114), where it praises Gilgamesh.

**Compared with the standard version:** parallels the end of Tablet VI (VI 160-179, pp. 52-53) and what follows in Tablet VII.
- **The horns:** to Inanna here; to Lugalbanda in the standard version (VI 165).
- **Events missing here:** washing hands in the Euphrates, the ride through Uruk's streets, the serving girls answering that Gilgamesh is the finest of men, and the feast in the palace (VI 167-179). (Thompson 1928 reads the answer as naming Gilgamesh the most splendid of heroes and, in a restored line, Enkidu the most famous, Tablet VI, ll. 182-185, p. 36.)
- **Outcome:** this poem ends with praise of Inanna and no consequence. In the standard version Enkidu dreams that same night (VI 180-183), and the gods rule that one of the two must die for killing the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba (Tablet VII, Hittite paraphrase, p. 54).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ **The whole translation:** George says it is far from definitive; the manuscripts follow different versions, spell oddly and are often corrupt (p. 122).
- ⚠️ **What Inanna looks at** when she first sees Gilgamesh is lost (S3.4).
- ⚠️ **What Gilgamesh gives his mother** (Ma 14) is lost.
- ⚠️ **Four broken lines** in Gilgamesh's second report (S3.7).
- ⚠️ **Who "it" is** in Ninsun's predator speech (S3.7); George takes it as Inanna (p. 123).
- ⚠️ **Inanna in heaven:** George is unsure his reading of the passage is right (p. 123), and says her reason, that she cannot give herself to Gilgamesh, is only what she apparently says (p. 123).
- ⚠️ **Ninsun's reply** before the fight (Ma 105-107) is mostly lost.
- ⚠️ **The end of Enkidu's speech** (to Ma 125) is mostly lost; the weak spot in George's synopsis (p. 123) does not appear in the surviving text.
- ⚠️ **Leg or shoulder:** George's synopsis speaks of a piece of the leg (p. 123); the translation says a shoulder (S3.14).
- ⚠️ **George's supplied words with question marks:** "(the wall?)," "(with you?)" (S3.14). Why Gilgamesh weeps is not stated.
- ⚠️ **Emended text:** footnote 3 (Ma 111: the tablet has "your" for "its" meat) and footnote 4 (Ma 137: the tablet has "your meat" and "my city").
- ⚠️ **Gilgamesh's god** (S3.10) is not named; Ninsun and Peshtur are sent to Enki's temple (S3.11).
- ⚠️ **Sisters:** Inanna speaks of Gilgamesh's sisters (plural, S3.6); only Peshtur is named here.
- ⚠️ **Uncertain renderings (italics):** "Merry one" (Ma 5); "take" (S3.2); "scythe," "he learnt" (S3.3); "you shall be" (S3.4, S3.5, S3.7); "catch" (S3.5, S3.6); "An" (A ii 8´); "belt" (Ma 93); "I shall smite" (Ma 108); "stood at its," "went up on its," "on his own" (S3.13).
- **Line counts:** the margin switches between manuscripts (Ma, A, Na, Nb, Nc, Nedg, Nj, Nk, Nn, Np), and in several stretches the counts between markers do not fit (see the note at the top). The rows of asterisks and the "four broken lines" note are George's.
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) spells the minstrel "Lugalgabagal" and says he convinces Gilgamesh to face the Bull (the clause that follows, about attacking alongside Enkidu, is ambiguous); George 2020 has "Lugalgabangal," whose news Gilgamesh first brushes off; the poem does not show the minstrel persuading him, and only Gilgamesh and Enkidu fight (S3.10, S3.13).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), Tablet six, gives the Bull of Heaven a name, "Gulaana"; George 2020 calls it only the Bull of Heaven, in this poem and in Tablet VI.
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), Tablet six, says Ishtar threatens Anu both with raising the dead and with screaming loud enough for heaven and earth to hear; in George 2020 the scream belongs to the Sumerian poem (S3.8), and Tablet VI has only the threat to bring up the dead (VI 96-100).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), Tablet six, says Enkidu throws one of the Bull's hindquarters at Ishtar; George 2020's Tablet VI has the shoulder (VI 155), Thompson 1928 "the member" (Tablet VI, l. 163, p. 35), and the Sumerian poem a shoulder thrown by Gilgamesh (S3.14).
- **Wikipedia cross-check:** Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124), Tablet six, says the pits swallow 300 men; George 2020 has one hundred and then two hundred, with Enkidu falling into the third pit (VI 119-124). Both readings are noted in S3.9's comparison.

## Leads (observations on the text's structure only)
- **Lead:** the poem pairs an instruction or announcement with its exact repetition: Ninsun's instructions and Gilgamesh carrying them out (S3.2, S3.3); Ninsun's model speech and Gilgamesh's speech to Inanna (S3.5, S3.6); the Bull's rampage told and then reported by the minstrel (S3.9, S3.10); the vow and its fulfillment (S3.12, S3.15).
- **Lead:** Inanna's proposal is spoken three times: by her (S3.4), by Gilgamesh to Ninsun (S3.5), and in part once more (S3.7).
- **Lead:** two screams share the same formulas, Ninsun's and then Inanna's (S3.7, S3.8).
- **Lead:** Inanna calls Gilgamesh a wild bull and a great bull (S3.4, S3.8) before the Bull of Heaven appears.
- **Lead:** Enkidu's "Ho, hurrah!" praise is the same formula used in "Gilgamesh and Huwawa."
