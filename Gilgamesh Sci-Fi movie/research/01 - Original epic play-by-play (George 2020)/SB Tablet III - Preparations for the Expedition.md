# Tablet III. Preparations for the Expedition to the Forest of Cedar: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1, pp. 22-28. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How to read these notes**
- Line numbers are George's margin numbers, printed every fifth line (shown as `{l.…}` in the extracted text). Any line number that does not fall on a printed marker is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about."
- In the play-by-play, [square brackets] mark words George restores where the tablet is broken, "(uncertain)" marks his italics (insecure reading or rendering, Note on the Translation, p. lvi), and "…" marks text that is lost.
- Speech is written as speaker → listener: the gist.

## George's synopsis (p. 22, paraphrased)
First the elders of Uruk counsel the two friends about the road ahead. Next the pair call on Gilgamesh's mother, the goddess Ninsun. She appeals to the Sun God, Shamash, and also seeks help from his consort Aya, the dawn goddess, and she takes the orphaned Enkidu as her adopted son. Gilgamesh then sets out how the city is to be run while he is gone, and the two leave.

## Scenes

### III.1 The elders advise Gilgamesh and put him in Enkidu's care
**Lines and pages:** "II end" (George's number for the restored closing couplet of Tablet II), then III 1-about 12; p. 22.
**Where and when:** Uruk, at a meeting of the elders (they speak of "our assembly," III about 11). This follows directly from Tablet II, where the elders warned Gilgamesh about Humbaba; the last part of Tablet II is lost. No time of day is given.
**Who:** the elders of Uruk (speakers; the words naming them are restored), Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Narration: the elders address Gilgamesh. This introductory couplet is wholly restored, and George numbers it as the last line of Tablet II.
2. Elders → Gilgamesh: they wish him a safe return to the city's quay, warn him not to trust only in his own strength, and tell him to watch carefully and make every blow count.
3. Elders → Gilgamesh: they cite a proverb: the man who walks ahead protects his comrade, and the man who knows the road keeps his friend safe.
4. Elders → Gilgamesh: so Enkidu should lead. He knows the route to the Forest of Cedar, he is battle-hardened, he will look after his friend, and he will bring Gilgamesh home safely to "the wives" (George marks this rendering as uncertain).
5. Elders → Enkidu (a stage direction added by George marks the change of addressee): the assembly is entrusting the king to him, and he must bring the king back and hand him over to them again.

**Specifics:** Uruk's epithet "the Sheepfold"; the city's quay; the Forest of Cedar; Gilgamesh called "the King"; the assembly; "the wives" (an uncertain rendering).
**Short quotation:**
- "(Sooth), he who goeth as vaward is able to safeguard a comrade," (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 24). Thompson 1928 reads the elders' closing words differently: they tell the king that they have looked after his welfare in their council and ask him to repay them with equal care, where George 2020 has them handing the king over to Enkidu's keeping.

**Gaps:** the whole introductory couplet (the elders' name and the speech tag) is restored. In III 1 the second half of the line, with the word "quay," is restored. "The wives" (III 10) is in italics, meaning George is unsure of the rendering. Everything else in III 1-about 12 is plain text. The stage direction is George's addition, like all small-type material (Note on the Translation, p. lvi).
**Repetitions:** the regents give this same speech again, with small changes, at the send-off (III 215-about 227; scene III.10).

### III.2 Gilgamesh proposes a visit to Ninsun
**Lines and pages:** III about 13-18; p. 22.
**Where and when:** Uruk, right after the elders finish. No time given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (speaker), Enkidu (listener).

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he suggests they go together to the Palace Sublime and appear before his mother, the great queen Ninsun.
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he explains why: Ninsun is clever, wise and knowledgeable about everything, and she will guide their steps with good advice.

**Specifics:** the Palace Sublime (Ninsun's residence); Ninsun titled "great Queen"; her epithet praises her as "clever and wise" and as knowing everything (III about 17).
**Short quotation:**
- "Wisest of (all) clever women, all-knowing;" (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 24)

**Gaps:** none; all of this passage is plain text.
**Repetitions:** the same praise of Ninsun returns, mostly restored, at III about 117 (scene III.7).

### III.3 At the Palace Sublime: Gilgamesh asks his mother's blessing
**Lines and pages:** III about 19-34; pp. 22-23 (p. 23 begins at III about 19).
**Where and when:** the Palace Sublime, before Ninsun. No time given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (speaker), Enkidu (present), Ninsun (listener). III about 36 says Ninsun heard what both Gilgamesh and Enkidu said, but only Gilgamesh's speech is given.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The two friends walk hand in hand to the Palace Sublime.
2. Gilgamesh stands up and goes in before Ninsun, the goddess who is his mother (the words naming her are restored).
3. Gilgamesh → Ninsun: he announces that, bold as he is, he will travel the long road to Humbaba's home, into a fight and along a route that are both unknown to him.
4. Gilgamesh → Ninsun: he asks for her blessing on the journey, so that he may see her again unharmed and come back happy through the city gate.
5. Gilgamesh → Ninsun (a vow): if he returns, he will hold the New Year festival two times in one year, with celebrations and drumming in her presence.

**Specifics:** walking hand in hand; Uruk's gate; a doubled New Year festival (twice in the year); drums; the long road to Humbaba's dwelling.
**Short quotation:**
- "warfare I know not, / [Follow a road] which I [know] not," (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 25)

**Gaps:** restored: the words calling Ninsun "the goddess, his mother" (III about 22), her name in the speech tag (III about 23), and Gilgamesh's opening verb "I shall tread" (III about 24). The rest is plain text.
**Repetitions:** Ninsun echoes Gilgamesh's lines about the long road, the unknown fight and the unknown route, recast in the third person, in her prayer (III about 47-50; scene III.5).

### III.4 Ninsun bathes, dresses and goes up to the roof
**Lines and pages:** III 35-45; p. 23.
**Where and when:** inside the Palace Sublime: first a bath-house, then a stairway to the roof. It follows Gilgamesh's request at once. No time given.
**Who:** Ninsun (acts; she does not speak). Gilgamesh and Enkidu are presumably still there, but the text does not say so.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Ninsun, called Wild-Cow (the epithet is restored here), listens to the two at length and with sorrow.
2. She enters the bath-house seven times and washes in water infused with tamarisk and soapwort.
3. She puts on a fine garment and chooses a jewel for her breast (both verbs are uncertain restorations).
4. She puts on a cap (uncertain restoration) and then her tiara.
5. A damaged line mentions harlots and the ground; its sense cannot be recovered.
6. She climbs the stairs to the roof and sets up a censer there for Shamash.
7. She scatters incense and raises her arms toward the Sun God in appeal (her prayer follows in III.5).

**Specifics:** "Wild-Cow" as Ninsun's epithet (her name means "Lady Wild Cow," Glossary, p. 223); a bath-house; seven immersions; tamarisk and soapwort water; a fine dress; a jewel worn at the breast; a cap; a tiara; harlots (in a broken line); a stairway; the roof; a censer; incense; arms lifted in prayer.
**Short quotation:**
- "Climb’d [she the stairway], ascended the roof," (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 25). Thompson 1928 reads the preparation differently: Ninsun enters her chamber and adorns herself with the flowers of a plant he reads, with a query, as "Tulal," and puts on festal garments and a circlet. George 2020 has the sevenfold bath in tamarisk and soapwort water.

**Gaps:** restored: the epithet "Wild-Cow" (III 35) and the verb "bathed" (III about 38). Conjectural or uncertain restorations (italics inside brackets): the verbs for putting on the dress (III about 39) and choosing the jewel (III 40), and the word "cap" (III about 41). III about 42 has lost its start and two further words.
**Repetitions:** none in this passage.

### III.5 Ninsun's prayer to Shamash for the journey
**Lines and pages:** III 45-about 99; pp. 23-25 (p. 24 begins at III about 51, p. 25 at III about 92).
**Where and when:** on the palace roof, beside the censer, straight after III.4. The prayer mentions days, nightfall and sunrise, but no time is given for the scene itself.
**Who:** Ninsun (speaker), praying to Shamash. She names Aya, Shamash's wife, and asks that Aya prompt him. Gilgamesh, Enkidu and Humbaba are talked about, not present in the prayer.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Ninsun → Shamash (complaint): she asks why he burdened her son with a spirit that cannot rest. Because Shamash has stirred him, Gilgamesh will now take the long road to Humbaba's home and face a fight and a route he has never known.
2. Ninsun → Shamash: she sets out the span of time she means: all the days of the journey out and back, until Gilgamesh reaches the Forest of Cedar, kills the fierce Humbaba and rids the land of the evil that Shamash hates.
3. Ninsun → Shamash: during that time, each day as Shamash makes his round of the world (an uncertain restoration), she wants Aya, Shamash's fearless bride, to remind him to place Gilgamesh in the keeping of the night watches. (A message Aya is asked to pass on.)
4. [About five lines are lost: III about 58-62.]
5. Ninsun → Shamash (praise of the sunrise): when Shamash rises he lets the herds out [through the gates] (uncertain), brings light to people, gives shape to the hills (uncertain), brightens the sky and rouses the wild animals with his red glow (uncertain).
6. [About five more lines are lost: III about 67-71.]
7. Ninsun → Shamash: the crowd assembles as his light appears, and the Anunnaki gods wait for his radiance. She repeats the request that Aya remind him to give Gilgamesh into the care of the night watches (largely restored).
8. Ninsun → Shamash (requests for the road): give Gilgamesh a safe road; touch Enkidu so that he walks in front, since he knows the way to the forest; let him open a path and make the mountains crossable (much of this is restored).
9. Ninsun → Shamash: let "him" carry a torch (a word after "torch" is lost) all the way to the forest; let the days be long and the nights short; let him travel girded and with long strides.
10. Ninsun → Shamash: let "him" set up camp at nightfall so they can lie down and rest in the evening.
11. Ninsun → Shamash: she wants Aya to remind him of one more thing: on the day the two meet Humbaba, Shamash should stir up the thirteen great winds against Humbaba (listed under Specifics), darken Humbaba's face, and so let Gilgamesh's weapons overcome him. (A request; Shamash does exactly this in Tablet V, V about 159-165.)
12. Ninsun → Shamash: once his own "fires" (uncertain) are lit, he should turn and look on the "supplicant" (uncertain).
13. Ninsun → Shamash (comforts promised to him): his swift mules will carry him on; a seat to rest on and a bed for the night will be waiting; his brother gods will bring him food he likes; and Aya will dry his face with the hem of her robe (most of the verbs here are uncertain restorations).

**Specifics:**
- Epithets: Shamash as the Sun God; Aya as "the Bride," described as unafraid; Humbaba as "ferocious" and as the evil thing that Shamash loathes (III about 54).
- Objects and acts: the censer and incense; a torch; a camp pitched at nightfall; Shamash's fast mules; a seat; a bed; food; the hem of Aya's garment.
- Nature: herds; uplands; the sky; wild beasts; the sun's red glow.
- Gods: the Anunnaki (George: a name for one of the two divisions of the gods, in later periods the gods of the Netherworld, Glossary, p. 221).
- The thirteen winds, in the order the text gives them (III about 89-91): 1 South, 2 North, 3 East, 4 West, 5 Blast, 6 Counterblast, 7 Typhoon, 8 Hurricane, 9 Tempest, 10 Devil-Wind, 11 Frost-Wind, 12 Gale, 13 Tornado.
- Figure 4 (p. 25) is a drawing of the Sun God seated in his temple, from a ninth-century BC stone tablet from Sippar (List of Figures). Its caption is III 95. It is an illustration, not part of the text.

**Short quotation:**
- "Why didst thou give (this) restlessness of spirit / With which didst dower Gilgamish, [my] son?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 25). Thompson 1928 calls Aya "Aa"; his text of the prayer breaks off after the Aya request, and he summarizes the following columns as badly damaged.

**Gaps:**
- George marks two lacunas with * * * and no note: about III 58-62 and about III 67-71.
- The hymn to the sunrise and the requests for the road (III about 63-66, about 72-75 and about 76-79) are heavily restored. The words for Shamash's daily round (III 55) are uncertain restorations.
- III 80: one word after "torch" is lost.
- III about 94-95: the words rendered "fires" and "supplicant" are in italics (uncertain).
- III about 96-98: the verbs describing Shamash's comforts are uncertain restorations.
- The text does not say who "him" is when someone is to carry the torch (III 80) and pitch the camp (III about 84). Both lines come after the request that Shamash touch Enkidu so he goes ahead (III about 77).

**Repetitions:**
- The request that Aya remind Shamash comes three times: III about 56-57, III about 74-75, and III about 86, where the reminder is the request about the winds.
- III about 47-50 repeats Gilgamesh's words from III about 24-27 in the third person.
- The span-of-time passage (III about 51-54) is said again by Gilgamesh, in the first person plural, at III 202-205 (scene III.9).
- The travel blessing of III about 82-85 (long days, short nights, girded loins, long stride, a camp at nightfall) is said again to both heroes at III about 131-134 (scene III.7).
- The thirteen winds and the darkening of Humbaba's face come back, almost word for word, when Shamash acts in Tablet V (V about 159-165).

### III.6 Ninsun's second request: Gilgamesh's place among the gods
**Lines and pages:** III 100-about 106; p. 25.
**Where and when:** still on the roof at the censer, directly after the first prayer (the text says she made her request "again," III 100).
**Who:** Ninsun (speaker) → Shamash.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Ninsun puts a second request to Shamash, framed as a series of questions.
2. Ninsun → Shamash: she asks whether Gilgamesh will not be counted among the gods (the verb is uncertain), share heaven with Shamash, and hold a scepter and crown together with the Moon.
3. Ninsun → Shamash: she asks whether he will not gain wisdom alongside Ea of the Ocean Below, govern the black-headed people alongside Irnina, and live alongside Ningishzida in the Land-of-No-Return.

**Specifics:** the Moon; a scepter and a crown; Ea, god of the Ocean Below; Irnina; the "black-headed people"; Ningishzida; the Land-of-No-Return. George's glossary: Ea is the god of the freshwater Ocean Below and the wisest god (p. 222); the Ocean Below is the fresh water under the earth, Ea's realm (p. 224); Irnina is a name of Ishtar and also a Netherworld deity (p. 223); Ningishzida, "Lord of the True Tree," is the Netherworld's chamberlain (p. 223).
**Short quotation (George 2020; no public-domain wording is available, since Thompson 1928 gives only a summary of these damaged columns):**
- "Will he not dwell with Ningishzida in the Land-of-No-Return?" (III about 106)

**George's comment:** in the Introduction, George reads these lines as Ninsun's foreknowledge of the place Gilgamesh will hold after death among the gods of the Netherworld, a fate never revealed to him (pp. lii-liii, quoting III 105-about 106). He adds that Gilgamesh did grow wise with Ea, as Ninsun foretold here (p. lii).
**Gaps:** the verb rendered "rank with" (III about 101) is in italics. After III about 106 a lacuna (* * *) of about nine lines runs to III 116.
**Repetitions:** all six questions use the same "Will he not …" pattern.

### III.7 Ninsun adopts Enkidu and blesses the two travelers
**Lines and pages:** III 116-about 134; p. 26.
**Where and when:** Ninsun comes down from the roof (an uncertain restoration); presumably back in the Palace Sublime, though this is not stated. It follows the prayers directly (III 116 opens by saying she had finished her charge to Shamash).
**Who:** Ninsun (speaker), Enkidu (called to her and addressed). The blessing at III 130-about 134 is spoken to "you" traveling together, so Gilgamesh is presumably there as well.

**Play-by-play:**
1. When her charge to Shamash is done, Ninsun, praised again as clever and wise, kisses the floor (uncertain).
2. She puts out the censer and descends from the roof (uncertain).
3. She calls Enkidu to her and makes a formal declaration.
4. Ninsun → Enkidu: although she did not give birth to him, from now on his offspring are to be counted with Gilgamesh's votaries: the priestesses, the hierodules and the temple women.
5. She hangs "the symbols" around Enkidu's neck.
6. Ninsun (declaration): she recalls that priestesses once took in the foundling and that the Divine Daughters raised the fostered child. She now takes Enkidu, whom she loves (uncertain), as her son, and says Gilgamesh will treat him with favor as a brother (uncertain).
7. [About one line is lost: III about 129.]
8. The speaker after the gap is presumably Ninsun (not named) → both: as they travel together to the forest, may their days be long and their nights short, their loins girded and their stride long; they should camp when night falls; and may Shamash guard them by day (this last line is a conjectural restoration).

**Specifics:** the censer put out; "the symbols" placed on Enkidu's neck (not described); Gilgamesh's votaries; priestesses; hierodules; temple women; the Divine Daughters; a foundling; a foster child; adoption as a son; Enkidu addressed as "mighty."
**Short quotation:** none; there is no public-domain wording for this passage (Thompson 1928's columns here survive only in his summary), and George's wording is not needed.

**Gaps:**
- III about 117 is mostly restored from Ninsun's earlier epithet (III about 17). In III about 118, the words "the mother of" and "kissed the" are uncertain restorations and "floor" is italicized. In III about 119, "came" is italicized and "down from the roof" is an uncertain restoration.
- "I love" (III about 127) and "brotherhood" (III about 128) are uncertain restorations.
- A lacuna (* * *) falls between III about 128 and III 130; by the margin numbers only about one line is missing.
- III 130-about 134 is heavily restored, and III about 134 is almost entirely conjectural.
- George's note after III about 134 (p. 26) says that another lacuna follows, then a very damaged episode in which the heroes seem to carry out rituals for a safe journey, then a long lacuna (see III.8).

**Repetitions:** III about 117 repeats the praise of Ninsun from III about 17. III about 131-134 repeats the travel blessing of III about 82-85, now addressed to both heroes in the second person.

### III.8 (Lost) Rituals for a safe journey
**Lines and pages:** about III 135-201, known only from George's editorial note; p. 26.
**Where and when:** not preserved.
**Who:** Gilgamesh and Enkidu, according to George's note.

**Play-by-play:**
1. George's note (p. 26): after a gap comes an episode that seems to show the two heroes performing rites to secure a safe journey; it is too badly damaged to translate, and a long gap follows. George prints none of its text.

**Specifics:** none preserved.
**Short quotation:** none.
**Gaps:** the entire passage. Its length comes only from the margin numbers: the preceding text stops at about III 134 and the next resumes at III 202, so roughly 67 lines are missing or untranslatable. ⚠️ (George gives no line count.)
**Repetitions:** none known.

### III.9 Gilgamesh's orders for Uruk while he is away
**Lines and pages:** III 202-about 211; pp. 26-27 (p. 27 begins at III about 208).
**Where and when:** Uruk, before the departure. No time given.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (speaker). His audience is not named; the regents, who appear in III about 208 (a conjectural restoration) and in III about 212, are present at the send-off that follows.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Gilgamesh, speaking for both travelers: he defines the period he means: the whole journey out and back, until they reach the forest, kill Humbaba and rid the land of the evil Shamash hates (almost all restored from Ninsun's prayer).
2. [About two lines are lost: III about 206-207.]
3. Gilgamesh: during that time the regents (a conjectural restoration) are not to assemble the young men in the streets; they are to hear the lawsuits of the weak and bring wrongdoers (uncertain) to account,
4. … while the two of them, like infants, chase their heart's desire and set their [weapons] in Humbaba's gate.

**Specifics:** the regents; the young men; the street; lawsuits brought by the weak; wrongdoers; the simile of "babes-in-arms"; Humbaba's gate.
**Short quotation:** none; no public-domain wording exists for these lines (Thompson 1928 lacks them).

**Gaps:** III 202-205 is nearly all restored, from Ninsun's III about 51-54. A lacuna (* * *) of about two lines follows (III about 206-207). The word "regents" in III about 208 is conjectural; "those who do wrong" (III about 209) is italicized; "weapons" (III about 211) is restored.
**Repetitions:** III 202-205 repeats Ninsun's III about 51-54, changed to "we" and to the evil that "Shamash" hates.

### III.10 The send-off: the regents repeat the elders' advice
**Lines and pages:** III about 212-227; p. 27.
**Where and when:** Uruk, as the heroes set out (the young men run along "behind" them). No time given.
**Who:** the regents (speakers), the young men of Uruk (a crowd), Gilgamesh, Enkidu.

**Play-by-play:**
1. The regents stand by with good wishes for Gilgamesh, the young men of Uruk crowd along behind, and the regents kiss his feet.
2. Regents → Gilgamesh: they wish him a safe return to the city's quay and repeat the warning not to depend on strength alone, but to keep watch and strike with care.
3. Regents → Gilgamesh: they cite the proverb again, in slightly different words: the one who walks ahead rescues his comrade, and the one who knows the road guards his friend.
4. Regents → Gilgamesh: Enkidu should lead, since he knows the route to the forest, has proved himself in battle and has often been through the mountain passes (uncertain). He will protect his friend and bring him home to "the wives" (uncertain).
5. Regents → Enkidu (again marked by George's stage direction): the assembly entrusts the king to him, and he must bring the king back to them.

**Specifics:** feet kissed; a mob of young men; the city's quay; the mountain passes; Gilgamesh as "the King"; the assembly.
**Short quotation:**
- "[We, O King], in our conclave [have paid deep heed to thy welfare]," (Thompson 1928, Tablet III, p. 26). This is Thompson's version of the repeated closing charge; on his different reading see III.1.

**Gaps:** restored: the verb "guard" in the proverb (III about 219) and the word "tried" (III about 222); the line about the mountain passes (III about 223) ends in a conjectural restoration; much of III about 224-227 is restored.
**Repetitions:** a close repeat of the elders' speech of III 1-about 12 (scene III.1). The differences, as printed:
- here the speakers are the regents; in III.1 they are the elders (by restoration);
- the proverb's two verbs differ slightly ("will save his comrade," "shall guard his friend");
- one line is added, saying Enkidu has often traveled the mountain passes (III about 223).

### III.11 Enkidu's reply and the departure (end of the tablet lost; supplied from the Yale tablet)
**Lines and pages:** III about 228-229, then lost; Yale tablet Y 272-about 277 (older tablet); pp. 27-28 (p. 28 begins at Y 275).
**Where and when:** Uruk, at the send-off. No time given.
**Who:** Enkidu (speaker) → Gilgamesh. The crowd is mentioned.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Enkidu begins to answer Gilgamesh (the verb and the addressee are restored). The standard version stops here.
2. George's note (p. 27): the rest of Tablet III, probably over ten lines, is missing; the Old Babylonian Yale tablet describes the departure, though its text becomes increasingly broken. George gives only its opening lines:
3. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he tells Gilgamesh to start the journey he has resolved on (uncertain), not to be afraid, and to keep his eyes on Enkidu.
4. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: he says he knew Humbaba's lair in the forest and the paths Humbaba roams, and tells Gilgamesh to speak to the crowd (uncertain restoration) and send them home.

**Specifics:** Humbaba's lair; the routes he roams in the forest; the crowd to be dismissed. The asterisk George prints before Humbaba's name here means the old form, Huwawa, has been changed to the later one for consistency (Note on the Translation, p. lvii).
**Short quotation:**
- "Thy heart [be not afraid,] trust to me!" (Jastrow and Clay 1920, Yale tablet, l. 274). Jastrow and Clay 1920 read the next line as a request that Gilgamesh entrust Huwawa's "dwelling(?)" to Enkidu's hand (l. 275), where George 2020 has Enkidu saying he knew the lair in the forest.

**Gaps:** the standard version is lost after III about 229. In the Yale lines, "set your mind" is italicized; the words at the start of Y 275 and the phrase about the ways Humbaba wanders are restored, and "to the crowd" is conjectural. The departure itself, which the synopsis reports, is not in the text George prints here.
**George's note on the source:** the Yale tablet (known as Gilgamesh Y) is Tablet III of the same Old Babylonian edition as the Pennsylvania tablet. It runs parallel to Tablets II-III of the standard version but differs in many places, and parts of it are used to restore chapter 1 (p. 172). Its full text is in chapter 3 (see the Old Babylonian file).
**Repetitions:** none in these lines.

## Elements list

### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Gilgamesh:** addressed by name by the elders and regents; called "the King" (III about 11, about 226); describes himself as bold (III about 24); Ninsun's son, to whom Shamash gave a restless spirit (III about 46). III.1-III.11.
- **Enkidu:** knows the route to the Forest of Cedar (III about 7, about 78, about 221); experienced in battle (III about 8, about 222); said to have often crossed the mountain passes (III about 223, conjectural); addressed as "mighty" (III about 121); once a foundling and foster child (III 125-about 126); taken by Ninsun as her son (III about 127). III.1-III.3, III.5, III.7, III.10, III.11.
- **Ninsun:** "great Queen" (III about 16, about 21); praised as clever, wise and all-knowing (III about 17, about 117); "Wild-Cow" (III 35, 100, 116, about 117); Gilgamesh's divine mother (III about 22, restored; III about 118, uncertain). III.2-III.7.
- **Shamash:** the Sun God (III 45). Addressed in III.4-III.6; named in III.7 and III.9.
- **Aya:** "the Bride," unafraid; Shamash's wife and goddess of dawn (synopsis; Glossary, p. 221). III.5.
- **Humbaba:** "ferocious" (III about 53); the evil thing that Shamash loathes (III about 54; again, restored, at III 205). Only spoken about: III.3, III.5, III.9, III.11.
- **The elders of Uruk** ("the Sheepfold"; their naming is restored; speakers): III.1.
- **The regents:** III.9 (conjectural restoration), III.10.
- **The young men of Uruk:** not to be assembled in the street (III.9); crowd behind the departing heroes (III.10).
- **The crowd:** to be sent home (III.11, older tablet).
- **Gilgamesh's votaries:** priestesses, hierodules and temple women; also the Divine Daughters. III.7.
- **Harlots:** named in a broken line (III about 42). III.4.
- **The black-headed people:** the people Gilgamesh might rule alongside Irnina (III 105). III.6.

### Places
- Uruk, "the Sheepfold" (III.1); its quay (III.1, III.10); its gate (III.3); the assembly (III.1, III.10); the street (III.9).
- The Palace Sublime (III.2, III.3), with its bath-house (III.4) and its stairway and roof (III.4-III.7).
- The Forest of Cedar (III.1, III.5, III.7, III.9, III.10); the long road to Humbaba's home (III.3, III.5); Humbaba's gate (III.9); his lair (III.11).
- Mountains to be made passable (III.5); mountain passes (III.10).
- Cosmic places: Shamash's daily round of the earth (III 55, uncertain); the uplands and the sky (III.5); the Ocean Below; the Land-of-No-Return (III.6).

### Objects and materials
- Tamarisk and soapwort bath water; a fine dress; a breast jewel; a cap; a tiara (III.4).
- Censer and incense (III.4, III.5); the censer put out (III.7).
- A torch (III.5); a nightfall camp (III.5, III.7).
- Comforts for Shamash: fast mules, a seat, a bed, food, the hem of Aya's robe (III.5).
- A scepter and crown, shared with the Moon (III.6).
- "The symbols," hung on Enkidu's neck (III.7).
- Festival drums (III.3); weapons, to be set in Humbaba's gate (III.9).

### Creatures
- Herds let out at sunrise; wild animals roused by the sun's glow (III.5).
- Shamash's mules (III.5).
- Humbaba, called an evil thing (III.5, III.9).

### Gods and what they do
- **Shamash:** gave Gilgamesh his restless spirit and stirred him to go (III about 46-47). Asked to protect him on the road, to touch Enkidu so he leads, to lengthen the days and shorten the nights, and to raise thirteen winds against Humbaba (III.5).
- **Aya:** asked to remind Shamash each day to put Gilgamesh in the care of the night watches, and to pass on the request about the winds; she will dry Shamash's face (III.5).
- **The Anunnaki:** await the sunrise (III.5).
- **Shamash's brother gods:** will bring him food (III.5).
- **The Moon, Ea, Irnina, Ningishzida:** named in Ninsun's questions about Gilgamesh's future: sharing a scepter and crown with the Moon, wisdom with Ea, rule with Irnina, a home with Ningishzida in the Land-of-No-Return (III.6).
- **Ninsun** (herself a goddess): prays, then adopts Enkidu (III.4-III.7).
- **The Divine Daughters:** raised the foster child (III.7).

### Numbers and time
- Seven trips into the bath-house (III about 37).
- New Year held twice; the festival twice in one year (III about 31-32).
- Thirteen winds, each named (III about 89-92).
- Time words: the journey out and back; each day; long days and short nights; camp at nightfall and rest in the evening; the night watches (III.5, III.7, III.9).
- Lost text: about 5 lines twice (III about 58-62, about 67-71); about 9 lines (III about 107-115); about 67 lines (III about 135-201, from the margin numbers); "perhaps more than ten" lines at the end (George, p. 27).

### Dreams and omens
- No dreams in this tablet.
- Ninsun's questions about Gilgamesh's future (III.6) are taken by George as foreknowledge of his place among the Netherworld gods after death (Introduction, pp. lii-liii).

### Customs and rituals
- The elders bless and instruct the king in the assembly and place him in Enkidu's care (III.1, III.10).
- A vow to hold a double New Year festival, with drums, after a safe return (III.3).
- Ninsun's preparation: seven baths in tamarisk and soapwort water, then fine clothes, jewel, cap and tiara (III.4).
- A rooftop offering to the Sun God: censer, scattered incense, raised arms; afterward kissing the floor and putting out the censer (III.4-III.7).
- Adoption: a spoken declaration plus "the symbols" on Enkidu's neck; his offspring assigned to Gilgamesh's votaries (III.7).
- Rites for a safe journey, lost (III.8).
- Rule in the king's absence: the regents hear lawsuits and must not assemble the young men in the street (III.9).
- The regents kiss Gilgamesh's feet as he leaves (III.10).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ "The wives" (III 10, 225) is an uncertain rendering; George does not explain it.
- ⚠️ The speakers of III 1-about 12 are the elders only by restoration. At III about 212-227 the same speech is given by the regents. The text does not say whether the two groups are the same.
- ⚠️ III about 42 (harlots … the ground) is too broken to make sense of.
- ⚠️ It is not stated who carries the torch (III 80) or who pitches the camp (III about 84): Enkidu or Gilgamesh.
- ⚠️ The renderings "fires" and "supplicant" (III about 94-95) and "rank with" (III about 101) are uncertain.
- ⚠️ What "the symbols" on Enkidu's neck are is not stated (III about 124).
- ⚠️ The speaker of the blessing at III 130-about 134 is not named after the gap; it is presumably Ninsun.
- ⚠️ Whom Gilgamesh addresses in III 202-about 211 is not stated.
- ⚠️ George describes the ritual episode (III.8) only as seeming to show rites for a safe journey, and does not translate it.
- ⚠️ The departure comes from the older Yale tablet; the standard version's own ending (over ten lines) is lost.
- ⚠️ III about 36 says Ninsun heard what Gilgamesh "and Enkidu" said, but no words of Enkidu are given before it.
- By the margin numbers, the gap marked * * * after III about 128 is only about one line long; George notes that his line count can disagree with the printed lines (p. lv).
- Thompson 1928 reads the elders' charge as a request that the king repay their care (Tablet III, pp. 24, 26); George 2020 has them entrusting the king to Enkidu. Thompson 1928 also has Ninsun adorn herself with flowers in her chamber (p. 25), where George 2020 has the sevenfold bath.
- Wikipedia cross-check (synopsis, revision 1377304124, "Tablet three"): it agrees with George 2020 on every point it covers and adds nothing missing here.

## Leads (structural observations only)
- One speech frames the tablet: the elders' advice opens it (III 1-about 12) and the regents repeat it at the send-off (III 215-about 227).
- Several passages are spoken twice by different speakers: the unknown fight and unknown road (Gilgamesh, then Ninsun); the span of the journey out and back (Ninsun, then Gilgamesh); the travel blessing (to Shamash, then to the two heroes).
- Ninsun's prayer moves through complaint, praise of the sunrise, requests for the road, requests for the fight, and promised comforts for Shamash, with the Aya reminder recurring three times.
- The request for the thirteen winds (III about 87-93) is fulfilled almost word for word in Tablet V (V about 159-165).
