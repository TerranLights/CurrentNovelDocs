# Tablet IV. The Journey to the Forest of Cedar: play-by-play
Research notes, 2026-10-25. Source: George 2020, chapter 1, pp. 28-35. A record of the original only; nothing here is adaptation or decision.

**How to read these notes**
- Line numbers are George's margin numbers, printed every fifth line (shown as `{l.…}` in the extracted text). Any line number that does not fall on a printed marker is counted from the nearest marker and marked "about."
- Where Tablet IV is broken, George fills the gap with passages from older tablets and numbers them by their own tablets: "Schøyen2" (an Old Babylonian tablet in the Schøyen Collection), "OB Ni" (an Old Babylonian tablet from Nippur) and "Ha1" (an Old Babylonian tablet from Tell Harmal). These are marked "(older tablet)" below.
- In the play-by-play, [square brackets] mark words George restores where the tablet is broken, "(uncertain)" marks his italics (insecure reading or rendering, Note on the Translation, p. lvi), and "…" marks text that is lost.
- Speech is written as speaker → listener: the gist.

## George's synopsis (p. 28, paraphrased)
On the road, the two friends stop every third day, make camp on a slope and carry out a rite meant to bring Gilgamesh a dream. Every time, Gilgamesh wakes in fright from a bad dream, and every time Enkidu reassures him that it actually means good fortune. After no fewer than five such dreams they come near the Forest of Cedar.

## Scenes

### IV.1 First stage of the march; the first dream ritual
**Lines and pages:** IV 1-about 15; p. 28.
**Where and when:** on the road toward Mount Lebanon, then on top of a mountain. The text gives a meal stop at 20 leagues, a camp at 30 leagues, and 50 leagues covered per day; by the third day they have covered a distance that would normally take a month and a half. The rite takes place at the end of the stage; the time of day is not stated. This is the first stage after leaving Uruk.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh speaks one line, addressed to the mountain.

**Play-by-play:**
1. They eat a meal after 20 leagues and make camp after 30.
2. Each day they cover 50 leagues; by the third day they have gone as far as a month-and-a-half's march, and they are getting closer to Mount Lebanon.
3. Turning toward the sun, they dig a well and fill their waterskins with fresh water (uncertain).
4. Gilgamesh goes up to the summit and pours out flour as an offering to the hill.
5. Gilgamesh → the mountain (a prayer): he asks it to send him a dream that shows a favorable sign.
6. Enkidu builds a House of the Dream God for Gilgamesh and hangs a door in its entrance to shut out the weather.
7. Enkidu has Gilgamesh lie down inside a circle he has drawn (uncertain), then lies down flat across the doorway himself (uncertain), like a net.
8. Gilgamesh sits with his chin resting on his knees, and sleep, the kind that overflows onto people, comes over him.

**Specifics:** distances: 20 leagues (meal), 30 leagues (camp), 50 leagues per day; the third day; a month and a half's march; Mount Lebanon; a well dug facing the sun; waterskins; fresh water; the mountaintop; the hill; a flour offering; the House of the Dream God; a door against the weather; a drawn circle; Enkidu lying like a net in the doorway; chin on knees.
**Short quotation:**
- "(Now) at the fortieth league did they break their fast [with a morsel], / (Now) at the sixtieth rested, and hollow’d a pit in the sunshine" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 29). Thompson 1928 reads forty and sixty leagues where George 2020 has twenty and thirty, and places this passage in his own Tablet V.

**Gaps:** in this first stage nearly everything is restored, in brackets, from the later repeats. Plain text survives only in IV 1-5: the march formula, apart from the restored words for "at twenty," "at," "fifty" and "a march" (so the numbers 20 and 50 are restored in this first stage, though they are plain text later), and the single verb "dug" in IV 5. IV about 6-15 is entirely restored. By the margin numbers, the five printed lines of the march formula occupy only four numbered lines (IV 1-about 4); George explains that a verse is sometimes printed as two lines (Note on the Translation, p. lv).
**Repetitions:** the march formula (IV 1-about 4) occurs five times: IV 1-about 4, about 34-38, about 79-83, 120-about 124 and about 163-165. The dream rite (well, flour offering, prayer to the mountain, House of the Dream God, sleep) also occurs five times: IV 5-15, about 38-48, about 84-93, 125-135 and about 166-176.

### IV.2 The first dream: the falling mountain
**Lines and pages:** IV about 16-33; pp. 28-29 (p. 29 begins at IV about 22).
**Where and when:** in the House of the Dream God on the mountain, at midnight ("in the middle of the night"). Enkidu promises a sign the following morning.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (dreamer, speaker), Enkidu (interpreter).

**Play-by-play:**
1. At midnight Gilgamesh's sleep ends; he gets up and talks to his friend.
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (the waking speech): he asks whether Enkidu called him or touched him, since he has woken with a start; whether a god went by, since his flesh has gone numb with cold; and he announces that he has had the first dream.
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (the dream, retold): it was a confused dream. The two of them stood (uncertain) on a mountainside; the mountain collapsed (between them, uncertain); but they flew over it like "flies" (an uncertain rendering).
4. Enkidu, the one raised in the wild who knew how to advise (mostly restored), explains the dream to his friend.
5. Enkidu → Gilgamesh (interpretation): the dream is lucky and valuable (partly uncertain). The mountain stands for Humbaba (an uncertain restoration). They will capture Humbaba, kill him (uncertain), and throw his body down on the battlefield; and in the morning the Sun God will give them a favorable sign (restored).

**Specifics:** midnight; "the first dream"; a mountain slope; a falling mountain; flies (an uncertain rendering); Humbaba's corpse; the battlefield; a sign from the Sun God next morning; Enkidu's epithet as the one born in the wild (IV about 26).
**Short quotation:**
- "(Surely) the mountain which thou hast beholden [must needs be Humbaba(?)]." (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 29). Thompson 1928 also identifies the mountain with Humbaba only by a queried restoration; he restores this dream as the "second" vision, with a note that his restorations are uncertain.

**Gaps:** IV about 16-21 (the waking speech) is entirely restored. Of the dream itself (IV about 22-25), only a few words are plain text: the opening "dream that I had," "a mountain slope," "fell down," and "we like flies," and "flies" is itself an uncertain rendering. Enkidu's identification of the mountain as Humbaba (IV 30) is an uncertain restoration, and so is the verb "slay" (IV about 31).
**Repetitions:** the waking speech occurs five times (IV about 16-21, about 49-54, about 94-99, about 136-141, about 177-182). The promise of a good sign from the Sun God next morning recurs at IV about 162 (scene IV.8). The epithet "born in the wild" recurs at IV about 107 (scene IV.6).

### IV.3 Second stage of the march; the second dream ritual
**Lines and pages:** IV about 34-48; pp. 29-30 (p. 30 begins at IV about 43).
**Where and when:** the next three-day stage toward Mount Lebanon, then a mountaintop. Distances and timing as in IV.1.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh speaks one line, to the mountain.

**Play-by-play:**
1. A meal at 20 leagues, camp at 30 (the words for the meal and the camp are restored).
2. Fifty leagues a day; by the third day, a month and a half's march; closer to Mount Lebanon (the last words of each line restored).
3. Facing the sun they dig a well and fill their waterskins (uncertain).
4. Gilgamesh climbs to the summit and pours flour to the hill (partly restored).
5. Gilgamesh → the mountain: he asks for a dream with a good sign (the second half is restored).
6. Enkidu builds the House of the Dream God and fits its door (mostly restored).
7. [All restored: Gilgamesh lies in the circle and Enkidu across the doorway like a net; Gilgamesh rests his chin on his knees and falls asleep.]

**Specifics:** as in IV.1. Figure 5 (p. 29) is a drawing of a fragment of a cuneiform tablet (late first millennium BC, Babylonia; British Museum ME 93052, List of Figures), captioned with the first words of the march formula. It is an illustration, not text.
**Short quotation:**
- "Mountain, a dream do thou grant" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 29)

**Gaps:** the march formula is plain text here except the last word or two of each line. In the rite, the well-digging line, Gilgamesh's climb, the pouring of flour, the start of the prayer to the mountain and the opening words about Enkidu are plain text; the rest is restored. "Water" (IV about 39) is italicized. IV about 44-48 is entirely restored. As in IV.1, the margin numbers allow one line fewer than are printed around IV about 38.
**Repetitions:** a repeat of IV 1-15 (scene IV.1), identical as restored.

### IV.4 The second dream: the mountain falls on Gilgamesh, and a man like a lion pulls him out
**Lines and pages:** IV about 49-54, then lost; the dream is supplied from the older tablet Schøyen2, about lines 3-24; pp. 30-31 (p. 31 begins at Schøyen2 about 14).
**Where and when:** the second night's camp, at midnight (restored). Schøyen2 gives no time or place.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (dreamer, speaker), Enkidu (interpreter).

**Play-by-play:**
1. [The waking speech at midnight, ending with Gilgamesh's announcement of a second dream; all restored.]
2. The standard version breaks off. George's note (p. 30): Tablet IV does not preserve how Gilgamesh told this dream. An earlier version, known from a Middle Babylonian paraphrase found at Hattusa (the Hittite capital), shows that in this dream the Sun God rescues Gilgamesh from under an avalanche. An older Old Babylonian tablet preserves the dream better, but there it is Gilgamesh's first dream. George quotes that tablet:
3. (Older tablet) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he says he has dreamed, asks why Enkidu did not wake him, and says the dream terrified him.
4. (Older tablet, the dream retold) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he was holding up a mountain on his shoulder when it fell and crushed down on him. His legs went weak and an overwhelming, frightening aura took the strength from his arms. Then a lion-like man, radiant and [handsome] beyond anyone in the land, came to him, took him by the upper arm and dragged him out from under the mountain.
5. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh (interpretation): the one they are going to fight, strange as he is, is surely the mountain; he says it twice, the second time naming Humbaba.
6. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: when Gilgamesh meets him, he will have one task only, the rite and duty of a warrior. Humbaba will turn his rage on him and fear of him will grip Gilgamesh's legs.
7. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the man in the dream was Shamash, the Sun God and king, who will take his hand when he is in danger.
8. (Older tablet) Gilgamesh is pleased that the dream was a good sign; his heart lifts and his face brightens.

**Specifics:** a mountain propped on Gilgamesh's shoulder; the mountain collapsing on him; weak legs; a terrifying aura over his arms; a lion-like man, shining and handsome (the word for handsome is restored); the upper arm; Shamash called "King"; Humbaba called "strange"; the fight described as a warrior's rite and a man's task.
**Short quotation:**
- "a man . . ., most comely of all the land was his beauty" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 30, translating the Boghazköy tablet). Thompson 1928's Boghazköy version also has the man give Gilgamesh water to drink and set his feet on the ground; George 2020's Schøyen2 lines have no water-giving (in George, water is given in the Tell Harmal dream, IV.10).

**Gaps:**
- IV about 49-54 is entirely restored. The standard text is then lost until IV about 79, about 24 lines by the margin numbers.
- George only summarizes the Hattusa paraphrase here; he does not quote it.
- In the Schøyen2 lines only the word for "comely" is restored. The asterisk before Humbaba's name marks the old form, Huwawa, changed for consistency (Note on the Translation, p. lvii).
- The dream numbering differs between versions: the dream George places second in the standard version is the first dream on Schøyen2 (George, p. 30).
**George's notes on the sources:** Schøyen2 is a complete Old Babylonian tablet in the Schøyen Collection in Norway, probably about a century later than the early eighteenth century BC. It covers the journey to the Cedar Forest, including Gilgamesh's first two dreams and Enkidu's explanations, and lines from it restore the standard version at IV 54 and IV 109 (p. 180). The Hattusa tablet Bo2 (thirteenth century BC) tells this second dream, in parallel with Schøyen2 (p. 199).
**Repetitions:** the waking speech (see IV.2). Enkidu says twice in a row that the mountain is the one they are going to fight. The promise that Shamash will take Gilgamesh's hand in danger returns in the Tell Harmal dream (IV.10).

### IV.5 Third stage of the march; the third dream ritual
**Lines and pages:** IV about 79-93; p. 31.
**Where and when:** the third three-day stage toward Mount Lebanon, then a mountaintop. Distances and timing as in IV.1.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh speaks one line, to the mountain.

**Play-by-play:**
1. [The full march formula: meal at 20 leagues, camp at 30, 50 leagues a day, a month and a half's march by the third day, closer to Mount Lebanon; all restored.]
2. [They dig a well facing the sun] and fill their [waterskins] (uncertain).
3. Gilgamesh [climbs to the] summit and pours [flour] to the hill (partly restored).
4. Gilgamesh → the mountain: he asks for a dream with a good sign (partly restored).
5. [Enkidu] builds the House of the Dream God and [fits] its door against the weather.
6. Enkidu has him lie in the drawn (uncertain) circle and lies [flat] across the doorway like a net.
7. Gilgamesh sits with his chin on his knees, and the sleep that overflows onto people comes over him.

**Specifics:** as in IV.1.
**Short quotation:**
- "Gilgamish, [squatting] bent-kneed, supported his haunches," (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 30). Thompson 1928 also has a chill gust of wind spring up and make the hero cower before he sleeps (p. 30); George 2020 has no wind in the rite.

**Gaps:** the march formula (IV about 79-83) is entirely restored. From IV 85 on, more and more of the rite is plain text, and the two lines on the chin-on-knees posture and the sleep (IV about 92-93) are wholly plain. "Their" (IV about 84) and "drawn" (IV 90) are italicized.
**Repetitions:** a repeat of IV 1-15 (scene IV.1).

### IV.6 The third dream: thunder, darkness and fire
**Lines and pages:** IV about 94-109; then the older tablet Schøyen2, about lines 50-52; p. 32 (p. 32 begins at IV about 96).
**Where and when:** the third night's camp, at midnight.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (dreamer, speaker), Enkidu (interpreter).

**Play-by-play:**
1. At midnight (the opening words restored) Gilgamesh wakes, gets up and speaks to his friend.
2. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (the waking speech, in plain text this time): did Enkidu call him or touch him, why is he startled awake, did a god pass by, why is his flesh numb with cold; he has had a third dream.
3. Gilgamesh → Enkidu (the dream, retold): it was a confused dream. The sky shouted and the earth roared. The daylight stopped and darkness came out. Lightning flashed and a fire started. Flames blazed up and death fell like rain. Then the brilliant fire faded and went out, and the flames sank down into embers.
4. Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he reminds Enkidu that he was born in the wild (restored) and asks whether they can consult together.
5. After hearing him (restored), Enkidu interprets the dream for Gilgamesh: it is a good dream and its message (uncertain restoration) is lucky.
6. The rest of the explanation is lost from Tablet IV. George fills part of the gap from the older tablet Schøyen2 (p. 32):
7. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: … Gilgamesh will reduce "his" weapons to ash; his dreams are good, a god is with him, and he will soon achieve what he intends.

**Specifics:** "a third dream"; the sky crying out; the earth rumbling; stillness; darkness; lightning; fire; flames; death raining down; embers; ashes; "his weapons" (whose is not said in these lines); a god at Gilgamesh's side.
**Short quotation:**
- "(Loud) did the firmament roar, (and) earth (with the echo) resounded," (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 30). Thompson 1928 ends Gilgamesh's speech with a proposal that they go down to the desert to take counsel together; George 2020 has a question whether they can take counsel, addressed to Enkidu as the one born in the wild.

**Gaps:** IV about 96-106 is almost entirely plain text; restored are the first word of IV about 104 ("flames"), the opening of IV about 106, the opening of IV about 107 ("you were") and the whole of IV about 108, and the word for "message" in IV 109 is an uncertain restoration. After IV 109 the standard text is lost until IV 120, about ten lines; the Schøyen2 lines fill only the end of that gap. George says lines from Schøyen2 restore the standard version "at Tablet IV 54 and 109" (p. 180).
**Repetitions:** the waking speech (see IV.2). "Born in the wild" repeats the epithet of IV about 26.

### IV.8 The fourth dream: the Lion-Bird
**Lines and pages:** IV about 136-141; then the older tablet "OB Ni" (Nippur), about lines 9-19 and 20´-26´; then IV 155-about 162; pp. 33-34 (p. 34 begins at OB Ni about 22´).
**Where and when:** the fourth night's camp, at midnight (restored). Enkidu again promises a sign the next morning.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (dreamer, speaker), Enkidu (interpreter).

**Play-by-play:**
1. [The waking speech, ending with the announcement of a fourth dream; all restored.]
2. George's note (p. 33): Tablet IV preserves this dream and its explanation poorly, but an Old Babylonian tablet from Nippur has a fuller text. He quotes it:
3. (Older tablet) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he has had a fourth dream, and it outdoes the other three.
4. (Older tablet, the dream retold) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he saw a Lion-Bird high in the sky, rising like a cloud and hovering over them. It was some kind of … (word lost), with a twisted face; fire came from its mouth and its breath was deadly. There was also a man of strange appearance who … (lost) and stood in the dream. He tied (uncertain) the bird's wings and took Gilgamesh by the arm; … he flung it down in front of him; … on top of it (the rest broken).
5. (Older tablet) After a short gap, Enkidu → Gilgamesh (interpretation): he repeats the description of the Lion-Bird; Gilgamesh will be afraid of its fearsome aura, but Enkidu will … its foot (verb lost) and help him up. The man was Shamash in his might.
6. The standard text resumes, badly broken. Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the dream is good …; … Humbaba like …; something will be kindled … against him; they will bring about his … and tie his wings; …; they will stand on top of him; and the next morning the Sun God will give them a good sign.

**Specifics:** "a fourth dream," said to surpass the other three; the Lion-Bird; a cloud; a distorted face; a fiery mouth and deadly breath; a man of strange form; bound wings; Gilgamesh's arm; the bird's foot; its terrifying aura; Shamash called mighty; binding "his" wings and standing on "him" in the standard version's explanation, where Humbaba is named (IV about 157-161). George's glossary: the Lion-Bird is the Babylonian Anzû, pictured sometimes as an eagle with a lion's head and sometimes as a winged stallion; it lived in the mountains, where Ninurta defeated it (p. 223).
**Short quotation:**
- "Didst thou not call me, O friend? (O), why am I waken’d (from slumber)?" (Thompson 1928, Tablet V, p. 30). This is Thompson's version of the waking speech, which opens every dream; for this dream the standard version's waking speech is wholly restored, and no public-domain text of the Lion-Bird dream exists.

**Gaps:**
- IV about 136-141 is entirely restored. The standard text is then lost from about IV 142 to IV about 154. George says the Nippur tablet restores the standard version "at Tablet IV 142" (pp. 183-184).
- In the Nippur lines, one word is lost in the description of the bird (both times); OB Ni about 16, about 18 and about 19 are broken; the verb "bound" is uncertain; a short lacuna separates the dream from Enkidu's explanation (the lines after it carry a prime mark, 20´ onward, in George's numbering).
- IV 155-about 161 is badly broken; most lines keep only a few words.
**George's note on the source:** an Old Babylonian school exercise from Tablet Hill at Nippur, dug up in 1951-52 in a private house that also served as a scribal school; mid-eighteenth century BC; now in the Iraq Museum, Baghdad. It holds text from the dreams on the way to the Forest of Cedar (pp. 183-184).
**Repetitions:** the waking speech (see IV.2). Enkidu repeats Gilgamesh's description of the Lion-Bird almost exactly (OB Ni about 11-14 and 20´-about 23´). The promise of a good sign next morning repeats IV about 33.

### IV.9 Fifth stage of the march; the fifth dream ritual
**Lines and pages:** IV about 163-176; pp. 34-35.
**Where and when:** the fifth stage, then a mountaintop. This time the march formula has only three parts (meal at 20 leagues, camp at 30, 50 leagues a day); the lines about the third day and the month and a half, and about nearing Mount Lebanon, are absent.
**Who:** Gilgamesh, Enkidu. Gilgamesh speaks one line, to the mountain.

**Play-by-play:**
1. Meal at 20 leagues; camp at 30; 50 leagues covered in a day (the numbers restored).
2. Facing the sun (restored) they dig a well and fill their waterskins (uncertain).
3. Gilgamesh climbs to the summit and pours flour to the hill (partly restored).
4. Gilgamesh → the mountain: he asks for a dream with a good sign (partly restored).
5. Enkidu builds the House of the Dream God and fits its door; he lays Gilgamesh in the circle and lies flat (uncertain) across the doorway like a net (mostly restored).
6. Gilgamesh rests his chin on his knees and sleep comes over him (mostly restored).

**Specifics:** as in IV.1, without the third day, the month and a half and Mount Lebanon.
**Short quotation:** none; Thompson 1928 has no text for this stage.
**Gaps:** about half of each line is restored. The shortened march formula is as printed: the line about fifty leagues ends with a full stop and the rite begins at once.
**Repetitions:** the fifth and last surviving occurrence of the march and rite formulas (see IV.1).

### IV.10 The fifth dream (announced); a wild-bull dream from Tell Harmal
**Lines and pages:** IV about 177-182, then lost; the older tablet "Ha1" (Tell Harmal), lines 3-about 17; p. 35 (p. 35 begins at IV about 179).
**Where and when:** the fifth night's camp, at midnight (restored). The Harmal lines give no time or place.
**Who:** Gilgamesh (dreamer, speaker), Enkidu (interpreter).

**Play-by-play:**
1. [The waking speech, ending with the announcement of a fifth dream; all restored.]
2. A lacuna. George's note (p. 35): a different account of one of the dreams, with Enkidu's explanation, survives on a tablet of the Old Babylonian period found at Tell Harmal, the ancient city of Shaduppûm. He quotes it:
3. (Older tablet) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he has had a dream, and he calls it ominous, bleak (both uncertain renderings) and obscure.
4. (Older tablet, the dream retold) Gilgamesh → Enkidu: he had seized a wild bull. Its bellowing split the earth and the dust it kicked up rose high into the sky, while he, facing it, leaned forward (uncertain). Then someone … held his arms; … pulled him free … by force (uncertain) …; something about his cheek …; and someone gave him water to drink from a waterskin (partly restored).
5. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh (interpretation): the god (restored) they are going to fight is not the wild bull, however strange he looks. The bull (uncertain rendering) was Shamash in his brightness, who will take their hands in danger.
6. (Older tablet) Enkidu → Gilgamesh: the one who gave him water from the skin was his own god, who honors him: Lugalbanda. If the two of them join forces, they will have a single task, a deed never before done in the land.

**Specifics:** "a fifth dream"; a wild bull; its bellowing splitting the ground; clouds of dust; a waterskin; a drink of water; Shamash called shining; Lugalbanda, Gilgamesh's personal god; a deed never done before in the land. George's glossary: Lugalbanda, "Little Lord," was a mortal king of Uruk who was later deified; one tradition makes him Gilgamesh's father, another his guardian god (p. 223).
**Short quotation (George 2020; the Tell Harmal tablet was found in 1947, so no public-domain translation exists):**
- "The _wild bull_ you saw was shining Shamash," (Ha1 about 12)

**Gaps:**
- IV about 177-182 is entirely restored. The rest of the fifth dream and its explanation are lost from Tablet IV.
- In chapter 1 George does not say that the Harmal dream is the fifth dream; he describes it only as a further version of one of the dreams. In chapter 3 he says he used it to fill the gap in the standard version at Tablet IV 183 (p. 185). ⚠️
- In the Harmal lines, about Ha1 6-9 are broken (who grips Gilgamesh and who pulls him out are lost); the word "god" (Ha1 10) is restored; the words for "ominous," "desolate," "bent myself forward," "by force" and "wild bull" (Ha1 about 12) are italicized.
**George's note on the source:** one of two Old Babylonian tablets dug up in August 1947 in a private house at Tell Harmal, ancient Shaduppûm, on the eastern edge of Baghdad; early eighteenth century BC; probably a scribal exercise; now in the Iraq Museum (p. 185).
**Repetitions:** the waking speech (see IV.2). Shamash taking the hand in danger repeats the Schøyen2 explanation in IV.4. The "single task" echoes Schøyen2's one task for Gilgamesh (IV.4).

## Elements list

### Characters (with epithets as given)
- **Gilgamesh:** the dreamer every night; wakes frightened, with his flesh numb. IV.1-IV.10.
- **Enkidu:** the one "born in the wild" who knew how to advise (IV about 26, mostly restored; again IV about 107); builds the House of the Dream God and sleeps across its doorway like a net; reads every dream as favorable. IV.1-IV.10.
- **Humbaba:** equated with the mountain of the first dream (IV 30, uncertain restoration) and of the second dream (Schøyen2, where Enkidu calls him strange and asks whether he is not the mountain); named in Enkidu's reading of the fourth dream (IV about 157); referred to as the "god" they are going against in the Harmal dream (Ha1 10, restored). IV.2, IV.4, IV.8, IV.10.
- **Shamash:** the Sun God who will send a good sign the next morning (IV about 33, about 162); called "King" (Schøyen2), mighty (OB Ni) and shining (Ha1). IV.2, IV.4, IV.8, IV.10.
- **The man in the dreams:** a lion-like man, radiant and handsome (Schøyen2), explained as Shamash; a man of strange form (OB Ni), explained as Shamash. IV.4, IV.8.
- **Lugalbanda:** Gilgamesh's own god, who honors him; called divine (Ha1 15); explained as the water-giver in the dream. IV.10.
- **"A god":** asked about in every waking speech (did a god pass by?); a god is with Gilgamesh (Schøyen2 about 51). IV.2-IV.10.

### Places
- The road toward Mount Lebanon, drawing nearer each stage (IV.1, IV.3, IV.5, IV.7).
- The camp at 30 leagues; the well dug facing the sun (every stage).
- The summit and the hill where the flour is poured (every stage).
- The House of the Dream God, with its door, doorway and drawn circle (every stage).
- Dream settings: a mountain slope (IV.2); under a fallen mountain (IV.4); the sky where the Lion-Bird flies (IV.8); ground split by the bull and a sky full of dust (IV.10).
- "The land," where the deed has never been done (IV.10).
- The Forest of Cedar, reached in the lost ending (IV.11).

### Objects and materials
- Bread eaten at 20 leagues; a well; waterskins; fresh water; a flour offering (every stage).
- The House of the Dream God, its door, the drawn circle (every stage).
- In the dreams: the mountain; fire, flames and embers (IV.6); ash and "his weapons" (IV.6, older tablet); the Lion-Bird's wings and foot (IV.8); dust clouds; a waterskin and a drink of water (IV.10).
- Humbaba's corpse thrown down on the battlefield (IV.2).

### Creatures
- Flies, in the simile of the first dream (IV.2; an uncertain rendering).
- The lion-like man (IV.4).
- The Lion-Bird (Anzû), fire-mouthed and death-breathed (IV.8).
- A wild bull (IV.10).
- Enkidu lying "like a net" in the doorway (a simile, not a creature) (IV.1).

### Gods and what they do
- **Shamash:** rescues Gilgamesh in the dreams as the lion-like man (IV.4) and as the strange man who binds the Lion-Bird (IV.8); is the wild bull (IV.10); is to send a good sign each next morning (IV.2, IV.8); will take Gilgamesh's hand, or both their hands, in danger (IV.4, IV.10).
- **Lugalbanda:** gives Gilgamesh water from his skin in the dream (IV.10).
- **The Dream God:** a house is built for him every camp (every stage); he is not named.
- **"A god":** each waking, Gilgamesh asks whether a god went by (every waking speech).
- **The mountain:** prayed to directly for a dream with a good sign (every stage).

### Numbers and time
- 20 leagues (meal), 30 leagues (camp), 50 leagues per day (every stage).
- By the third day, a month and a half's march (stages 1-4; missing from stage 5).
- Five stages and five dreams in the standard version as it survives; "at least five" (synopsis).
- Midnight (every waking); the next morning (IV about 33, about 162).
- The text numbers the dreams: first (IV about 21), second (IV 54), third (IV about 99), fourth (IV about 141; the Nippur text says it outdoes the other three, OB Ni 10), fifth (IV about 182).

### Dreams and omens
- **Dream 1** (IV.2): a mountain collapses and the two fly over it like flies. Enkidu: the mountain is Humbaba; they will capture and kill him.
- **Dream 2** (IV.4, supplied from Schøyen2, where it is the first dream): Gilgamesh holds up a mountain that falls on him; a lion-like man pulls him out. Enkidu: the mountain is Humbaba and the man is Shamash. George: in the Hattusa paraphrase, the Sun God saves Gilgamesh from under an avalanche (p. 30).
- **Dream 3** (IV.6): thunder, quaking earth, darkness, lightning, fire and raining death, then the fire dies to embers. Enkidu: good and lucky; (Schøyen2) Gilgamesh will turn "his" weapons to ash, and a god is with him.
- **Dream 4** (IV.8, supplied from Nippur): the Lion-Bird with fire for a mouth; a strange man binds its wings and throws it down. Enkidu: Gilgamesh will fear its aura; the man is Shamash; (standard version) they will bind "his" wings and stand on "him."
- **Dream 5** (IV.10): only announced; the Harmal wild-bull dream fills the gap. Enkidu: the bull is Shamash and the water-giver is Lugalbanda.
- **Omens:** each dream is requested as a good sign and each is declared good; a sign from the Sun God is promised for the next morning.
- **The waking questions:** was he called, was he touched, did a god go by (every dream).

### Customs and rituals
- The dream rite, every third day: a well dug facing the sun; water in the skins; a climb to the summit; flour poured for the hill; a prayer to the mountain for a dream; a House of the Dream God with a door against the weather; a circle drawn for the sleeper; Enkidu lying across the doorway; the sleeper with chin on knees (IV.1, IV.3, IV.5, IV.7, IV.9).
- Dream interpretation: the dreamer tells the dream to his companion in the night, and the companion explains it (every dream).
- The fight with Humbaba named as a warrior's rite and a man's task (Schøyen2, IV.4).

## Open questions and uncertainties
- ⚠️ The standard version's own text of dreams 2 and 5 is lost, and most of dreams 1 and 4 is restored or supplied; only dream 3 survives largely in plain text.
- ⚠️ George fills the gaps with three older tablets (Schøyen2, OB Nippur, Tell Harmal). Their wording is close to the standard version but not the same: for example, Schøyen2's Gilgamesh opens by asking why Enkidu did not wake him and saying how frightened he was, unlike the standard waking speech.
- ⚠️ The dreams are numbered differently in different versions: the dream George places second is the first dream on Schøyen2 (p. 30). Since Schøyen2 holds "Gilgamesh's first two dreams" (p. 180) and also restores IV 109, its lines in IV.6 would come from its own second dream; George does not say this outright.
- ⚠️ It is not stated whether the Harmal dream is the standard version's fifth dream (IV.10).
- ⚠️ The first dream's mountain is identified with Humbaba only by an uncertain restoration (IV 30); in the second dream (Schøyen2) the identification is plain text.
- ⚠️ Whose weapons Gilgamesh will turn to ash (Schøyen2 50) is not stated in the lines quoted.
- ⚠️ "Flies" (IV 25), "drawn" (IV 90), "falling flat," and the words about the waterskins and fresh water are uncertain renderings or restorations throughout.
- ⚠️ Tablet IV's ending is lost; the synopsis's "at least five" dreams leaves open whether there were more.
- The margin numbers count one line fewer than the printed lines in the march formula and in a few speech introductions; George notes that a verse is sometimes printed as two lines (p. lv).
- Thompson 1928 (Tablet V, pp. 29-30) reads the march distances as forty and sixty leagues, not twenty and thirty; restores the falling-mountain dream as the "second" vision; adds a chill gust of wind to the rite; and has the man in the Boghazköy version of the second dream give Gilgamesh water, which George 2020 has only in the Harmal dream.
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124, "Tablet four") says the heroes camp on a mountain "every few days"; George 2020 has a camp every third day.
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) says Enkidu denies that the frightening dream images stand for the forest guardian; George 2020 has Enkidu identifying Humbaba with the mountain in dreams 1 (by uncertain restoration) and 2 (Schøyen2), and saying only in the Harmal dream that the god they face is not the wild bull.
- Wikipedia's synopsis (revision 1377304124) places in Tablet IV the heroes hearing Humbaba bellow as they near the mountain and urging each other not to be afraid; George 2020 has the end of Tablet IV lost, and puts both the mutual encouragement (V.2) and Humbaba's bellowing (V.7) in Tablet V.

## Leads (structural observations only)
- The tablet is built from three blocks repeated five times in the same order: the march (meal at 20 leagues, camp at 30, 50 a day), the dream rite, and the midnight waking with its three questions, each followed by a dream and Enkidu's explanation.
- The repeats get shorter at the end: the fifth march formula lacks the third-day and Mount Lebanon lines.
- Every dream is frightening and every dream is explained as favorable; Shamash is named as the helper figure in dreams 2 and 4 and in the Harmal dream.
- The Lion-Bird's fiery mouth and deadly breath (OB Ni) match the elders' and Enkidu's warnings about Humbaba in Tablet II, where his speech is fire and his breath is death (pp. 18, 21).
