# The Convergence Route, the Central BC Struggle Zone, and Prince Rupert

Working notes, 2026-09-19. Built from real-world BC geography, highway infrastructure, and Prince Rupert's
actual fishing-industry history, then adapted for this universe's post-2083 First Interwar Period setting.
Companion file to `Vancouver - Lower Mainland Cascadia Worldbuilding.md`. Answers three things the author asked
for: a physically real route that puts the convergence "roughly the middle of BC," concrete texture for the
struggles between there and Act 3, and a grounded reason a large, salvageable vessel would actually be sitting
in Prince Rupert waiting to be found.

## The Route — Real Highways, Real Junction Point

Two real highways make the geography work almost exactly as already imagined, with no invention needed for the
route itself:

- **Lucy + Human #1, from the north:** Whitehorse → south on the Alaska Highway to Watson Lake, Yukon → onto
  the **Stewart-Cassiar Highway (BC Highway 37)**, which runs 725 km south from Watson Lake and terminates at a
  junction with Highway 16 at **Kitwanga, BC**.
- **Jennifer + Human #2, from the south:** Vancouver → north (the real-world Cariboo corridor, BC Highway 97)
  to Prince George → west on **Highway 16 (the Yellowhead Highway)**, through Vanderhoof, Burns Lake, and
  Smithers, arriving at that same **Kitwanga junction**.

**This makes Kitwanga/New Hazelton the natural convergence point** — it's genuinely the spot where a
north-originating and a south-originating party on these two real roads would physically meet, it sits close
to the literal middle of the province east-west, and it's already on the road to Prince Rupert: continuing west
on Highway 16 from that junction runs directly through Terrace and on to the coast. The whole shape the author
described — converge in the middle of BC, then continue together toward Prince Rupert for Act 3 — is already
how these two roads are laid out in reality, not something invented to fit the plot.

## The Central BC Struggle Zone (Post-Convergence, Pre–Act 3)

The stretch from the Kitwanga junction west through the Hazelton Mountains and the Bulkley/Skeena valleys
toward Terrace and Prince Rupert gives real, specific texture for "continue to run into more problems and
struggles":

- **Terrain:** a mountainous landscape broken by large plateaus, hundreds of lakes, and several of the
  province's largest rivers (the Bulkley and the Skeena) — deep fjords, dramatic canyons, and sheer mountains
  further west toward the coast.
- **Climate shift as they travel west:** the region gets progressively wetter heading toward the coast, with
  interior spruce/pine forest gradually giving way to western hemlock and red cedar rainforest — a visible,
  usable marker of progress toward the ocean as the journey continues.
- **Wildlife hazard, concretely:** healthy grizzly and black bear populations, concentrated especially near the
  salmon-bearing rivers the route follows — the Bulkley and Skeena are exactly that kind of river.
- **A real, mundane survival hazard worth using:** water from streams and rivers in this region needs to be
  boiled or treated before drinking even in the real world — a small, grounded detail (untreated water making
  someone sick, or the group having to stop and boil water) that costs nothing to invent and adds real texture.

## Prince Rupert — Why a Large, Decommissioned Boat Is Plausibly Just Sitting There

This is the strongest finding of this pass: **Prince Rupert's real history already contains almost exactly the
scenario the author wants, with minimal invention required.**

- Prince Rupert was historically nicknamed **"the Halibut Capital of the World"** — cold storage facilities
  opened in 1910, the Grand Trunk Pacific Railway reached the town in 1914, and by the 1930s the Halibut Fleet
  was landing catches in the hundreds of thousands of pounds in a single day. This establishes, with real
  historical weight, that Prince Rupert supported a genuinely large working fishing fleet — not a handful of
  small boats, a substantial industrial one.
- **The North Pacific Cannery**, one of the historic canneries at the mouth of the Skeena, stopped operating as
  a cannery and was converted into a **maintenance and reduction facility for the fishing fleet**, continuing
  in that role until it finally closed in 1981. In other words: real Prince Rupert history already includes a
  facility whose whole later purpose was **servicing and maintaining an aging fleet until it eventually shut
  down** — precisely the kind of place where a large vessel could plausibly sit half-repaired, mothballed, or
  simply never finished, long after the facility itself stopped operating.
- **Vessel size/type reference, for concreteness:** real BC halibut/seine fishing vessels of the classic era
  ran genuinely large — the *Phyllis Cormack*, a 1941-built wooden seiner, was 82 feet long, displaced 99 tons,
  and carried a crew of up to 12; smaller wooden longliners like the 46'8" *Donna Lou* were still substantial,
  multi-person vessels. Either scale comfortably fits "a rather large boat that can comfortably fit all four of
  them," and both are exactly the kind of wooden-hulled, mechanically-heavy vessel a skilled engine mechanic
  would need to bring back to working order rather than something that just starts on the first try.

## Applying This to the Post-War Setting

None of the above needs heavy adaptation — the real pattern (a boom-era industrial fishing port, a fleet
maintenance facility that eventually stops operating, aging wooden vessels left in varying states of repair)
maps directly onto a post-2083 reconstruction-era Prince Rupert without requiring new invention: **a once-large
halibut/cannery fleet, partially rebuilt after the war as part of Cascadia's maritime-trade economy (see the
companion Vancouver file), with a maintenance yard that has since scaled back or gone idle** — leaving exactly
the kind of large, mechanically-troubled, but structurally sound vessel the group could plausibly find,
claim, and repair themselves. **This is where Lucy's own established transition — railyard engines to ocean
engines — does real work**: a big, aging wooden fishing vessel with a tired but salvageable engine is precisely
the kind of machine her skill set was already built for, just relocated from rail to sea.
