# Lucy Grayson — Doorway #1, Midpoint/Mirror Moment, Doorway #2: Constraint Specifications

2026-09-21. **Not the beats themselves** — the *attributes* each beat has to have, and the ones it can't, so
that whatever event eventually fills the slot is guaranteed to fit the character and the structure.

Built against `reference/story-structure-definitions.md` (the full composite) and her Stage 1 Derived Sheet at
`../../background notes/characters/Lucy Grayson - Stage 1 Derived Sheet.md`. Everything here is **proposed** —
a constraint that turns out to be wrong for the book should be struck, not worked around.

**On the Midpoint:** it is genuinely open-ended — Snyder allows False Victory *or* False Defeat, Bell's Mirror
Moment allows the three-question form *or* four different flavors of "death," and the Campbell beats attach
wherever they fit. So that section is split in two: **constraints that hold no matter which shape you pick**,
then **a menu of candidate shapes**, each with what it would additionally require. The menu is a set of options
to choose among or combine, not a recommendation.

**Working from:** Social 5w6 (80%) + SP 3w4 undercurrent (20%). Flaw: refuses help — **doubly locked**, one
lock per profile. Goal: to be appreciated, not simply used. Established deficit: no developed skill besides
engines. Opening situation: 12–16 hour days at the Whitehorse railyard under a boss who doesn't value her.

## Doorway #1 / Crossing the First Threshold (~20%)

*Framework: the hero commits and enters the new world — literally or internally. Should read as a door closing
behind them; the old rules no longer apply.*

Plot slot: she leaves the railyard with Human #1.

### MUST be true

1. **The decision is made privately, then presented as already settled.** A Five deliberates internally and
   announces conclusions. Her Debate beat can be visible to the reader; it cannot be visible to another
   character.
2. **The offer must be structured so that accepting it does not feel like accepting help.** This is the
   load-bearing one. Both her profiles refuse help; the only door a Social Five with an SP Three undercurrent
   walks through voluntarily is one framed as *her providing value*, not receiving rescue. Whatever Human #1
   puts in front of her has to read as a job, a commission, a problem that needs her specifically — a
   transaction in which she is the competent party.
3. **The pull is recognition, not escape.** The Social Five moves toward the field where expertise is seen.
   Her threshold is someone wanting *her* work, by name, for a reason.
4. **It must cost her the institutional home of her competence.** The railyard is the one place her skill is
   legible and structurally supported. Leaving means giving that up with nothing equivalent waiting. For a Five
   whose entire security system is *capability = safety*, that — not the geography — is the actual threshold.
5. **She refuses at least one form of help within the same sequence.** Small, in-character, unremarked:
   carrying her own gear, declining an advance, turning down an arrangement that would make it easier. Plant
   the flaw at the door so the arc has something to travel.
6. **The door closes in a way specific to her legal status.** She's property in a pre-personhood era. Her
   leaving is not a human quitting a job — there's an ownership or contractual dimension, and whatever it is,
   it must make return impossible rather than merely unappealing. This is the version of "the old rules no
   longer apply" that only a non-person protagonist can have.
7. **Her expertise is demonstrated on the page before she goes.** The reader needs to see the thing she's good
   at, so they know what she's surrendering and can feel her starving for it later.
8. **Her situation must be tolerable, not abusive.** Being *used and not valued* is a different injury from
   being mistreated. If the boss reads as a villain, leaving costs her nothing and the threshold evaporates.

### CANNOT be true

1. **She cannot be rescued.** If Human #1's arrival solves her problem, the arc is finished at 20%.
2. **She cannot say what she actually wants.** "I want to be appreciated" is unavailable to her —
   SP Three cannot be caught wanting recognition. Voicing it here spends the Mirror Moment early.
3. **The choice cannot be primarily emotional.** Isolation is her defense: she thinks about the decision
   without feeling it. The feeling can be fully present in the prose and entirely absent from her awareness.
4. **It cannot be impulsive.** Scattered impulsivity is her *disintegration* direction (5→7). Leaving on a whim
   would place her unhealthy movement at a commitment beat and front-load her collapse.
5. **The choice cannot be made for her.** No firing, no layoff, no sale, no railyard closure. A Doorway is a
   commitment; passivity at her own act break is fatal.
6. **She cannot already have a second developed skill.** The established deficit has to still be true here —
   it's what the journey tests.
7. **No one at the railyard can be in a position to actually change her situation.** A colleague's private
   kindness is fine and makes excellent Care Package material; someone with the *authority* to value her
   properly would satisfy the goal and unmotivate the departure.

## Midpoint / Mirror Moment (50%)

*Framework: Snyder — False Victory or False Defeat. Bell — Who am I? What have I become? What must I become?
Or: "I'm probably going to die," where death may be physical, social, professional, or psychological.*

### Constraints that hold regardless of which shape you choose

**MUST be true**

1. **It strikes at competence-as-identity.** Whatever the event, its damage lands on *what she is for*, not on
   her safety or her relationships.
2. **It happens while she is working, succeeding, or trying at maximum.** Her collapse cannot find her idle —
   partly because it's untrue to SP Three workaholism, and partly because the beat is much crueler if her
   effort is total.
3. **The realization stays unspoken.** Interior only. If she explains herself to another character, she isn't
   a Five.
4. **It connects causally to her refusal of help.** Ideally the situation exists *because* she refused
   something earlier, or her isolation is what makes the damage complete.
5. **It exposes her stated goal as insufficient.** "A setup where I'm appreciated" must become visibly
   inadequate — not because appreciation is bad, but because she cannot accept it when offered. That's the
   psychological-death reading Bell describes: a belief that was not merely wrong but *falsely life-supporting*.
6. **Her workaholism visibly breaks down.** Either she works past the point where work helps, or she's
   prevented from working and has no idea what to do with herself. (For SP Three, downtime reads as incapacity.)
7. **The external facts are irreversible.** Even a False Victory has to be a genuine, standing victory in the
   world — the falseness is in what it fails to deliver, never in it being undone.

**CANNOT be true**

1. **Not a physical near-death.** Her death-flavor is professional/psychological. Physical jeopardy is the
   other register this book has available, and doubling it here flattens both.
2. **She cannot be helped out of it.** If a human solves it for her at 50%, her flaw is bypassed instead of
   confronted, and the second half has nothing to work on.
3. **She cannot receive the recognition she wants.** That belongs at Doorway #2 or the Finale. Granting it here
   spends the engine of the arc.
4. **She cannot announce her new self-understanding.** No line of dialogue where she states what she's realized.
5. **It cannot be coincidental.** Per the framework's own rule, only the Catalyst may be random; this must
   follow causally from what came before.
6. **It cannot leave her more capable.** A midpoint that upgrades her is the wrong direction — the back half
   needs her stripped, not equipped.
7. **It cannot resolve the A-plot** (finding Human #1, or securing the vessel). Those are 75–100% business.

### Candidate shapes — options, not a decision

These can be picked singly or combined; several overlap naturally.

- **False Victory — the work lands and buys her nothing.** She does the best work of her existence, it
  succeeds completely, and it produces none of what she was actually after. Directly attacks *my value is my
  usefulness* by delivering usefulness in full and showing it to be beside the point. Additional requirement:
  the victory must be unambiguous to everyone else, so that only she perceives the hollowness.
- **False Defeat — the professional death.** An engine she cannot fix: no parts, no tools, conditions past
  her. The one thing she is, fails. Additional requirement: the failure must be genuinely unfixable rather
  than a puzzle she solves three pages later, or it's a setback, not a death.
- **Psychological death — the belief goes.** The specific belief to kill is *my expertise will earn me
  appreciation.* Additional requirement: she must have been living on that belief visibly enough in Act 1 for
  its loss to register.
- **Atonement-flavored — confronting what holds ultimate power over her.** Her template carries an
  `ATONEMENT WITH THE FATHER` slot near the midpoint, and the framework defines that beat as confronting and
  being initiated by whatever holds ultimate power in one's life, often realizing that power is mortal and
  flawed — and *having to rely on this imperfect being for help regardless.* For a doll without legal
  personhood, the candidate for "whatever holds ultimate power" is ownership itself, with a human face on it.
  The last clause is what makes this shape interesting for her specifically: her flaw is refusing help, and
  this beat's own definition ends in forced reliance. Additional requirement: the authority figure has to be
  *flawed and available*, not an abstraction — Atonement needs someone to atone with.
- **Temptation-flavored — the thing she wants, in a corrupted form.** Her template has a `TEMPTATION` slot
  immediately before the midpoint. The tempting offer for her is recognition on terms that would return her to
  being used — appreciation as a nicer-looking version of the same arrangement. Additional requirement: the
  offer must be genuinely good, not obviously poisoned, or there's no temptation.
- **Belly of the Whale-flavored — total loss of control.** Submersion in circumstances she cannot engineer her
  way out of. Additional requirement: it has to remove her agency without removing her competence, so the
  helplessness is situational rather than a demotion.

## Doorway #2 / Break into Three / Crossing the Return Threshold (~80%)

*Framework: the moment of clarity — the hero finally understands what they need to do, and more importantly
**who they need to become** to do it. Where the A-story and B-story intertwine, and the B-story's lesson clicks
into place.*

**Who she needs to become: someone who accepts help.** Both locks have to break.

### MUST be true

1. **She accepts help — deliberately, visibly, at cost.** This is the beat her entire flaw was constructed
   for. Tolerating assistance is not enough: she must *ask*, or accept something she could plainly have
   refused. One action has to override both the Five's depletion-belief and the SP Three's self-sufficiency
   project.
2. **It is witnessed.** Structural mirror of Doorway #1: that one was decided alone and announced afterward,
   which is a Five's native mode. This one has to happen in front of someone, or the change hasn't occurred in
   the world — only in her head.
3. **The clarity is about who to become, not only what to do.** The framework's "more importantly" clause is
   explicit; a purely tactical realization doesn't satisfy this beat.
4. **It is causally enabled by the Midpoint.** She can accept help now specifically because the belief that
   made refusal necessary died at 50%.
5. **Her expertise returns, repurposed.** The Finale needs her mechanical competence operative (the vessel).
   So this beat restores her function while changing what it's *for* and who it's *with* — same skill,
   different meaning.
6. **It is a decision, with a door closing.** Same irreversibility rule as Doorway #1.
7. **It costs her the SP Three self-image.** Something she would previously have classified as weakness has to
   be done where it can be seen.
8. **The B-story's lesson is what makes acceptance possible.** (B-story currently undefined — see Open Threads.)

### CANNOT be true

1. **No one can explain her to herself.** No mentor speech delivering the realization. She has to arrive.
2. **Her goal cannot be handed to her.** Nobody says "we appreciate you" and thereby fixes it.
3. **The acceptance cannot be incidental or unnoticed by her.** It has to register, to her, as a threshold
   crossed.
4. **She cannot become a different kind of competent.** She's an engine mechanic. The transformation is
   relational, not vocational — no new vocation, no sudden second skillset.
5. **It cannot precede the Dark Night of the Soul's completion.** Moral Decision and Q-Factor sit inside that
   span and feed this beat.
6. **It cannot resolve her arc.** Doorway #2 is clarity, not achievement; the Finale still has to execute it.
7. **She cannot be talked into it.** Persuasion at an act break makes the protagonist an object.

## The Weiland Layer — One Archetype Only

### The length constraint, and what it forbids

This book is targeted at **ideally under 300 pages, possibly 300–350**. At that length, with a four-person
main cast, Lucy can realistically occupy **one single Weiland archetypal arc and no more.** That is a hard
budget, and it rules out several things that would otherwise be tempting:

1. **No arc-to-arc transition.** She does not complete her arc and begin the next one. Her arc's Resolution is
   the end of her material — full stop.
2. **The Weiland arc is a *lens on the existing beats*, not an additional set of beats.** Weiland's skeleton
   (Normal World → Inciting Event → First Plot Point → First Pinch Point → Midpoint → Second Pinch Point →
   Later False Peak → Third Plot Point → Climax → Climactic Moment → Resolution) has to be *the same events*
   as the composite beat sheet, read a second way. A 300-page book with four leads cannot afford two parallel
   beat sheets for one character.
3. **No shadow-archetype detour.** Each arc has a passive and an aggressive shadow counterpart; a doll who
   falls into shadow and is then redeemed needs room this book doesn't have.
4. **She starts at her arc's Beginning state on page one.** No pre-arc runway, no warm-up stage before the
   archetype engages.

### Assigned arc: **the Maiden Arc — "Coming of Age."** *Protected World → Real World.*

Story Type: Initiation. Symbolic setting scope: Home.

**Lie → Truth:** *"Submission to authority is necessary for survival"* → *"Personal sovereignty is necessary
for growth and survival."*

**Why this one, and not the Hero Arc — the only other live candidate at her life-stage:** the Hero's Lie is
*"my actions are insignificant in the overall scope of the world,"* and Lucy does not believe that. She knows
her work is excellent; her injury is that *others* don't value it. The Hero also begins "complacent but
unfulfilled," and she is not complacent — she's overworked and acutely dissatisfied. The Maiden's Lie, by
contrast, is a precise description of her situation: she submits to an arrangement that overworks and
undervalues her because, for property in a pre-personhood era, submission genuinely *is* what survival looks
like. Her Lie isn't a distortion she invented; it's an accurate read of her legal position, which is what
makes moving past it an actual risk rather than a correction.

### How the Maiden beats land on the three beats already specified

- **Beginning — "Provided For But Unprepared."** The railyard provides for her: a place, work, structure. It
  chafes. She follows its rules to avoid consequence, not from conviction. And her established deficit — no
  developed skill besides engines — *is* the "unprepared" half, literally.
- **Inciting Event — "Predator's Proposal."** An outside force arrives offering an apparent way out. Her
  already-written Inciting Event is Human #1 arriving in Whitehorse. These are the same event.
- **First Plot Point — "Protector Arrives" = her Doorway #1.** She accepts the proposal and takes an
  irreversible step out. The framework is explicit that the Protector figure is **not her savior** but a
  catalyst for her own change — which is the same constraint already specified above under "she cannot be
  rescued," arriving from a second direction. Also worth keeping live: the framework notes the Predator and
  the Protector **need not be different characters** — a single figure can be both.
- **Midpoint — "Identities/Loyalties/Wants Conflict."** A Moment of Truth forcing a choice between who she
  used to be and who she's becoming, in which she claims real responsibility for a choice for the first time.
  Compatible with every Midpoint shape in the menu above; it adds one requirement — whichever shape is chosen
  must contain **a choice she owns**, not only a realization she undergoes.
- **Later False Peak — "Bride Price"** (~62–75%, *not* the Midpoint). The Predator raises the cost of
  returning, tempting her to believe the sacrifice for growth wasn't worth it. Note this beat is distinct from
  the Snyder-style False Victory available at her Midpoint — they sit at different percentages and do different
  work.
- **Third Plot Point — "Wandering in the Wilderness."** Caught fully between worlds: unable to return to what
  she was, not yet arrived at what she's becoming. In this book that liminal state is also the literal
  setting, which is free thematic reinforcement worth not wasting.
- **Climactic Moment — "Comes of Age": she is now an equal.** For a doll without legal personhood, "she is now
  an equal" carries a weight it could not carry for a human character. This is the terminus her whole arc is
  aimed at, and it lands exactly on her wound: being *used* is precisely the condition of not being an equal.

### Additional MUST / CANNOT from the archetype

**MUST**

1. **Her Doorway #2 must read as sovereignty, not as rescue reversed.** Accepting help has to be *her choosing
   to be in relation* — an exercise of sovereignty, not a surrender of it. If it reads as her finally letting
   someone take care of her, the Maiden Truth is inverted.
2. **The authority she submits to must be concrete and present in Act 1**, not an abstraction. The arc needs
   something specific to be cast off at the Resolution.
3. **She must claim responsibility for a choice at the Midpoint** — see above.

**CANNOT**

1. **She cannot end the book having begun a second arc.** No gesture toward what comes next.
2. **She cannot be granted equality; she must arrive at it.** Legal status changing around her, or a human
   declaring her an equal, would hand her the Climactic Moment instead of letting her reach it.
3. **The Protector cannot solve her arc.** Catalyst only — the framework is explicit.

## Open Threads

1. **Arc placement vs. book placement — a real blocking decision.** The premise has Human #1 going radio-silent
   *some time after* leaving Whitehorse, which means the book's two doll arcs cannot both sit at the book's own
   20/50/80 marks unless the story runs parallel tracks from page one. Either it does, or the other arc is
   offset and runs on its own clock. This changes pacing substantially and should be settled before beats get
   written. Everything above is placement-independent and holds under either choice.
2. **Her departure shares its mechanism with the book's other doll arc** — leaving alongside a departing human
   man. The constraints above deliberately push her *reason* toward recognition-of-expertise so the two
   sequences don't read as the same scene twice, but the pair should be checked against each other directly.
3. **B-story undefined**, and Doorway #2 can't be fully specified without it, since the framework requires the
   B-story's lesson to click into place there.
4. **Fake Ally and Opposition undefined.** Her template carries slots for both, including `FAKE-ALLY ATTACK` in
   the run-up to the midpoint. The framework's definition is specific: not a traitor, but someone acting in
   genuine accordance with their own interests, which happen to conflict with hers.
5. **Care Package undefined** — the setup half of a pair whose Q-Factor payoff at ~78% feeds directly into
   Doorway #2 above.
6. **Physical description unwritten** despite 15 reference images existing at `../../doll/Lucy Grayson/`.
7. **Her arc template still carries no Weiland slots.** Her archetype is now assigned (see the Weiland Layer
   section above), but `y-template/structure/character arcs/doll.md` — and therefore her own arc file — has no
   rows for the archetypal beats. Since the archetype is meant to be a lens over the same events rather than a
   second beat sheet, this may not need fixing at all; if it does, it's a line-wide change to the canonical
   template.
