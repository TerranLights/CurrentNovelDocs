I have not read the books in full yet; the chapter-level judgments below come from their tables of contents and a keyword count, not from the chapters themselves.

## The film's needs (AUTHOR, from the Author Statements file)
Lead is a robot (Garcelle) and the audience stays with her; the scavengers die from their own vices, not stupidity; **psychological, liminal, metaphysical and somewhat paranormal horror, with minimal or no body horror**; the paranormal element is a tear in space-time, not ghosts; a derelict wheel station; 85 to 120 minutes; the author's only horror work, so it should be built with care.

## Chapter-level map and relevance to the film
### A. *A Sense of Dread* (Stevens): HIGH
- **Contents (read):** 1 the biological basis of fear (startle response, the brain); 2 psychologically based fears (instinctive, cross-over, learned, phobias; nightmares and nightmare motifs); 3 fears based in culture (paranoid fears, possession and mind control, secret codes, intruders, madness, authority and family institutions corrupted, urban legends); 4 **the toolbox of dread** ("the emotional reaction is in the structure, not in the surprise"); 5 putting theory into practice (a script worked through); conclusion "Why horror?"
- **Most relevant:** the fear catalogues (fear of being trapped or paralyzed, being lost, being pursued, **inability to flee**, bodily or identity change, madness, **possession and mind control**, sinister codes); the nightmare motifs, which are close to liminal logic; the toolbox chapter. Exercises are attached to each chapter.
- **Extraction value:** a vocabulary of specific fears to give each scavenger and each location, and the author's rule that dread is built in structure.

### B. *Writing Fear* (Cubero): HIGH
- **Contents (read):** 1 introduction to horror and subgenres (gore, **psychological**, killers, monsters, **paranormal**); 2 psychology of fear (why we like being scared; phobia, tension, anxiety, horror, shock, and how to combine them); 3 **premise and theme** (the central fear, the monster as metaphor, **thematic conflict: how horror confronts human desires**); 4 **characters** (antihero, final girl, monster; **the relationship between monster and hero: opposite reflections or two sides of the same coin**; backstory and secrets); 5 **structure** (three acts, turning points, cliffhangers, pacing, **alternative structures: experimental and fragmented**); 6 scenes of fear (the unseen threat, jump scares, **silence and sound**, cadence); 7 **setting and atmosphere** (**the environment as a character**, sensory detail); 8 dialogue and subtext; 9 **audiovisual technique** (editing, sound design, light and shadow); 10 **monster and threat design** (**rationing information about the threat**); 11 endings (types, emotional impact, sequel or closure); 12 analyses of five classic scripts (*The Exorcist*, *Psycho*, *The Blair Witch Project*, *Hereditary*, *Halloween*); 13 to 15 selling and the market.
- **Most relevant:** monster-and-hero relationship, thematic conflict, experimental structure, the environment as a character, silence and sound, rationing the threat. The best fit to the film's two hard problems: a **monster who is the lead**, and a **setting that is itself the threat**.
- **Note:** chapters 13 on (selling, markets) are lower value for now.

### C. *Scream Writing* (Long): HIGH
- **Contents (read):** 1 the genre (definition, historical trends, subgenres); 2 **monsters** (monsters as metaphors, designing them, origins, **the origin of human evil**, ecology and biology, the supernatural, possession and repressed desires, weaknesses, **staging the big reveal**); 3 **scares** (fear, shock, jump scares, **decoy protagonists and hero reversals**, endings and expectations, disgust, **anxiety**, suspense and tension, atmosphere); 4 **settings** (overused locations, **psychological baggage and places of disempowerment**, location as threat, **places of isolation and confinement**, locational mood); 5 **plots** (**the seven struggles of horror: mystery, survival, escape, disaster, transformation, madness, menace, and combining them**; story structure including Syd Field's paradigm and **Dan Harmon's Story Circle**; the human element; hidden meanings; momentum; triggering events); each chapter ends with exercises.
- **Most relevant:** the "seven struggles" taxonomy (the film is plausibly **menace** from Garcelle's side and **survival or escape** from the scavengers'; **madness** and **mystery** for the tear), settings of isolation, hero reversals and decoy protagonists, and the Harmon Story Circle, which the author already uses.
- **Overlap:** substantial with B; the two together give two independent treatments of the same ground.

### D. *Horror Screenwriting: The Nature of Fear* (Watson): MEDIUM-HIGH
- **Contents (read):** 1 history of horror film; 2 the nature of fear; 3 types of horror and the toolkit; 4 scenes, pacing and avoiding "talking heads"; 5 dialogue; 6 **the life of a horror character** (plotting a character's **lifeline** through the story; **why horror has disposable characters**; how plot points come from characters' actions); 7 completing the first draft (twists, tension, **"story velocity"** measured in a quantifiable way); 8 tips, copyright, agents.
- **Most relevant:** the **character lifeline** and "disposable characters", which bear directly on the author's idea that each scavenger's vice kills them; pacing; measuring velocity.
- **Lower value:** chapter 8 and the history chapter (useful background, not essential).

### E. *How to Write a Horror Screenplay* (Fields): MEDIUM
- **Contents (read):** five parts: foundations; **from idea to structure** (idea to concept, logline and story engine, **characters as structural tools**, **the antagonistic force: defined by how it operates, not by its appearance**, **rules, cause and effect, escalation**); three-act structure for horror (acts one, two, three); scenes, scares and execution (scene craft, **scares and setpieces**, **violence, suggestion and restraint**); revision (common structural mistakes, rewriting for fear, logic and rhythm, formatting).
- **Most relevant:** the **rules and escalation** chapter (the rules of the tear), the **antagonist-by-operation** idea (fits a threat that is a place and a mind), and **violence and restraint** (the "minimal body horror" constraint).
- **Caution:** very short (about 600 words per chapter on average), so each chapter is a summary; useful as a checklist, not as a source of depth.

### F. *The Biology of Horror* (Morgan): LOW-MEDIUM, selective
- **Contents (read):** introduction "The Body Is His Book"; 1 mortal coils: the comic-horror double helix; 2 the muse of horror (traditions of dreadful imagining); 3 macabre aesthetics; 4 the anxiety of organism; 5 America and the muse of horror; 6 dark carnival; 7 **languishment: the wounded hero**; 8 **sinister loci: the properties of terror**; 9 apotropaion and the hideous obscure; 10 **the soul at zero: dark epiphanies**.
- **Why selective:** the book's argument is that horror is fundamentally **bio-horror**, the body as the site of dread, which is **the opposite of the author's "minimal body horror"** for a robot lead. It is the densest and slowest of the six (academic prose).
- **Most relevant chapters:** 7 (the **wounded hero**, close to Garcelle's burned eyes and the lead who is also the threat), 8 (**places of terror**, for the station), 10 (**dark epiphanies**, the moment of self-knowledge, for her arc and the climax), and chapter 9 (a derelict ship and clashing frames of reference, which the extraction found more useful than expected). *Correction after reading (2026-10-20):* the book uses "uncanny" only as a loose label and builds no theory on it beyond Freud's *heimlich* and *unheimlich* ("liminal" appears only four times), so it is **not** a developed source on liminal horror.

## What the shelf does not cover (computed and PROPOSED)
- **Liminal, uncanny or "eerie" horror is thin.** A keyword count (computed) found "uncanny" or "liminal" far more often in Morgan's book (47 lines) than in the five screenwriting guides (5 or fewer each), but *reading* Morgan shows those are mostly a loose general label ("liminal" four times), so **none of the six books develops liminal horror**. The film's central mood, **liminal and metaphysical horror**, gets almost no direct treatment; the Graymatter breakdown video partly fills it.
- **Robots, artificial minds and science-fiction horror are barely covered** (a few mentions per book; computed). A robot lead who is the threat is not a case any of these books centers.
- **The monster as the protagonist** is covered only in passing: Cubero's monster-and-hero relationship and antihero sections are the nearest.
- **PROPOSED additions to the shelf, if the author wants them (my memory, not checked): ⚠️M:** Freud, *The Uncanny* (1919); Mark Fisher, *The Weird and the Eerie* (2016), on eerie and weird fiction; Marc Augé, *Non-Places* (1992), the idea behind many "liminal" spaces; Noël Carroll, *The Philosophy of Horror* (1990), on monsters and horror's emotional logic; Carol J. Clover, *Men, Women, and Chain Saws* (1992). None is a screenwriting guide; the author decides whether any is worth owning.

## Proposed extraction plan (PROPOSED; awaiting the author's go-ahead)
- **Order:** A (Stevens), B (Cubero), C (Long), D (Watson), E (Fields), then F (Morgan) chapters 7, 8, 10, the introduction, and the uncanny passages.
- **Method:** one reading agent per book, reading it in full and writing **my own structured summary** to `horror-craft-extractions/` in this folder, with the chapter cited for every item. **No copied text** beyond short attributed quotations (the book files are copyrighted, as with the rest of the shelf).
- **What each extraction captures:** (1) frameworks, taxonomies and lists (for example the seven struggles, the fear catalogues); (2) rules and techniques, stated as usable instructions; (3) the films and scripts each author cites, with what each illustrates (a **watch-list** the author can use); (4) each book's exercises that fit this film; (5) a short "**application to the Garcelle film**" section per chapter, labeled PROPOSED; (6) contradictions or disagreements with the other books.
- **Then a synthesis:** one document comparing the six on the film's questions (monster as lead, the vice-driven deaths, the tear's rules, the setting as threat, the ending), and listing the gaps above.
- **Cost:** about 313,000 words in total (computed), so roughly six parallel readers, each doing one book.
