## 1. A monster who is the lead

### 1.1 What the six books say

**The headline: only one book gives the situation a name.** Five of the six assume a victim-protagonist and a separate antagonist; their gap sections say so explicitly (A sec 5; B sec 5; D "weakly or not at all"; E "weakly or not at all"; F sec 6). Only C builds a structure around a monster as the lead. Its seventh "struggle," **menace**, is the one struggle with an archetypal character: it takes the monster from the monsters chapter and makes it the protagonist, so the audience holds a privileged view of a menace that cannot be stopped, and the fear shifts from "will the hero survive" to "what will it do next." The monster seeks victims instead of being found (C ch 5). That is the film's Garcelle-side structure, and Long calls menace films "the most interesting (and most unpleasant)" kind.

**Whose story it is, and alignment.**
- D treats the audience's identification with a character as "the most important objective" and builds it from shared ordinary experience, but assumes the identifying character is the human protagonist (D ch 6).
- A shows how to choose the viewpoint character for dread: the opening of *Night of the Living Dead* deliberately uses the most helpless and uninformed person, so the audience meets the catastrophe at their own level of knowledge (A ch 4).
- B's monster archetype has a narrative function that matches Garcelle's relation to the scavengers: sustain tension and "catalyze internal conflict" among the protagonists so their true selves surface (B ch 4). B also offers two exercises that put the writer in the monster's head: a scene from the antagonist's point of view, and "what does it feel like to be horror incarnate" (B ch 4, ch 15).
- B's control theory of why we enjoy fear suggests a safe-experience reason a monster-lead can work: the viewer holds the upper hand (B ch 2). B does not apply this to a monster-lead; that is my extension.

**How to earn understanding without excusing.**
- C's primer-and-trigger model for human monsters: a past wound (the primer) plus a present event that sets the killing off (the trigger). The rule that matters: the audience should be able to **fathom** the acts, not necessarily **relate** to them (C ch 2). C also warns against assigning monstrousness to a trait such as an illness or sexual orientation, and says mental illness shown as monstrous needs dimension (C ch 2).
- A argues that torture, mutilation and cannibalism are largely socialized responses, that the people who commit them are "all too human" rather than monsters, and that we also fear such acts as **dark temptations in ourselves** (A ch 3). A also supplies the reversal mechanism: "the helpless as victimizers," where the figure the audience protects becomes the menace (A ch 3).
- B's **tragic villain** (Norman Bates: dangerous and pathetically human) and the protagonist qualities of empathy, transformation and flaws-and-contradictions give the vocabulary (B ch 4).
- D's psychological branch lists **revenge** among the motives and cites the first *Friday the 13th*, a killer driven by a sympathetic wound whose victims are not the people who caused it; D also reframes *Frankenstein* so the creator and the crowd are the monsters and the creature is pitiable (D ch 3). The first is very close to the film's displaced revenge (the Brief: the scavengers are strangers, not the Garrison guard).
- F's avengers, Montressor, Hop Frog and Mrs. Voorhees, are the book's patient, wounded hunters; Montressor ends his ritual feeling flat (F ch 6). F's **host and visitor** pattern (a wounded host in a decayed place, a visitor who tries to heal and fails) is the nearest to Garcelle as the lead, but F's wounded figure is passive, and F says its own wounded figure who hunts is outside the book (F ch 7).
- E warns that explaining an antagonist's origin weakens fear, and says nothing about an antagonist the audience might root for (E ch 7).

**How revenge films earn their violence.** C's key sentence for the film: menace-revenge films (*Ms. 45*, *I Spit on Your Grave*, *Mandy*, *Revenge*) "turn from survival to menace," and **the time spent on survival justifies the ruthless violence of menace**; without the survival half the protagonists would be monsters, and once menace begins their acts are as brutal as any monster's (C ch 5). Garcelle's survival phase (the Garrison, the shocks, the launch) is offscreen and precedes the opening, so the film must find another way to deliver it or another source of connection (see 1.4).

**Protagonist swap, decoy protagonist, hero reversal.**
- B: the midpoint of *Psycho* removes the apparent lead and shifts focus to Norman, "the true axis of the horror" (B ch 5, ch 12).
- C: a **decoy protagonist** is the apparent lead killed early (*Psycho*, *Scream*); a **hero reversal** is a capable, avenging figure killed late (*The Stepfather*, *Friday the 13th: The Final Chapter*), stronger because more investment has accrued. C warns that horror audiences have long memories and both can degrade into tricks (C ch 3).
- The film's version is unusual: the audience knows from minute one that Garcelle is the hunter, so the decoy is not a death but an **expectation**: the optimistic crew read as the protagonists until it becomes clear whose story it is (B ch 5 application; C ch 3 application).

**The triangular structure** (C ch 5, from *Red, White & Blue*): a revenge story built on a base of two "first acts" (both pointing at one third act), which delays the payoff by following the perpetrator, drops the usual middle act of preparation and growing confidence, offers compassion to both sides, and slows the pace so the violence lands harder. The film's first two scenes (Garcelle's kill, then the crew) are already the two base points.

**The monster's own vice or flaw.** None of the books builds a flaw for a monster-lead. What they offer: every monster needs a weakness, and the strongest ones are **tied to what the monster represents** (Freddy feeds on fear, so the heroine beats him by taking her fear back) (C ch 2); a supernatural force can be read as a corruptor that works on the protagonist's weakness (the Overlook and Jack's drinking) (C ch 2); a flaw is structural when it restricts options under stress (E ch 6); and the "real roadblock" in a story is usually the character, not the villain (D ch 4). F's slasher reading adds that revenge figures can be flat afterward (F ch 6).

**The Frankenstein-style reading, and its cheapness.** The idea that the human is the real monster runs through the shelf: D (ch 1 man as the new monster after *Psycho*; ch 3 the *Frankenstein* reframing), B (ch 1 the Golem and *Frankenstein* as made beings turning on makers; the Universal tradition of the misunderstood monster), C (ch 2 *Nightbreed*, where the human is the real monster; *Frankenstein* as a metaphor for many things), A (ch 3 cruelty is human). The books also contain the guard rails: C says audiences will assign a meaning to the monster whatever the writer intends, so think through readings and avoid accidentally feeding hatred (C ch 2); F warns that horror's visceral triggers can be turned to political ends and that Lovecraft's alien-takeover dread is xenophobic projection (F intro; F ch 9); F also holds that the strongest horror gives no authoritative moral voice (F ch 9).

**The robot-mirroring remark (UNSOURCED).** C says the brain mirrors a watched human but not a watched robot, and uses this to argue shocks land best when a **character visibly reacts**, and that disempowering places work through mirroring (C ch 3, ch 4). The extraction flags the study as vague and unnamed (C sec 6), and I could not check it; treat it as an author's assertion, not a finding. D's opposite assumption is that identification can come from shared experience (D ch 6). A's uncanny-valley passage (A ch 2) covers near-human figures but only for deformity and CGI, so its use for a gynoid is the extraction's extension.

### 1.2 Usable techniques and rules

1. **Give the audience her survival half somewhere** (C ch 5). Revenge violence is earned by showing the wrong done; decide the delivery (see 1.4).
2. **Let the audience fathom, not relate** (C ch 2): primer (the Garrison) plus trigger (each new boarding party). Avoid blaming a trait; do not make her damaged eyes or lost memory the reason she is dangerous.
3. **Use dramatic irony as the engine** (C ch 3, Hitchcock's bomb under the table; A ch 4, the *Apollo 13* re-entry): suspense does not require empathy with the hunter, only knowledge. The opening already supplies that knowledge.
4. **Give a monster a weakness and tie it to what she represents** (C ch 2); give the lead a flaw that restricts her options (E ch 6; D ch 4).
5. **Plant a decoy expectation, then reverse it** (B ch 5; C ch 3): the audience picks a likely survivor among the crew; the vice-driven death of that person is the hero reversal.
6. **Write her interior as behavior** (E ch 1): what she touches, refuses, where she waits. Reveal history through marks and objects, keep the tear's origin unexplained (E ch 7, my reconciliation in the extraction).
7. **Discovery in every scene** (D ch 6): each Garcelle scene should let the audience discover something new about her (optics, restraint, cruelty or mercy).
8. **Use the monster's catalyzing function** (B ch 4): her presence exists to bring out the scavengers' true selves.
9. **Check the reading** (C ch 2): list the likely interpretations of "a robot who kills humans because she hates them" before locking the script.

### 1.3 Films that best illustrate it (from the extractions' watch-lists)

- *Henry: Portrait of a Serial Killer* (menace; unknowable killer; shock by character identification) (C ch 2, ch 3, ch 5).
- *Ms. 45*, *I Spit on Your Grave*, *Mandy*, *Revenge* (revenge as menace) and *Red, White & Blue* (triangular structure) (C ch 5).
- *Psycho* (focus swap; tragic villain) (B ch 5, ch 12; C ch 3), *Friday the 13th* (revenge motive) (D ch 3), *Scream* (primer and trigger; decoy) (C ch 2, ch 3).
- *Frankenstein* (1931) and the Golem films (D ch 1, ch 3; B ch 1); *2001* (HAL as the technological monster) (B ch 10); *The Terminator* for how to describe a robot in one sentence (C ch 2).
- *Night of the Living Dead* (vulnerable viewpoint) (A ch 4); *The Mist* (logic failing at the end) (C ch 3); *Halloween* as the counter-model of an unexplained villain (B ch 12; C ch 2).
- F's literary avengers: "The Cask of Amontillado" and "Hop Frog" (F ch 6).

### 1.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED)

**Alignment options.** (a) *Hers throughout:* the audience hunts with her; the scavengers are seen through her attention. (b) *Split by sequence:* hers for the cold open and her scenes, theirs for the crew's scenes, which is the triangular structure's logic (C ch 5), with dramatic irony binding the two. (c) *Hers first, theirs in the middle, hers again:* sympathy shifts as the crew become specific people, and returns, uneasily, at the end. **Recommendation (PROPOSED):** (b), because the books agree that fear needs characters the audience believes in (C ch 3; D ch 6) and the author's own plan gives the scavengers exposition. Long's warning about two emotional bids producing a third, unplanned effect (C ch 5) means the ambivalence may be the film's real subject; decide that deliberately.

**Delivering her survival half** (the Garrison). Options: (1) fragments through objects and behavior in her lair and routines (E ch 7; C ch 4 on objects as psychology); (2) place-echoes that recall Cell E without a flashback: a chair, light in the eyes, a mass-driver sound (C ch 4 on places of disempowerment); (3) one concentrated delivery near the midpoint, matching Bell's Mirror Moment questions (Structure file; see sec 6); (4) a scavenger finds a record or sees her damage and understands; (5) a flashback sequence, which D's lifeline permits ("storylines can hop around the lifeline") (D ch 6). The film's Garcelle file lists what happened after the mass driver as open, so the film can invent that interval without conflict.

**Making the revenge cost something.** Because the people she kills are strangers, the books' revenge logic needs a gap filled: either her class-wide hatred is the engine (the Garcelle file: "the only good human is a dead human," quoted in the Brief), and the film asks whether the vice-rule is justice or rationalization; or the displacement itself is the wound. F's Montressor (feels flat afterward) is a precedent for the second (F ch 6).

**Her own vice or flaw (options, not decisions).** From the Garcelle file and the books' definitions of structural flaws (E ch 6): (a) *patient withdrawal as avoidance* (the 8w9 waits, endures; the flaw is that waiting has become a way not to live); (b) *judgment by class* (she applies the vice-rule to people as examples of a category, and the rule is her own invention); (c) *pride in "you cannot take what I don't let you take"* (the Garcelle file's moral tendency) becoming an inability to accept any help or being seen; (d) *excess* (the Brief lists lust/excess as the Enneagram 8 passion, ⚠️M, the author's system, not a book's): too much ritual, too much relish; (e) *dependence on the revenge* itself as the reason to continue. The symmetry is available: she kills others for flaws, and the film can let her own flaw shape what happens to her (C ch 2: weakness tied to meaning).

**Need versus want (leave open).** Her want is revenge (Brief). Candidate needs, each consistent with the books' tools: to be named or seen (the lost name); to stop (the Dark Night of the Soul as exhaustion); to be unwitnessed and left alone (her 8w9 core); to accept help once (inverting F's failed healing); to leave the station; to learn whether her rule is right (the Mist logic-shock, C ch 3); to end the loop. The structure file's Truby pair (Moral Decision tied to a flaw set up in Act 1; Self-Revelation) and Bell's Mirror Moment give the test: the need should be the thing her flaw prevents. The Garcelle file lists weakness, goal and internal conflict as TBD, so nothing contradicts any option.

**Avoiding the cheap "humans are the real monsters" reading.** The books supply guard rails; from them I propose: (1) make every scavenger specific and give each a trait that cuts against the vice, so they are not a category (D lifeline template, cautions; F ch 4 on introducing flat types, then making them real); (2) let the film **not** endorse her class judgment: the camera can show the kills with her logic and the audience can still see the cost (A ch 3 on cruelty being human; F ch 9 on the absence of a moral voice); (3) distinguish death by vice (causal) from punishment (moral): the books' slasher morality (F ch 6) rewards discipline and is conservative, and a vice film risks being read that way, so avoid a surviving "pure" character unless it is the point; (4) keep the Garrison's cruelty human-sized and specific rather than abstract, as A's argument would suggest (A ch 3); (5) check the political reading (C ch 2; F intro).

**The mirroring problem (flagged UNSOURCED).** If viewers do not mirror a robot, the film should (PROPOSED): (a) lean on dramatic irony and structure, which do not need empathy (C ch 3); (b) give her human-readable behavioral tells, especially the eyes, which are the one expressive feature the opening already emphasizes; (c) use the scavengers as mirrors for fear (their reactions carry the audience's fear, C ch 3), and design what the audience feels toward Garcelle separately: understanding, dread, recognition, perhaps complicity; (d) use witnesses: a scavenger who sees her and reacts as the book's character-reaction rule asks. Because the claim is unverified, a test screening of a rough cut with and without her face shots would be a cheap check (not a book recommendation).

### 1.5 What the books do not cover

- Staging kills from the killer's point of view, and keeping suspense when the audience is aligned with the hunter (A sec 5; C sec 5; B sec 5).
- How a monster-lead changes (B notes the lead should transform and does not discuss a monster-lead who barely changes) (B ch 3 application).
- A robot's inner life, an audience's reaction to a machine's pain, machine vengeance (A sec 5; B sec 5; C sec 5; D; E; F sec 6).
- An ending whose valence splits: cathartic for her, pessimistic for them (B ch 11 application).
- A wounded figure who hunts (F ch 7).

## 2. Vice-driven deaths and the scavenger cast

### 2.1 What the six books say

**No book teaches this directly, but four give the pieces.** The extractions agree that nothing on the shelf is a method for a cast of victims each killed by a personal flaw (A sec 5; B sec 5; C sec 5; D "ensembles where each death is individually motivated"; E "ensembles and kill order"; F sec 6 "individualized vices as plot engine"). The nearest tools:

- **D's lifeline method** is the strongest. Draw a line from birth to death; the story occupies one stretch; mark **motivational incidents** before the story that explain why the person acts as they do, and an **incident line** inside the story (D ch 6). D's own caution: his "disposable characters" exist to prove the danger and then die (D ch 2), which is the wrong frame for a crew whose flaws are the point; only the cold-open victim fits it. D adds: stress reveals character, and each person should solve the same crisis differently, tied to profession and temperament (D ch 7); the real roadblock is usually the character (D ch 4); introduce people through behavior, not summary (D ch 3); plant a throwaway detail early and pay it off (D ch 7); the steno-pad character sketch (D ch 8).
- **E's structural characters.** Characters are defined by what they trigger. A flaw is structural when it restricts options, delays the right action or raises cost; secondary characters often exist to **demonstrate consequence before it reaches the lead**; passive characters weaken fear; no one may break a rule without cost (E ch 6). E's mistake list includes "characters who do not affect the outcome" (E ch 16).
- **C on possession and haunting.** The supernatural force finds the victim's weakness and often gives them what they secretly want (Josh's rage, Mia's withdrawal); the *Shining* reading makes the Overlook a corruptor aimed at one person's drinking and stress (C ch 2). C also gives the mirror image: in the Leprechaun example the monster's own greed becomes the exploit, so a flaw can doom a character and briefly save another (C ch 2). C's suspense section: characters whose flaws make them **suppress their doubts** turn dramatic irony into tension (C ch 3).
- **F on vice-fitted punishment.** Medieval and Renaissance art imagined torments suited to Avarice, Gluttony and Vanity; *Seven* revives it; and F's slasher chapter reads indulgence and neglect as what the genre punishes, but warns this is a conservative demand for discipline, with survivors rewarded for it (F ch 2, ch 6). F's languishment chapter gives vice a visible state: torpor, entrancement and lassitude as the organism giving up (F ch 7).

**Fear-to-vice maps.** A does not link fears to vices; the A extraction's proposed table (A 3d) pairs each vice with a fear or technique from the book and a body-free mechanism (see 2.4). B's phobia (B ch 2) and secrets (B ch 4) are the other levers. B's thematic-conflict rule is the closest to the author's design: **connect the dilemma to the threat so the monster exposes it** (B ch 3).

**How many scavengers, and functions.** No book gives a number. Functions the books name: the vulnerable viewpoint (A ch 4); the insider antagonist who sabotages and turns the group on itself (D ch 7, "Bryant"); the person who demonstrates consequence first (E ch 6); the likely hero to reverse (C ch 3); the skeptical expert who notices anomalies (D ch 4); the final girl (B ch 4); the Mist-style factions in containment (C ch 4). D's worked example uses five science-team members plus a commander (D ch 3).

**The stupid-victim trope.** C's rule is the one to hold: characters should act consistently with their established values and know nothing they could not know, or they fall into a kind of uncanny valley of behavior (C ch 3). E's trap: "the rule they are most likely to break" invites a dumb mistake, so reword it as the vice **limiting options** (E ch 6 application). D's template test is the sharpest: could a wise version of this person have survived? If yes, the death is stupidity, not vice (the D extraction's lifeline template, row 11).

**Exposition for the scavengers.** D's mission-briefing scene: common, quick, dies as "talking heads" unless character-building is folded in (D ch 3; *Event Horizon* precedent). B: subtext, where words and actions diverge, reveals flaws without naming them (B ch 8). A: foreshadow by an object and cut to reactions before reveals (A ch 5). D: break dialogue with action and give everyone something to do (D ch 2, ch 4). E: scenes get shorter as danger rises (E ch 17), so early scenes can be longer and warmer.

**The Enneagram.** None of the six books uses it (the extractions do not mention it). The nine passions listed in the Brief (1 anger/resentment, 2 pride, 3 deceit/vanity, 4 envy, 5 avarice, 6 fear, 7 gluttony, 8 lust/excess, 9 sloth) are ⚠️M and part of the author's own system. The books' vices overlap with them only incidentally: avarice, gluttony and vanity (F ch 2), indulgence and neglect (F ch 6), rage and withdrawal as repressed weaknesses (C ch 2), drink (C ch 2).

### 2.2 Usable techniques and rules

1. **Make the vice a limiting choice, not a mistake** (E ch 6, ch 16; D ch 4). Each scavenger's vice should be visible in how they solve the shared crisis (D ch 7).
2. **Three beats per vice:** shown early by behavior, tested in the middle where it costs something small, used against them at the end (D ch 6, ch 7 as adapted in the extraction; E ch 11 escalation cycle; the Brief's own proposal).
3. **Lifeline sheet per person** (the D extraction's lifeline template, section d): sketch, vice and the roadblock it creates, three motivational incidents (where the vice began; when it paid off; when it cost and they continued), the three incident beats, stress response, pivotal scene, plant-and-payoff detail, speech habits, what the audience learns about Garcelle or the station from them, the death, a gauge score.
4. **Keep a cut-against trait** (generosity in a miser) so people are not flat (D ch 6 on transparency; D template caution 2).
5. **First death teaches the rule; later ones tighten it** (E ch 6 application; E ch 8).
6. **Vary the tier of each death** so five identical punishments do not read as a pattern instead of an escalation (D lifeline template, cautions 3; E ch 16 "same location, same outcome").
7. **Let a vice also be the lock** that keeps them aboard: greed to stay, pride to refuse help (C ch 4 application of "what prevents characters from leaving"); charged places are emotional, not only physical (C ch 4).
8. **Let a death change the survivors' behavior and options** (E ch 15, ch 6): no death is a body count.
9. **Plant and pay off** one object or phrase per person (D ch 7; A ch 5).
10. **Use an inside antagonist for human-to-human vice conflict** (D ch 7) and containment breakdown (C ch 4, *The Mist*), so scenes without Garcelle still escalate.

### 2.3 Films that best illustrate it

*The Shining* (corruptor; drink as the weakness) (C ch 2, ch 4; B ch 7); *Seven* (sin-shaped killings) (F intro, ch 2); *Friday the 13th* and *Halloween* (indulgence punished) (F ch 6); *The Mist* (containment, factions) (C ch 3, ch 4); *The Thing* (paranoia in a group; stress reveals) (A ch 4; B ch 3; C ch 4); *Event Horizon* (the briefing; experts) (D ch 1, ch 3); *Hereditary* (a death off-screen as demonstration; legacy of flaws) (E ch 6; B ch 12); *Don't Breathe* (greed meets a dangerous "helpless" occupant; A's extraction notes this as its own extension, A watch-list); *1408* (a space that tailors torment to the visitor) (C ch 4); *Poltergeist II* and *Insidious* (weaknesses exposed) (C ch 2).

### 2.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED)

**Cast size.** No book gives a number. D's example (five plus a commander), E's suggestion of roughly three or four escalation cycles in Act Two (E ch 11 application), and the 85 to 120 minute length suggest **five scavengers plus the cold-open victim** as a working figure, with one possibly dying outside a full cycle (for example, the hero-reversal death that doubles as the midpoint). If the author wants fewer, four gives each more screen time. This is my reasoning, not a book's rule.

**Function map (menu).** Pick roles so no two scavengers do the same job: (1) the likely leader or "hero" (reversal candidate, C ch 3); (2) the vulnerable viewpoint, the least informed (A ch 4); (3) the skeptic or expert who notices anomalies in professional terms (D ch 4); (4) the insider whose secret endangers the group (D ch 7; B ch 4 secrets); (5) the one who most resembles Garcelle in some way (the mirror, F ch 10; see sec 7); optionally (6) the demonstrator of consequence (E ch 6), who can be the first to die and who teaches the tear's rule. A person can hold two roles.

**A fear-and-technique map for vices (menu, from A 3d and B ch 2; no gore required).** The mechanism is the book's: the vice makes a space feel safe, then the tear violates that safety (A 3d note; A intro).

| Vice (generic; the author's list may differ) | Book fear or technique that dresses the build-up | Body-free mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Avarice | Curiously afraid (A ch 1); narrow space (A ch 4) | The prize is the lure; each step toward it is the approach walk; the vault becomes the closing space |
| Vanity or deceit | The watching self (A ch 2); mask or painted face (A ch 3) | The tear shows a version of them that acts independently; the mask the crew sees is what fails |
| Pride | Humiliation (A ch 2); being lost (A ch 2) | Refuses to admit a looping corridor; the shame of being seen lost is the trap |
| Sloth or fear | Heavy feet, sleep paralysis (A ch 2) | Stays put or rests; the sense of a presence arrives while immobile |
| Gluttony or appetite | Intimate acts violated (A ch 3) | A private indulgence in a "cleared" room is intruded upon |
| Envy | Double; corrupted trusted person (A ch 2, ch 3) | A crewmate appears to receive what they covet |
| Anger or resentment | Pursuit; loss of self (A ch 2, ch 3) | Chases a voice that leads away, acts on feelings that are not their own |
| Fear as a flaw | Pattern-seeking; hearing your name (A ch 3) | The tear speaks to them in their own anxious language |
| Curiosity or obsession | Barriers made of smoke (A ch 5) | Solves the station's puzzle; completing it pushes them through |
| Excess or addiction | Possession or mind control (A ch 3) | The habit is the occupying force and the tear supplies it |

**Exposition without slowing the film.** (1) Use the briefing scene on the crew ship as the main delivery, with the vices in behavior while they talk about the salvage plan (D ch 3 application: a man pockets something, a woman steers the money talk). (2) Words versus action as subtext (B ch 8). (3) One planted object each (A ch 5; D ch 7). (4) Let the early scenes be warm, longer and funnier, then shorten them as danger rises (E ch 17). (5) Use stress-response differences for the second dose of character in the first crisis (D ch 7). (6) Reserve a few recorded fragments or logs for each (D ch 7 playback) rather than speeches.

**The Enneagram as a spine.** Using the nine passions gives each scavenger a ready vice and the author already uses the system, so it would be consistent. The cautions from the books: variety of death tiers (D template), cut-against traits (D ch 6), and that nine passions overshoot a cast of five, so choose the ones that give the most contrasting stress responses. The A table above shows which book technique can dress each; the choice of passions is the author's.

### 2.5 What the books do not cover

- Building a cast of victims who are each an arc of their own (D "disposables"; E "ensembles").
- Kill order and pacing of an ensemble's deaths (E).
- Making vice the cause of death while avoiding both stupidity and moralizing (A sec 5; C sec 5).
- A cast that is hated by the lead as a class but must be individually feared for.

## 3. The tear: rules, escalation, consistency

### 3.1 What the six books say

**Agreement: a metaphysical element needs rules, and the rules must hold.** E is the most forceful: danger must behave predictably, a rule must be simple, observable and consistent, the audience learns it through consequence rather than explanation, and any exception without cost collapses fear (E ch 8, ch 16). C says the same for the supernatural: "anything goes" is a trap and rules matter ten times more; set your own rules and never break them whether or not you state them, because audiences sense contradictions (C ch 2). B asks for "clear rules for the threat" and at least one limitation (B ch 10). D calls the lightest tool the steno-pad list of the biggest world rules, "the manual for operating your universe" (D ch 8).

**Teaching the rule.** E: the audience should understand the danger before the characters do, so a scare **confirms** a rule (E ch 8, ch 14). C: the audience can learn with the character (*Final Destination*), and rules for magick are inferred from ritual and desire (C ch 2). A: a weakness must be justified, but briefly, because more explanation only shows its thinness (A ch 5). F goes the other way on explanation: characters who explain the preternatural rationally are the ones who fail, and obscurity itself is the effect (F ch 9; see sec 10).

**Escalation without new powers.** E gives the clearest recipe: keep the rule fixed and turn three dials, **tighter space, shorter response time, higher cost for the same mistake**; no new rules in Act Three; consequences are permanent and nothing resets (E ch 4, ch 8, ch 14). E's four-step escalation cycle for Act Two: the threat appears, characters respond, the response fails, the cost increases (E ch 11).

**The antagonist as a system.** E defines an antagonist by operation, not appearance, and gives four types (persistent, conditional, territorial, contagious) and four design questions: how it advances, what triggers it, what it controls, what cannot stop it (E ch 7). C's location chapter supplies more kinds of place-as-threat: a **corruptor** (the Overlook), a **liminal threshold** that dooms on entry and solves "why don't they leave" (*The Grudge*), a **pocket dimension** tailored to the visitor (*1408*), a **place of infection** that leaks outward (*Event Horizon*, *Prince of Darkness*), plus the Poltergeist-style **geographic limit** (C ch 2, ch 4). B adds that the environment can "feed" on a character's weakness and evolve (B ch 7).

**The barrier idea.** A's one-line theory of horror: it is the sudden penetration of a barrier believed impermeable (human and beast, life and death, steel and flesh, the screen and the world), the villain's list of "smoke" barriers (A ch 5). A's definition of dread is the same shape: the safe, secure or sacred penetrated by the Forbidden, the Unknown, the Unnatural (A intro).

**Hunting from multiple angles.**
- **The Lewton Bus** (C ch 3): expectation from dramatic irony, then the audience's privileged knowledge of where the hunter is removed, then attention misdirected, then surprise from the neglected direction. Long's reasoning: losing the hunter's position frightens more than seeing it.
- **The malevolent hover** (F ch 3): a persecutor who seems to be everywhere at once and makes the victim feel always watched (Covey, Babo, Annie Wilkes). F's best model of omnipresence.
- **The Haunting hand** (A ch 4): a character is certain who is beside them; a cut reveals the person is elsewhere. Its fairness comes from setup by omission.
- **The Thing kennel** (C ch 2): partial, conflicting views the audience cannot assemble.
- **Hidden in plain sight** (C ch 2): the threat is present in the frame and unannounced.
- **The unseen attacker** (D ch 7) and **limited perspective** (B ch 6, ch 10): the audience sees only the victim's view.
- **Setpiece by proximity** (E ch 14): *Alien*'s vents and tracker, where distance is the clock.
- **Graymatter's stitched loop** (Graymatter notes): identical stretches of corridor stitched into one take, and teammates who fall behind then are suddenly beside the lead.

**Rationing information about the threat.** B gives three tools: gradual reveal, limited point of view, character reactions (B ch 10). D: know the whole truth, dole it out, withhold some entirely; staged discoveries (evidence, clue, hidden lab, recorded logs) and replace exposition with playback (D ch 3, ch 7). E: reveal behavior, not origin; indirect threat in Act One (E ch 7, ch 10). F: obscurity is essential, knowing the full extent of danger removes fear (F ch 9). C: avoid the "Coupon Shot," a reveal with no escalation, and stage the monster's form across several stages (C ch 2).

### 3.2 Usable techniques and rules

1. **State the tear as a five-line rule** (E ch 8): the rule; the trigger; the first consequence; the escalated consequence; what is irreversible. Ask: does repeating it make things worse every time?
2. **Give it a fixed signature**, one visual or sound cue the audience learns (E ch 3).
3. **Keep it observable**: the audience sees cause and effect; the physics stays in the bible (E ch 8; D ch 8).
4. **Escalate cost, not powers** (E ch 8): tighter space, shorter response time, higher cost.
5. **No reset, no mercy**: a loop that undoes a death or lets someone off for convenience breaks trust (E ch 4, ch 8, ch 16); any fold or loop must cost something unrecoverable (E ch 8 application).
6. **Give the system a limit and a price** (E ch 8 application, fairness; B ch 10 weakness; C ch 2 weakness types; A ch 4 balance: powerful, not invulnerable).
7. **Justify weaknesses briefly** (A ch 5), and avoid patching holes with contradictory special rules (C ch 2, *The Bye Bye Man*).
8. **Answer E's four antagonist questions for Garcelle plus the tear as one system** (E ch 7).
9. **Withhold by stages**: show the tear's rule through consequence, its mechanism not at all (B ch 10; D ch 3).
10. **Stage proximity with a clock that can lie** (E ch 14): a readout of where she is that the tear falsifies (extraction application).

### 3.3 Films that best illustrate it

*Oculus* (a perception rule that is immediate and repeatable), *A Quiet Place*, *It Follows*, *The Platform* (one rule, escalating scarcity), *The Descent*, *The Lodge* (isolation triggers the environment's response), *Candyman*, *Drag Me to Hell* (E ch 4, ch 8, ch 17); *Poltergeist*, *The Shining*, *The Bye Bye Man* (a negative example), *Final Destination* (C ch 2); *1408*, *The Grudge*, *Event Horizon*, *Prince of Darkness* (C ch 4); *Cat People* (the Lewton Bus) (C ch 3); *The Haunting* (A ch 4); *Alien* (E ch 14); *Triangle* (a loop structure) (A ch 4; B ch 5); *Ghostwatch* (hiding in plain sight) (A ch 4; C ch 2).

### 3.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED)

**Candidate rule frames (a menu; the author chooses).** The Physics file separates two mechanisms that can coexist: **mouths** (two openings joined, a wormhole-like tear) and **loops** (space whose geometry closes on itself, as in the Graymatter video) (Physics file §1; Graymatter notes). Each pairs with a book frame:

| Frame | Rule in one sentence | Book anchor | Cost or limit that keeps it fair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vice door | The tear opens toward what a person reaches for | C ch 2 (possession exposes weakness); E ch 8 | It opens only to a vice acted on, so the human's choice triggers it |
| Corruptor | The station works on each person's flaw; only she kills | C ch 2 (Shining) | The tear cannot kill; it delivers |
| Geographic limit | The tear exists only inside the wheel | C ch 2 (Poltergeist) | Leaving the station ends it, so what keeps them aboard must be emotional |
| Rim loops, hub mouths | Loops on the rim lengthen or close walkways; mouths in the hub and spokes join distant points | Physics file §1, §4; F ch 8 (service zones) | Mouths are fixed; loops cost time or air |
| Time offset | Mouths sit at different times; the structure weakens as the offset grows | Physics file §3 | A free ticking clock; the tear is slowly destroying the station |
| Her price | Using a mouth costs her something she cannot recover | E ch 8 application | Otherwise it reads as convenience |

**A worked five-line rule as an example only (PROPOSED):** *Rule:* a mouth opens where a person's attention is fixed on what they want. *Trigger:* the vice acted on. *First consequence:* the corridor they walk is longer than the wheel allows. *Escalated:* the mouth places them where she already waits. *Irreversible:* what they leave behind through it does not come back.

**Escalation using the three dials in the wheel.** Tighter space: the usable section shrinks as sections close or loop (E ch 8 application). Shorter response time: the tear answers the vice faster each time. Higher cost: the first indulgence costs a tool or a bruise, the last a life. All three work on one rule.

**The cost to Garcelle.** E's fairness principle says she should pay (E ch 6, ch 8). Options (PROPOSED): she cannot cross at will; each crossing costs her something she cannot regain (a sense, a memory, time); she is also subject to the loops and has learned the rim by hand; the tear is slowly pulling her toward the same loop as the people she kills. The Garcelle file leaves her post-launch interval open, so how long and how well she knows the tear is the author's to set.

**The impermeable barrier as a design list.** Following A's list (A ch 5), the barriers the film could dissolve: here and there; inside and outside the hull; before and after (if the tear touches time); human and machine; prisoner and jailer (extension). Order them across the film so each death or reveal breaks one.

**Hunting from multiple angles, ranked by fit (PROPOSED).** (1) The Lewton Bus (C ch 3) is the best fit: the audience knows she hunts, the tear removes their knowledge of where she is. (2) The malevolent hover (F ch 3) for the first third, when she is felt but not seen. (3) The Haunting hand (A ch 4) for one scene. (4) Graymatter's "fell behind, then suddenly beside him" for repeated subtle use (Graymatter notes). (5) A single large "never-closer corridor" shot saved for one key scene (Graymatter notes).

**Rationing (PROPOSED).** The opening already shows Garcelle, so ration the mechanism, not the monster. Withhold in this order: the tear's existence (felt as small wrongness, sec 4), its rule (learned through the first death), its cost to her (late), the accident (by playback of logs, D ch 7), the origin (never). D's "identity known, mechanics unknown" reading of the opening is the working compromise (D ch 2 application).

### 3.5 What the books do not cover

- Time loops or time-bending: only B's circular and fragmented structures (B ch 5) and E's non-reset principle (E ch 8). Nothing on how to keep a loop consequential (E).
- Rules for a threat that is both a system and a person (E ch 7 notes the gap).
- How a non-supernatural, science-justified anomaly is explained to an audience (C ch 2 on science, *Mimic*: if you invoke science, get it right; the Physics file is for the author's bible).
- Sound and instruments in a vacuum station (B ch 6, ch 9).

## 4. The setting as threat; liminal and uncanny space

**Be explicit first: no book develops liminal horror.** All six extractions say so (A sec 5; B sec 5; C sec 5; D "weakly"; E "weakly"; F sec 6). The words "liminal" and "uncanny" are scarce: B essentially never uses them; C uses "liminal" a few times (the *Grudge* house, transformation's "between," madness films) (C sec 5); F uses "uncanny" as a loose label about forty times and "liminal" four, with no theory beyond Freud's *heimlich* and *unheimlich* (F cross-index). A recommends Freud's *The Uncanny* in its reading list without using it (A front). What the books do instead is treat **place as a character or a threat** and borrow wrongness from specific films and stories. The Graymatter video partly fills the gap (Graymatter notes); the Triage's proposed additions (Freud, Fisher, Augé) would fill more, but they are not on the shelf and that proposal is from memory (⚠️M).

### 4.1 What the six books say

**Environment as character.** B: the setting can press on characters like an antagonist; three rules (connect it to the characters; give it a symbolic relationship; make it evolve), with the Overlook that "feeds" on Jack's weakness as the model; four kinds of fearful place (enclosed, open and isolated, subverted everyday, steeped in history) (B ch 7). C separates **Location Is Character** (a place expressing a mind: *The Cell*, *Caligari*) from **Location as Character** (a place with its own life: the Overlook), and adds **location as threat** and **places of infection** (C ch 4). F: places are extensions of the afflicted figure; a sick body and a sick place share one fate (F ch 7, ch 8).

**Isolation, confinement, space.** A's catalogue of trapped settings includes spaceship and "haunted house in outer space" (*Event Horizon*, *Pandorum*, *Triangle*, *Alien*); the loss of social support is itself the dread (A ch 4). C: *Alien* "drained the glamour" from space and is the central case; isolation, confinement and a thin wall against the void; the Nostromo is a grimy working machine (C ch 4). D relays *Event Horizon*'s rationale: the old haunted-house escape (just leave) disappears when the nearest habitable place is far away (D ch 1). C's isolation-and-confinement section adds the **breakdown of social norms** under containment (*The Mist*) and the point that charged places depend on emotional investment (C ch 4).

**Non-places, service zones, wrong geometry (F only).** F ch 8 is the shelf's richest source. Its points: unplanned gaps and interstices are unheimlich; service zones behind a polished front (the oily elevator shaft, the cage elevator) concentrate dread; Ballard's *Concrete Island* is a person marooned in a pocket no one planned; **wrong geometry** is an attack on the sense of spatial perspective, with examples that are **small and almost deniable**: a room a fraction too short, buildings that do not adhere, a house whose older and newer parts are half-displaced; lostness and a detour into stranger ground; "terror in sunlight," where the ordinary and the horrible sit side by side; neglect and untidiness as the visible sign someone stopped warding off decay (F ch 8). F ch 9 adds the **derelict ship** as a recurrent image of failing protection (Melville's *San Dominick*, the *Demeter*), the visitor who rationalizes his forebodings, and **clashing frames of reality**, where the visitor looks back at his own ship hoping its order will break the spell (F ch 9).

**Wrongness without a clear threat, in the screenwriting books.** E gives the small readable change: a door that was closed is open; a hallway that "stretches, too long"; a sound with no source (E ch 3). A gives the nightmare logic (the pursuer always closes; the self watching itself; familiar places that are dream constructions) (A ch 2). A also gives mundane wrongness as a ratchet (the dry town with the sound of splashing water, A ch 5). B notes the Expressionist idea of impossible angles as horror itself (B ch 1, ch 9; C ch 1; D ch 1).

**The conflicting instruction.** E says to build fear on **spatial clarity**: the reader must know where the character is and where threat could appear (E ch 13). F says obscurity is essential: knowing the extent removes the fear (F ch 9). The resolution is in the extractions' own application: establish the real geometry first so that a violation of it can be felt (E ch 13 application; F ch 8 application). This is the most useful cross-book finding for the setting (see sec 10).

**Light, sound, silence.** See sec 9; setting-specific: B ch 7 notes that weather is unavailable in a station and the book has no equivalent, so power state, rotation and air must stand in (B ch 7 application).

### 4.2 Usable techniques and rules

1. Ask what the setting's **advantage** is for the effect you want and whether another place would serve better (C ch 4).
2. Question the default mental model of a "derelict station" (white corridors, flickering lights) and break each default (C ch 4).
3. Make the place reflect the characters' fears and evolve (B ch 7; F ch 7).
4. **Introduce wrongness as small deniable distortions first** (F ch 8; E ch 3).
5. Build the **gatekeeper and delayed entry** (F ch 8): the docking as a ritual threshold.
6. Use the **good-frame beat**: a character looks back at their own ship for reassurance and the view is slightly wrong (F ch 9).
7. Put the dread in **service zones and interstices**, not the habitable ring (F ch 8).
8. Decide neglect versus immaculacy deliberately (F ch 8; see below).
9. **Objects tell psychology**: every mentioned object is read as character (C ch 4).
10. Use **absence and mundane wrongness** (A ch 5; E ch 3).

### 4.3 Films and stories that best illustrate it

*Alien*, *The Thing*, *Event Horizon*, *Pandorum*, *Triangle*, *The Cell*, *1408*, *The Grudge*, *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari*, *Cube* (a destructive environment without a conscious being), *The Shining* (the Overlook's impossible architecture), *Death Ship* (A ch 4; C ch 1, ch 4); *Session 9* (a long institutional corridor with one open door), *Lake Mungo* (time and stillness), *The Babadook* (the stretching hallway), *Oculus* (E ch 3, ch 8, ch 13); *The Haunting of Hill House*, "Benito Cereno," "The Last Feast of Harlequin," *Concrete Island*, "The Fall of the House of Usher," *The Blair Witch Project* (the failed map), *Body Snatchers* (F ch 7, ch 8, ch 9; F watch-list). For production sense: Escher and *Portal* as the video's visual references (Graymatter notes).

### 4.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED)

**The Graymatter breakdown's two techniques.** (Graymatter notes; I have not seen the images, only the narration.)
- **Subtle loops**: two identically dressed stretches of corridor, reset camera and actor, stitched into one take; teammates fall behind then stand beside him. Fit: the Lewton Bus step (C ch 3), "multiple angles," and F's deniable distortion. Use most of the film.
- **The never-closer corridor**: one head-on shot where the end never gets closer (Gaussian splats, per the video). Fit: A's heavy-feet nightmare (A ch 2) and F's lostness (F ch 8). Hold it for one key scene.

**The Physics file's options meet F.** *Loops on the rim:* the wheel's rim is already a closed loop and its floor curves up ahead, so a rim that is a little too long, or never completes, extends something physical (Physics file §4; Graymatter notes; A ch 2 on being lost). *Mouths in the hub and spokes:* service zones where F says dread concentrates (F ch 8); the weightless hub as the strangest place. *A time offset* adds a ticking clock (Physics file §3). The rim also gives half-gravity, dropped objects drifting sideways, and different feelings walking with or against the spin (Physics file §4), which are physical, deniable and require no effects.

**Boarding as the San Dominick.** Give the crew a named, ordered ship as the "good frame" and the station as the unheimlich one; a long, ritualized docking with a hail answered late or in the wrong way (F ch 8, ch 9). Then the *Rover* beat: a scavenger looks out at their ship and it is slightly wrong (F ch 9 application). Pair with the thin wall of the void (C ch 4) and D's no-escape rationale (D ch 1).

**Neglected or immaculate?** F's default says neglect is what horror reads as evil; "terror in sunlight" says order where there should be none is eerier (F ch 8). C says the Nostromo's grime is the point and notes this contradicts the Brief's "highly advanced tech" image (C ch 4). **PROPOSED:** both: decay in the zones the tear has taken, and unsettling order in the places she uses (made bed, ordered tools). The cold open's clean tech then becomes her lair, not the station's state.

**Her damaged eyes as the film's grammar.** F says loss of clear seeing is loss of the apotropaion and that eye injury is the most dreaded (F ch 9); A rates the eyes the most sensitive site (A ch 1). **PROPOSED:** the picture degrades consistently whenever we are in her sight (noise, halos, a doubled image), used for a few shots only. The station can echo her: eyeless windows and sensors that give wrong images (F ch 7 application).

**Psychic carryover and the original crew's things.** Looters taking the former crew's objects invert exorcism (F ch 8). Her own sparse set can tell her history (C ch 4).

**The home inversion.** The station is her home; the crew are the intruders (A ch 3; B ch 1). Dread in A's definition is the home penetrated, so the audience sits on the occupant's side.

### 4.5 What the books do not cover

- Liminal space theory, the eerie, non-places (the Triage's list; F's loose use).
- How to write geometry that does not fit (C sec 5).
- A rotating habitat: spin gravity, decompression, Coriolis (A sec 5; C sec 5; F sec 6).
- A hostile space with a conscious inhabitant who is the lead.
- A space wrong without a monster, apart from isolated examples (A ch 3 *Vanishing Hitchhiker*; E ch 3).

## 5. Minimal body horror

### 5.1 What the books say

**The graphic-versus-implied question is the shelf's most argued point.** B: less is more; the imagination fills an unseen threat with something worse (B ch 6). E: violence is a decision, not a default; show it only when it changes the balance of power (removes a character, confirms dominance, closes an escape); otherwise suggest, and restraint builds inevitability (E ch 15). A refuses the claim that implication is inherently better: it depends on the story, and the test is what a graphic image does for story, character, theme and emotion, and **when** (A ch 4). C lists mutilation as disgust's most common source but endorses suggestion (*Psycho*, *Texas Chain Saw*) and offers non-gory disgust (C ch 1, ch 3). D notes that implied violence still earned *Chain Saw* an X rating, and that a motionless card player disturbed more than gore (D ch 6). F is the dissenter: horror is essentially bio-horror, the body is primary and psychological readings over-psychologize (F intro), and F's one nod to restraint is Burke's obscurity (F ch 9).

**The two models for how a graphic opening licenses restraint afterward.**
1. **Front-load** (A ch 4): *Dawn of the Dead* puts its most graphic violence in its first ten minutes, then never again at that level; the audience is so unsettled that "anything might happen," with an occasional jab. Alternative in A: **modulate**, as in *The Thing*, spacing big sequences among small, non-threatening horrors (the frozen blood streams, a scalpel blood test).
2. **One shown act** (E ch 15 application): show violence when it confirms the antagonist's dominance; E warns the most extreme moment arriving too early collapses escalation, and the extraction reads the opening as the single full-strength act, with later deaths suggested.
Both agree on the law of diminishing returns (A ch 4) and on permanence: deaths must change behavior and remove options (E ch 15).

**Non-bodily kinds of disgust and fear.** C: **intellectual disgust** from ideas (a character defending torture) and **environmental disgust** (the filthy dining room of *Chain Saw*), with sound-only disgust as a retreat response; careless intellectual disgust can aim at the filmmakers (C ch 3). A: fears of procedure that removes autonomy (the hospital), of paranoia and mind control, of being lost, of the intruded-upon intimate act, of the barrier made of smoke, nightmare presence (A ch 2, ch 3, ch 5). D: a death that is non-painful for the victim and still horrifying (the pleasure-chemical creature) (D ch 7). B: tension, anxiety and shock as types separate from gore (B ch 2).

**Implication techniques named.** Cut away (D ch 4; A ch 4 Un Chien Andalou's false relief cloud before the blade); scream then silence (E ch 15, *Kill List*); the unseen attacker's sounds only (D ch 7); Blatty's long dull shot then a quick break and a cut before the blow (C ch 3); the reaction of a witness, as in the *Body Snatchers* kiss (A ch 4); "witnessing as part of the punishment" (E ch 15, *Bone Tomahawk*); the aftermath as characterization (D ch 4).

### 5.2 Usable techniques and rules

1. Show a death only if it shifts power; otherwise suggest (E ch 15).
2. Ask of any graphic image: purpose, and timing (A ch 4).
3. Use the **sensitive-site list as threat and anticipation, not damage**: a hand near an eye, a visor lowered (A ch 1 application).
4. Use sound to carry the act (E ch 15; C ch 3).
5. Cut before the blow; stay on a witness's face (A ch 4; C ch 3).
6. Use **procedure** and loss of control as the fear (A ch 2).
7. Use **environmental and intellectual** disgust (C ch 3).
8. Rewrite a violent moment with less description; if it becomes more disturbing, restraint is working (E ch 15 exercise).
9. Keep aftermath consequential for survivors (E ch 15).

### 5.3 Films that best illustrate it

*The Haunting* (1963), *The Others*, *The Innocents* (A ch 4; D ch 1); *Psycho* (suggestion) (C ch 1); *Texas Chain Saw Massacre* (implied, place-based disgust; but see sec 13: A lists it as explicit); *Dawn of the Dead* and *The Thing* (the two models) (A ch 4); *Kill List*, *Bone Tomahawk*, *Funny Games* (E ch 15); *Event Horizon* (graphic imagery shown late; D ch 6); *Cat People* (C ch 3).

### 5.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED)

**The opening.** It contains the shelf's most sensitive site, the eyes (A ch 1), and a peeled face and dug-in fingers, which are body horror by the books' definitions (B ch 1: torture and gore; C ch 3: mutilation). The author decides how much is shown; options in order of restraint: (a) *as written*, treated as the one shown act and the film's front-load (A ch 4; E ch 15); (b) *eyes in extreme close-up, his face shown only by sound and her reflection*; (c) *her face and eyes as the entire image, with sound doing the act* (the Brief already moves between her eyes and his face); (d) *the act cut before contact*, relying on the eye-imagery rhyme with her history. Each leaves later deaths free to follow E's grammar. Because D shows implication can still earn a severe rating (D ch 6), the rating question travels with this choice (Brief open item 8). **Recommendation (PROPOSED):** treat the opening as the single full-strength act, and make every later death carry its horror through implication, a witness and aftermath, so the escalation comes from what is withheld.

**Deaths without gore (menu).** (1) *Through the tear and not back*: the person steps through and does not return (E ch 15 application). (2) *Sound only*: a scream, then silence (E ch 15). (3) *The witness*: the survivors see it through another angle (E ch 15; C ch 3 reaction rule). (4) *Aftermath only*: the empty room and the changed behavior (D ch 4; E ch 15). (5) *A death that is not painful but is horrifying because of what it shows about the person* (D ch 7). (6) *Procedure*: the vice turns into loss of control with no wound (A ch 2). (7) *Lewton cut*: relief that is false (A ch 4). Vary the tier from death to death (sec 2).

**Substitutes for the body (F's question).** If dread is not bodily, what stands in for the body? Candidates from F: the station (wound and place), Garcelle's optics and name, the crew's sense of an ordered world (F sec 6 question 1). Add the intellectual disgust of the vice-rule itself (C ch 3).

**Restraint is not safety.** F's warning about Lovecraft and the political misuse of visceral triggers (F intro; F ch 9) reminds that even body-free horror carries meaning; C's reading check (C ch 2) still applies.

### 5.5 What the books do not cover

- A toolkit for dread with no blood (A sec 5; C sec 5; D "weakly"; F sec 6). The books mostly assume bodily fear and often defend gore.
- Rating strategy and runtime (C sec 5; D).
- Eye violence without eye damage: only the false-relief cut (A ch 4).

## 6. Structure and pacing across 85 to 120 minutes

### 6.1 What the books say

**Three acts.** B: Act One establishes fear under an appearance of safety and ends on an inciting incident; Act Two escalates with subplots, paranoia and a midpoint twist (the *Psycho* swap); Act Three stops running and confronts the source; the ending need not be happy (B ch 5). E: acts are "pressure maps" by function, not length; page ranges **1 to 25, 26 to 75, 76 to 100** are expectations, not rules; escalation means fewer options, not louder scares; Act One prepares the audience to be scared and delays direct threat; Act Two's cycle is threat, response, failure, rising cost, and its midpoint is the end of avoidance (characters become targets); Act Three resolves the engine, cost over victory, brief, ending on an image (E ch 9 to 12). C: Field's paradigm (about 30/60/30 with a midpoint) is over-specific and rigid for horror and independent film; use it as vocabulary only (C ch 5). D: do not steer by act structure while drafting, mark breaks afterward, know the ending before Act Two; two or three planted twists; a ticking clock; 90 to 120 pages (D ch 5, ch 6, ch 7, ch 8). A works at scene level and gives no feature structure (A sec 5); F gives none.

**Where scares, reversals and revelations go.** E: the first scare should confirm the rule; scares leave damage; violence placed to shift power (E ch 14, ch 15). B: breather scenes before spikes; micro-cliffhangers at scene ends, macro at act ends (B ch 5). C: the deepest shock belongs at the end (serial-position effect; attachment), openings set rules, hero reversals are strongest late (C ch 3). A: ratchet, release, then another scare (A ch 4). D: information-based plot points paired with physical ones (D ch 7; C ch 5 on momentum).

**Pacing models.** C: **steady ramp** (opens with a horror scene, escalates, victories brief; best for survival, menace, disaster) versus **slow burn** (interior, discoveries; best for transformation, madness); the best films combine both (C ch 5). B: alternate frenetic and slow rhythms; avoid constant panic (B ch 5, ch 9). D's velocity gauge (+1, 0, -1 per scene against the through line) is a revision tool (D ch 8).

**Alternative and loop structures.** B: fragmented (*Memento*, *The Lodge*), ensemble (*REC*), circular (*Triangle*), episodic (*Southbound*); warns of losing the viewer without a unifying element (B ch 5). C: the **Russian nesting doll** (a three-act structure nested inside acts two and three; the lead of act one vanishes for act two) and the **triangular** structure (C ch 5). C also says horror "corrupts" the Story Circle: it strips the character's power and makes the comfort zone unreturnable (C ch 5).

**The cold open against the advice to delay.** D: the opening is the most important writing; do not show the danger early or later reveals repeat (D ch 2, ch 3). E: Act One's threat should be indirect; early clarity weakens fear (E ch 10). C: openings cannot carry the deepest shock, but they set rules and stakes (C ch 3). A: the *Night of the Living Dead* opening ratchets from a vulnerable viewpoint (A ch 4). B is silent on a monster shown first (B ch 5 application). The reconciliations in the extractions: treat the cold open as a prologue that puts the audience ahead of the characters, and ration the mechanism, not the monster (E ch 10 application; D ch 2 application).

**Mapping onto Harmon and the composite.** Only C treats the Story Circle. Its mapping of the circle onto the scavengers (You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, with Return and Change denied) and onto Garcelle (a corrupted circle in which Return is solitude that is no longer comfort and Change is the open question) is the extraction's application, not Long's text (C ch 5 application). ## 7. The ending

### 7.1 What the books say

**Types.** B: open (the threat lingers; risks dissatisfaction), cathartic (the lead overcomes; risks predictability), pessimistic (the threat wins; risks cheapness if inorganic), circular (a return, which needs something new to justify it), plus the twist ending; the ending must reinforce the theme; draft a hope version and a tragedy version and compare (B ch 11). E: Act Three is not about winning but consequence; the climax resolves the engine and confirms the core fear; cost over victory; brief; end on an image, no explanation (E ch 12). C: fundamental shocks (a belief is challenged): *The Exorcist*'s ambiguity about who won, *The Mist* (the logical protagonist's logic fails), *It Comes at Night* (pointless violence, lingering on grief) (C ch 3). A: the *Others* twist, the *Hitchhiker* coat, a "still out there" coda (A ch 4, ch 5, ch 3). D: know the ending before Act Two; endings are "none wrong, but some more right" (D ch 6, ch 7).

**Emotional impact.** B: decide what the audience should feel (relief, lasting horror, both) and let the protagonist's win or defeat state the message (B ch 11). C: the biggest shocks come last because of attachment and the serial-position effect, and a character's visible reaction helps the shock land (C ch 3, with the mirroring caveat of sec 1).

**The dark epiphany and mirror moment (F ch 10).** The chapter is mainly about what horror does for the audience (ritual purification of the abject, reintegration afterward), and its one character example, Carys in Barker's *The Damnation Game*, gives a three-step shape: despair or numbness; seeing one's own condition mirrored in another; a choice to disintegrate or pull oneself together (F ch 10). F's chapter 7 gives the failed-healing end ("they haven't got a cure for anything") and F's chapter 9 argues against a moral voice or a real-world epilogue (*Stigmata*'s ending error) (F ch 7, ch 9). The author's own **Mirror Moment** (Bell) and **Self-Revelation** and **Moral Decision** (Truby) are in the Structure file.

**Sequel versus closure.** B: a sliver of a new threat leaves a sequel door; a definitive end answers the questions raised; balance satisfaction with curiosity (B ch 11). C's shared-universe warning applies to the Tepenian setting (C ch 1).

### 7.2 Usable rules

Choose the core fear first, then the ending that confirms it (E ch 4, ch 12). Draft two versions (B ch 11). Save the deepest shock for the end and build it from the audience's belief (C ch 3). Let a character react on screen (C ch 3). Keep the last act short and end on an image (E ch 12). Do not moralize (F ch 9).

### 7.3 Films that best illustrate it

*It Follows* (open), *A Quiet Place* (cathartic), *Hereditary* (pessimistic), *The Cabin in the Woods* (circular), *The Sixth Sense* (twist) (B ch 11); *The Mist*, *The Exorcist*, *It Comes at Night* (C ch 3); *Saint Maud*, *Midsommar*, *The Wailing*, *Texas Chain Saw* (E ch 12); *The Others* (A ch 4).

### 7.4 Applied to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED; listed neutrally)

**Arc and outcome options, with their book support and cost.**
1. *Revenge completed; she is more alone* (E ch 12: cost over victory; confirms isolation as the core fear).
2. *Revenge completed; the logic is shown to be wrong* (C ch 3: The Mist, a fundamental shock to the audience's belief in her judgment).
3. *The mirror turns her* (F ch 10): she sees herself in a scavenger and breaks or reconstitutes; both are supported.
4. *A survivor is spared or escapes* (B ch 4 final girl; B ch 11 open): who and why is the question.
5. *She is caught in the tear's loop* (B ch 11 circular): the next crew finds her; needs a revelation to justify the return.
6. *She leaves the station* (reversal of her launch); the Garcelle file leaves her post-launch status open.
7. *The tear's price claims her* (E ch 8 fairness).
8. *Ambiguity about who won* (C ch 3, The Exorcist): the audience must answer.

**Valence.** An ending is cathartic for her and pessimistic for them, or the reverse, and B has no category for a split valence (B ch 11 application). B's two-version drill is the method to find which reading the film wants. **Closing image:** the station as a dot against the Moon, changed (E ch 12 application); F's reintegration beat suggests a long shot of open space as "fresh air after the vault" (F ch 10 application). **Sequel versus closure:** a single film defaults to closure (B ch 11 application); keep the film self-contained for viewers who know nothing of the Tepenian universe (C ch 1).

### 7.5 What the books do not cover

Endings whose valence splits (B ch 11); a lead who is the threat at the climax (E ch 12 application); ending types beyond shock (C sec 5); theme in endings (E).

## 8. Premise, theme and metaphor

### 8.1 What the books say

**Premise.** B: four components, a clear conflict, a specific threat, a unique context, a universal emotion (B ch 3). E: a concept is a system with one dominant **core fear** (invasion of safety, loss of control, isolation, inevitability, contamination), a situation that cannot reset, escalation without new rules, and simplicity with depth (E ch 4). A: dread is the safe, secure or sacred penetrated (A intro); horror asks three questions, are those around us who they appear, are we, is the world (A concl).

**The monster as metaphor.** B: inner reflection (*The Babadook*), external agent (*Get Out*), warning from the future (*Black Mirror*); rules: define what it stands for, pace the reveal, make it memorable (B ch 3). C: monsters are metaphors by their situation; you may start from the meaning, find it while building, or never find it; audiences will supply one anyway (C ch 2); "horror is human": drop a horror element into an ordinary dramatic situation (C ch 5); the fantastic as a sponge for displaced meaning (C ch 5). D: social fear shapes each era's monster (D ch 1); the shadow self (D ch 2).

**Thematic conflict.** B: four types, fear of change, guilt or the past, beliefs or values, the rational versus the irrational; the monster should expose the conflict (B ch 3).

## 9. Sound, image and craft details

**Silence and sound.** B: three silences (before a scare, as reflection, as threat); describe the sound environment rather than writing "silence"; music as anticipation, repeated pattern, or musical silence before a hit; diegetic versus non-diegetic sound (B ch 6, ch 8, ch 9). E: sound initiates fear and silence sustains it (E ch 13). A: the *Apollo 13* radio blackout counts down against silence (A ch 4). F: interference, static and garbled signals as form (F ch 9). **PROPOSED:** a station sound-bed of hum, rotation and structural groans (B ch 9 application); a fixed sound signature for the tear (E ch 3); the crew's link to their own ship garbled at the key moment (F ch 9 application). None of the books treats sound in vacuum or a rotating habitat.

**Editing rhythm.** B: frenetic versus slow; slow with latent tension, then accelerate; the opening's eye close-ups are already that shape (B ch 9 application). C: a paragraph of action reads as one shot; Blatty's long dull paragraph then a short break (C ch 3). A: fake tension break (A ch 4). E: scenes shorten as danger rises (E ch 17).

**Light and shadow.** B: low-key, localized (flashlights), flickering, color tonalities; shadows invite the viewer to fill in; *Caligari*'s unreality (B ch 9). C: lighting as a writer-controlled lever (*The Thing* kennel) (C ch 2, ch 3). **PROPOSED:** localized emergency light in the derelict zones; her eyes as a fixed visual element (B ch 3 "make it memorable").

**Dialogue and subtext.** B: concise; fragments under fear; words versus action; silence (B ch 8). D: no one speaks in complete sentences; compress; every line must teach, head somewhere, serve purpose and plant a payoff; read aloud; break dialogue with action (D ch 4, ch 5). D calls stiff, uncontracted speech "a robot reading a manual," a phrase relevant to a robot lead: avoid or subvert it deliberately (D ch 5). Garcelle's words: write her key moments in three modes, dialogue only, action only, both, and keep the action-only version unless a line earns its place (D ch 5 exercise application).

**The rules for scenes.** E: entry has an intention; delay is where fear lives; spatial clarity; sound-silence rhythm; the outcome changes the situation; do not fully resolve (E ch 13). D: seven scene elements (time and place, emotional development, goal, dramatic action, conflict, emotional change, thematic significance), a turning point with four effects (surprise, curiosity, insight, new direction), everyone does something (D ch 2, ch 6). B: a long scene begins with an unsettling element, develops with small rising events, climaxes in the reveal (B ch 6).

**Formatting.** E, D, A advise short action lines (three or fewer), no camera directions, white space as rhythm (E ch 1, ch 2, ch 18; D ch 2; A ch 4). A's extraction treats the "no directing on the page" rule as less binding when the writer will direct (A sec 5, unsure). **PROPOSED:** a clean script plus a separate shot list for the director's cuts such as the eye close-ups and the stitched loops (Graymatter notes).

## 11. A merged framework cheat-sheet

1. **Dread** is a believed-safe space penetrated by the Forbidden, Unknown or Unnatural; the emotion is in the structure, not the surprise (A intro, ch 4).

3. **Seven struggles**: mystery, survival, escape, disaster, transformation, madness, menace; two bids can produce a third effect (C ch 5).

5. **Lewton Bus**: expectation, removed knowledge, misdirection, surprise (C ch 3).
6. **Suspense versus tension**: dramatic irony gives suspense, suppressed suspense gives tension (C ch 3).
7. **Rule framework**: simple, observable, consistent, taught by consequence; dials of space, time, cost (E ch 8).
8. **Antagonist by operation**: advance, trigger, control, what cannot stop it; persistent, conditional, territorial, contagious (E ch 7).

11. **Lifeline** with motivational incidents and incident line; **flaw as limitation** (D ch 6; E ch 6).
12. **Escalation cycle** and midpoint-as-targeting (E ch 11); **setpiece** of space, rule, antagonist behavior (E ch 14).

## 13. Source reliability notes

- **A (Stevens).** Many scientific claims are stated without checking (Grandin's animal work, the 51 percent snake figure, the visual-cliff correction, the toxoplasma example, a speculative hypnic-jerk theory) (A ch 1, ch 2). The fear catalogue is Western and mid-century (A sec 5). Dates conflict: *Get Out* 2016 in text, 2017 in the filmography; *The Descent* 2005/2006 (A watch-list). A lists *Texas Chain Saw* as explicit, while D and C treat it as implied (A ch 4 versus D ch 6; C ch 3).
