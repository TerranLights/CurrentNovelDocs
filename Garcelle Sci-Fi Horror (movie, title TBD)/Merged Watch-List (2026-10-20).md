# Garcelle sci-fi horror film: merged, ranked watch-list (staging, 2026-10-20)

Staging file. Merges the film and work lists from the six horror-craft extractions (A Stevens, B Cubero, C Long, D Watson, E Fields, F Morgan) into one deduplicated list. Nothing here is canon; nothing goes to the Timeline repo.

**Sources.** The film brief (`Author Statements (2026-10-19).md`) and the six extraction files in `horror-craft-extractions/`. I read the watch-list or works-list table in each extraction in full, then searched each extraction's chapter text for titles that the table leaves out and added the ones I found (mostly from C's chapter text: *The Stone Tape*, *Shutter*, *The Changeling*, *Splinter* and similar). Where a title appears in chapter text but not in a table, the "Cited by" cell says "chapter text". The extractions record what each book says; I did not return to the books themselves.

**What this file is for.** The film is the author's only horror film: a robot lead (Garcelle) hunts human scavengers aboard a derelict rotating station near the Moon, about the year 3000; each scavenger dies of his or her own vice; the mode is psychological, liminal and metaphysical, with a tear in space-time and minimal body horror; the opening has her gouging an intruder's eyes before cutting to the optimistic crew.

**How to read the table (Part 1).**
- One row per title. If the books give a film under two titles, spellings or a remake pair, I merged them and said so (for example *The Grudge / Ju-On*; *Le Manoir du Diable / The Devil's Castle*; *The Orphanage / El orfanato*; *Se7en / Seven*). Remakes that the books list as separate films (*Ringu* and *The Ring*) stay separate.
- **Year** is the year the books give; "n/g" means no year given. When books disagree I show both (see "Disagreements" below). I filled in no years from memory. The only memory-based content in the table is a few format or language notes in Part 4, each labeled ⚠️M.
- **Cited by** shows the number of books first, then the book letter and its chapter numbers. Chapter numbers are each book's own. "Ack" means the book's acknowledgements, "front" means front matter, "passing" means named in a list of passing mentions.
- **Relevance** (H, M, L) is my merged judgment from the books' own ratings and the film brief. A reason follows only where one is worth stating; a bare **L** or **M** means the title is a subgenre or history example with no direct bearing on this film's problems. The extractions rate relevance differently, so treat the grade as a guide.
- **Watch for** is my viewing prompt, built from what the extractions say each title does. Where it names a specific scene or element, it comes from the extraction (or is a general prompt); I did not add scene detail from memory.
- Rows are sorted alphabetically (ignoring "The" and "A") so you can look a title up.
- Several rows are groups the books themselves group (for example the 1950s monster films, the Full Moon titles, James Whale's other films). A few rows are not films: novels, short stories, an essay and television, because the extractions list them in the same works list (F lists only literary works plus a few films).

**Counts.** 413 rows. Cited by six books: 2 titles. Five books: 3. Four books: 16. Three: 24. Two: 77. One: 291. Most of the single-book rows are list items (the subgenre examples in B, the franchise and background titles in C, the history in D).

**Scoring for Part 2.** Score = (number of books citing the title) times (relevance weight: H = 3, M = 2, L = 1). *The Blair Witch Project* scores 18 (six books times 3); *Alien* scores 15 (five times 3). Citation counts reflect only what the extractions list; a book that discusses a title but did not put it in its table is not counted, so the counts are approximate.

### 2b. The two hardest problems: a monster who is the lead, and liminal or uncanny space

The books are thin on both (every extraction says so), so a title cited by one book can matter more than a score suggests. Seven titles, in the order I would watch them.

**A monster who is the lead**
1. ***Henry: Portrait of a Serial Killer*** (1986; A, C, D). C names it as the kind of film that follows the killer and aligns the audience with the menace. Watch how the film places the audience with the killer.
2. **The revenge-as-menace group: *Ms. 45*, *I Spit on Your Grave*, *Mandy*, *Revenge*** (C ch 5) **with *Red, White & Blue*** (2010; C ch 5, the source of the triangular structure). C's point is that these films earn the lead's violence by showing the wrong done to her first ("survival first"). Garcelle's wrong is offscreen, so these are the structural question: how does the audience get the grievance? (Content note in Part 4.)
3. ***Frankenstein*** (B ch 1; D ch 1, 3; F). The made being who escapes its makers; D's idea that the real monster is the creator and the crowd. The closest the books come to a robot-lead problem.
4. ***Don't Breathe*** (2016; A ch 3). A uses it for the blind man who is "helpless" and dangerous; the extraction's own extension is that the intruders came to rob an occupied house, which matches the scavengers' position. Watch how sympathy moves.
5. ***The Babadook*** (2014; A, B, E). Monster as the lead's own inner state (B), the stretching hallway (E), and sound for an unseen monster (B).

**Liminal or uncanny space**
6. ***The Haunting*** (1963; A, C, D) **and Jackson's novel *The Haunting of Hill House*** (F ch 8). The books' best account of a place that is subtly off, done with sound and angles. F rates the novel high for wrong geometry, thoughts "given" by the place, and a gatekeeper, and tags it "liminal/uncanny."
7. ***Session 9*** (2001; C, E ch 13). A derelict institution; E uses a long corridor with one open door to argue that spatial clarity is what lets a violation read. This pulls against confusion, and is the useful counterweight to *Caligari* and *1408*.

**Also in the top tier for this problem, in the table:** *1408* (C) for a space that tailors its torment; *The Cell* (C) and *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* (A, B, C, D) for non-Euclidean or distorted space as psychology; *Oculus* (B, E) for a perception rule; *The Last Feast of Harlequin* and *Concrete Island* (F, both reading) for the uncanny town and the non-place; *Event Horizon* (A, C, D) for the sci-fi version; and the author's own reference, *Graymatter* (not in any book; see the Graymatter notes), which fills the gap that all six books leave.

## 4. Content and access notes

## 5. What is missing from the books

**All titles in this part are from my own knowledge and are not claims of any book. They are labeled ⚠️M (my memory, unverified), including the years, which are my best recollection.** I chose titles I am confident exist. They fill the gaps that every extraction itself reports: the books are thin on liminal or uncanny space, on a monster who is the lead, on robots and artificial minds, on space settings, and on deaths that arise from a flaw.

The author has already named one reference that fills part of the first gap, *Graymatter* (Corridor Crew short film, 2024; see the Graymatter notes).

**Liminal or uncanny space, and eerie sci-fi**
1. ***Stalker*** (1979, Tarkovsky) ⚠️M. A guided entry into a zone whose rules shift and are never explained; dread made of space, waiting and no gore.
2. ***Solaris*** (1972, Tarkovsky) ⚠️M. A space station where a planet makes the crew's memories physical; each person is confronted by what they carry, which is close to a tear that opens onto what each person wants or fears.
3. ***Annihilation*** (2018) ⚠️M. A bounded anomalous zone where nature refracts and duplicates; an unexplained rule set explored by a small team.
4. ***Sunshine*** (2007) ⚠️M. A crew alone on a ship under strain, with a shift late in the film into something stranger; a space-station-scale confinement with psychological pressure.
5. ***Moon*** (2009) ⚠️M. One worker alone at a lunar base with a machine companion; isolation, identity and a quiet reveal rather than gore.
6. ***Coherence*** (2013) ⚠️M. A small closed group, a single anomaly that produces doubles and divergent versions of the same place; a low-budget model of "a rule that bends space and the people in it."
7. ***Picnic at Hanging Rock*** (1975) ⚠️M. Eerie, unexplained and gore-free; the landscape itself is the unease.
8. ***House of Leaves*** (2000, novel by Danielewski) ⚠️M. A house whose inside is larger than its outside: the plainest literary treatment of geometry that does not fit, which is the same idea the Graymatter breakdown works on.
9. ***The Backrooms (Found Footage)*** (2022, Kane Pixels web series) ⚠️M. Short-form liminal horror: empty, endless, wrongly lit corridors; relevant to a wheel whose rim can be walked in a loop.

**Robot or android leads**
10. ***Ex Machina*** (2014) ⚠️M. A machine at the center whose motives are concealed and whose final act serves her own interest; a lesson in holding or testing audience sympathy for an artificial lead.
11. ***Blade Runner*** (1982) ⚠️M. Artificial beings with a grievance against their makers, and a closing scene told from the machine's side.
12. ***Westworld*** (1973) ⚠️M. A relentless robot hunter, shown from the human side; an inverse model for how a machine pursuer is made frightening.

**A monster as protagonist**
13. ***Under the Skin*** (2013) ⚠️M. The film stays with a predator who is not human; the camera follows the hunter and withholds explanation. The film has some disturbing sequences (⚠️M).

**Deaths that come from a flaw**
14. ***And Then There Were None*** (1939, novel by Christie) ⚠️M. Isolated guests killed one by one, each tied to a past wrong; the structure of a series of deaths that each mean something.
15. ***Tales from the Crypt*** (1972, anthology film) ⚠️M. Short stories in which each character's own deeds earn a fitted end; a compact model of the "vice, shown, tested, used" cycle in one segment at a time.
