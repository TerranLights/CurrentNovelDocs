## Book at a glance
**Thesis.** Horror works through structure, not through ideas, monsters or atmosphere. Fear comes from how information is revealed, how danger escalates, and how consequences close in (intro). The stated goal "is not inspiration. The goal is control" (front). The ideal horror story "feels inevitable," and structure is what makes it so (intro). A concept is a system, not a spark; rules enforced without exception are what create anticipation (ch 4, 8). Revision is subtraction (Part V, ch 17).

**Structure.** Five parts, 18 chapters. I (ch 1 to 3): the screenplay as a visual document, format, visual storytelling. II (ch 4 to 8): idea to concept, logline and engine, characters, antagonist, rules. III (ch 9 to 12): three acts. IV (ch 13 to 15): scenes, scares and setpieces, violence. V (ch 16 to 18): structural mistakes, rewriting, formatting. Every chapter has the same shape: claims, boxed "real screenplay" examples with a short "Analysis," a technique list, a "design before writing" list, and a practical exercise.

**Target reader.** Beginners and writers refining structure; spec-script writers who need to be read quickly by professional readers. It is a script-on-the-page book, not a directing or prose book.

**Where it is thin (read this first).** About 600 words a chapter. Each idea gets a heading, a few bullets, a two-line example and an exercise. There is no theory of fear, no psychology, no treatment of the uncanny, no genre-specific advice, and the same handful of principles (rules, cause and effect, permanence, restraint, subtraction) are restated in every chapter. It works best as a **checklist and a vocabulary**, not as a source of depth. For this film it is most useful on rules and escalation (ch 4 to 8), the act functions (ch 9 to 12) and violence (ch 15).

## Part I. The foundations of screenwriting (chapters 1 to 3)
Part I opening (before ch 1): horror "does not explain fear," it "shows the conditions that allow fear to exist." Promise: by its end the reader knows what distinguishes a screenplay, how description creates tension, and why restraint is essential.

### Chapter 1. What a screenplay is
**Core claims.** A screenplay is not literature; it is written to be turned into images and sound, and describes only observable behavior, never internal states (ch 1). Its primary function is to be read quickly and clearly by a time-pressed professional reader; in horror, long description slows tension, too much detail dilutes fear, unclear space weakens threat (ch 1). Description controls time: short lines speed the reader, white space is a pause, minimalism sharpens focus (ch 1). The script's job is to "set conditions," not to explain themes, justify behavior, analyze emotion or over-specify camera moves (ch 1).

**Frameworks and lists.**
- Three formats compared: novel (controls the reader's imagination directly), television script (episodic rhythm, continuity), feature screenplay (a complete experience, efficiently) (ch 1).
- A horror screenplay must be: concise, visual, escalating within limited pages, reliant on implication over exposition (ch 1).
- A page should: communicate visually, escalate tension, remove safety gradually; a page that can be cut without changing the experience should be cut (ch 1).
- Five techniques: write only what can be seen or heard; short action lines for rhythm; let environment suggest emotion; trust implication; design pages for speed and clarity (ch 1).

**Rules.** Replace a named emotion with behavior and consequence (she freezes, her breath catches, rather than "she feels terrified") (ch 1). If a scene works only because of internal monologue, it does not work as a screenplay (ch 1). Write for the reader first; confusing, dense or slow pages fail before the film exists (ch 1).

**Examples cited.** *Halloween* (1978): a dark house, a light upstairs, silence; fear from absence and expectation. *The Witch* (2015): still forest, no wind or birds; silence as an active element. *It Follows* (2014): a figure walking behind her, line breaks slowing the moment (ch 1).

**Exercise.** Take a paragraph from a horror short story or novel and recast it as a screenplay moment: delete internal thoughts, keep visible behavior, at most three action lines; read aloud. **Fits this film: yes, strongly**, because the lead is a robot whose inner life must be shown through behavior and her interior is exactly what the audience should not be told.

**Application (PROPOSED).** Garcelle's hatred, patience and memory of the Garrison cannot be narrated. The only way to give the audience her center is through observable behavior: what she touches, what she refuses, where she waits, what she does with the hand she tore. The "set conditions, not explain" rule also argues for not explaining the tear in the script's action lines.

### Chapter 2. Basic screenplay format
**Core claims.** Format is functional, not decorative: it governs reading speed, clarity and pacing; in horror, "format is part of the scare" (ch 2). A properly formatted script "disappears" and the reader experiences the story (ch 2). Departures from standard format lower the reader's confidence and fear; creative formatting should be rare and purposeful (ch 2).

**Lists.**
- Scene headings: clear, concise, consistent (ch 2).
- Action should: establish space, imply danger, control rhythm (ch 2).
- Dialogue in horror is used to: misdirect, delay, reveal pressure; silence is often stronger than speech (ch 2).
- White space: short paragraphs accelerate, isolated lines pause, dense blocks slow (ch 2).
- Avoid: long action blocks, excessive parentheticals, camera directions, emotional explanation (ch 2).
- Format controls: how fast information arrives, how long anticipation lasts, when silence becomes noticeable (ch 2).

**Rules.** Keep action lines under three lines; use white space to control rhythm; let silence replace dialogue; avoid unneeded camera direction (ch 2).

**Examples.** *Texas Chain Saw Massacre* (1974): minimal slugline, mood implied. *Hereditary* (2018): repeated "too quiet" for unease. *A Quiet Place* (2018): silence becomes a rule and the format reinforces the mechanic. *The Descent* (2005): single-action lines force hesitation, claustrophobia created structurally (ch 2).

**Exercise.** Reformat one of your horror scenes: break long action blocks, remove emotional explanation, isolate silences, reread for speed and unease. **Fits the film:** useful later, as a drafting pass.

**Application (PROPOSED).** A lead who speaks little suits the book's silence-as-rule principle; a robot's silence is natural, so the script can use isolated lines for her. No deeper application.

### Chapter 3. Visual storytelling in horror
**Core claims.** Horror presents conditions, it does not explain; what the audience sees and does not see determines tension (ch 3). A single visual imbalance or unexpected sound can signal danger long before the antagonist appears (ch 3). What is absent can scare more than what is shown: empty frames and static rooms are threatening when they contradict expectation, so "absence becomes information" (ch 3). More description does not mean more tension; the reader fills gaps with a personal fear (ch 3).

**Lists.**
- Techniques: use sound to suggest presence; delay visual confirmation; let absence signal danger; keep visual rules consistent; write only what can be seen or heard (ch 3).
- What visual writing focuses on: movement, distance, framing, sound (ch 3).
- Mechanisms: image and sound as first carriers of threat; showing instead of explaining; anticipation and delay; negative space and absence; visual consistency; visual economy (ch 3).

**Rules.**
- **Visual logic must be consistent.** If darkness hides danger in one scene but not in another, tension weakens; visual rules must behave consistently to maintain trust (ch 3).
- **A small, readable change in the environment is enough:** a door that was closed is now open (ch 3).
- Delay works when the audience knows something is wrong but not when or how it will arrive (ch 3).
- Internal thought exists on the page only if externalized through action or environment (ch 3).

**Examples.** *Sinister* (2012): faint sound then silence. *The Others* (2001): opened door, empty room, continuing noise = presence without confirmation. *The Orphanage* (2007): laughter, she turns, nothing. *Blair Witch Project* (1999): stillness and silence as threat. *It Comes at Night* (2017): the door that was closed is open, "rule of safety" violated. *The Babadook* (2014): "The hallway stretches. Too long." described as subjective but visual: distance becomes unsettling (ch 3).

**Exercise.** Write a short horror moment with no dialogue, no emotional explanation, no reveal of the threat, using one sound, one delay and one environmental change. **Fits the film:** yes. Good for the liminal register and for the first sign of the tear that the scavengers notice.

**Application (PROPOSED).** This is the book's closest approach to liminal horror. The *Babadook* hallway ("too long"), the *It Comes at Night* door and the *Others* empty-room-with-sound are ready-made grammar for a space-time tear: a corridor longer than it should be, a hatch that was sealed and is open, a sound with no source. The consistency rule says the **visual signature of the tear must be fixed** (same cue each time: a sound, a lighting shift, a curvature of the floor) so that the audience can learn it before the characters do. Garcelle's black corneas and mismatched irises can be treated as one of those fixed visual rules.

## Part II. From idea to structure (chapters 4 to 8)
Part II opening: horror "requires systems — concepts, rules, engines, and forces that escalate without constant invention." Promise: evaluate whether an idea can sustain a feature, design engines that escalate naturally, treat characters as pressure points, define rules that govern cause and effect. Part II assumes visual thinking is in place; "the focus now is why the story moves."

### Chapter 4. From idea to concept
**Core claims.** An idea is a spark, a concept is a system; ideas (haunted house, curse, masked killer) are abundant, concepts that sustain escalation are rare (ch 4). The concept defines how fear sustains escalation over time. Concept comes before character, acts and scenes (ch 4).

**The four elements of a concept (framework, ch 4).**
1. **Core fear:** the anxiety the monster activates, not the monster itself. Examples listed: invasion of safety, loss of control, isolation, inevitability, contamination. A strong concept expresses one dominant fear clearly.
2. **Central situation:** where fear lives. Answers who is exposed, where escape is limited, what condition cannot be avoided. A good concept traps characters in a situation that "cannot reset."
3. **Escalation potential:** the concept must escalate **without changing its rules**. Tests: can danger intensify without new information? does inaction make things worse? does survival require sacrifice?
4. **Simplicity with depth:** explainable in one sentence, explorable for a whole film; depth comes from consequence, not complexity. Overloaded concepts collapse.

**When an idea fails as a concept (list, ch 4).** Fear depends on surprise alone; escalation needs constant explanation; rules change to serve scenes; danger resets after each scare. Such ideas may work as scenes but not as films.

**Design before writing (ch 4).** Define the core fear, the central situation, the rule that enforces escalation, and the cost of survival.

**Techniques.** One dominant fear; a non-resetting situation; escalation enforced by rules; simplicity with consequence; test longevity before writing (ch 4).

**Examples.** *It Follows*: core fear is inevitability. *The Ring* (2002): time-bound situation (seven days), absolute rule. *The Descent*: physical space enforces escalation, "no way back." *A Quiet Place*: minimal rule, constant situation, depth from daily survival (ch 4).

**Exercise.** For one of your ideas write the core fear (one sentence), the central situation, one rule that enforces escalation, one consequence that cannot be undone; if it escalates without added twists, it is ready for structure (ch 4). **Fits the film: yes, do this first.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **Candidate core fears for the film** (author decides): *isolation* (on the station, and Garcelle's exile), *inevitability* (each vice finds its owner), *loss of control* (the geometry will not hold still). The book wants one dominant fear; this is a choice to make explicitly.
- **Central situation:** a derelict wheel near the Moon, no help within reach, and (if the ship is lost or locked down) no leaving. The "cannot reset" test is a real constraint on the tear: if the rip can undo a death or loop back time, the situation resets. The book would say the tear may move people but **consequences must stay permanent**.
- **Rule that enforces escalation:** the leading candidate is "each human is led, through the tear, toward their own vice." That rule is simple and observable. Whether it needs also to be stated by Garcelle is a question (see chapter 8).
- **Cost of survival:** not yet set; the book asks for it. For the scavengers it could be tied to what they came to loot.

### Chapter 5. The logline and the story engine
**Core claims.** A logline is a **diagnostic tool**, not marketing: it shows whether a story can generate sustained pressure without constant invention. A strong logline implies an engine; a weak one hides its absence (ch 5). A horror logline suggests movement through consequence, not a static situation (ch 5).

**Frameworks.**
- **What a functional logline defines (ch 5):** who is under pressure; what threat enforces it; what rule prevents escape; what consequence escalates over time.
- **Story engine:** the mechanism that forces the plot forward. Typical engines: time limits, proximity, contamination, behavioral rules, escalating cost. The engine answers: **what makes the situation worse if nothing is done?** If nothing worsens, there is no engine (ch 5).
- **Cause and effect, in three beats:** characters act, rules respond, consequences accumulate (ch 5).
- **Engine longevity tests:** does repetition increase danger or reset it? does survival require sacrifice? does the engine tighten options over time? An engine that peaks early collapses Act Two (ch 5).
- **When loglines fail:** vague threat; escalation by surprise; undefined rules; reversible consequences (ch 5).

**Design before writing (ch 5).** Define the engine trigger, the rule enforcing escalation, what makes the situation irreversible, how pressure increases. "Structure depends on the engine. Scenes depend on structure."

**Techniques.** Build escalation into the premise; rules instead of twists; consistent consequence; inaction worsens the situation; test longevity before drafting (ch 5).

**Examples.** *The Ring* (clock as engine). *It Follows* (engine is movement; distance equals temporary safety; no decision resets the threat). *A Quiet Place* (sound equals death). *The Descent* (space enforces escalation; every movement removes safety) (ch 5).

**Exercise.** One-sentence logline with the character under pressure, the enforcing rule, the escalating consequence; then answer "what gets worse if the character does nothing?" (ch 5). **Fits: yes.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- Draft logline to test, not to adopt: *Scavengers who board a derelict wheel station near the Moon are hunted by the robot who hides there, through a tear in space-time that opens onto each person's own weakness, and every death makes the station smaller and the way out shorter.* (The last clause is only a placeholder to show what an escalating consequence looks like; the author may reject it.)
- **The film has two engines to find, not one.** The scavengers' engine ("what gets worse if they do nothing?"): needs a pressure that grows when they stall (oxygen, power, a failing ship, a closing window of orbit). Garcelle's engine: her revenge is a want, and a want is not an engine. The book's question for her is what *worsens for her* if she does nothing; open (the author's "need" is not yet set).
- The cause-and-effect triad maps neatly onto the vice structure: character acts (indulges a vice), rule responds (the tear presents it), consequence accumulates (death, lost ship, lost ally).

### Chapter 6. Characters as structural tools
**Core claims.** In horror, characters are defined by **what they trigger**, not by backstory; their primary function is structural: activate rules, test limits, provoke consequences. "Fear grows when character behavior makes things worse" (ch 6). Personality matters, but structure determines impact (ch 6).

**Frameworks.**
- **Protagonist:** the character under pressure. Objective need not be heroic but must be **specific and blocked by rules** and must demand action that risks escalation. Survival alone is rarely enough (ch 6).
- **Flaws as limitations under stress:** a flaw is structural when it restricts options, delays correct action, or increases cost. "Pressure reveals flaws. Flaws feed escalation." (ch 6)
- **Supporting characters' functions:** deny danger, enforce rules, break safety, trigger irreversible outcomes. Secondary characters often exist to **demonstrate consequence before it reaches the protagonist** (ch 6).
- **Character choices as triggers:** to investigate, ignore, confront, delay. Passive characters weaken fear; active ones accelerate it (ch 6).
- **No one escapes the rules:** if one character breaks a rule without cost, fear collapses. "Horror relies on fairness, not mercy." (ch 6)

**Design before writing acts (ch 6).** For each character define: what they want; which rule they are most likely to break; what consequence follows; how their failure escalates danger. "Characters are not carried by structure. They carry structure forward."

**Techniques.** Objectives that risk escalation; flaws as pressure points; secondary characters demonstrate consequence; every choice triggers a response; rules without exception (ch 6).

**Examples.** *The Babadook*: she opens the book again, an action that violates a rule and advances the engine. *The Witch*: hesitation removes support, isolation increases danger. *Hereditary*: a character looks away and dies off-screen, function is demonstration not development. *The Ring*: she watches the tape again; knowledge costs. *A Quiet Place*: a sound escapes, rule absolute (ch 6).

**Exercise.** Pick a character; write their objective, one limitation under pressure, one rule they are likely to violate, one irreversible consequence (ch 6). **Fits the film: yes, directly, once per scavenger.**

**Application (PROPOSED).** This chapter is the book's best fit for the author's "vice, not stupidity" rule, with one correction to keep in mind: the book's "rule they are most likely to break" invites a mistake (the character does something dumb). The film's rule is subtler: **the vice is the limitation under stress** (chapter's own wording: flaws restrict options and delay correct action). For each scavenger fill in the book's four lines like this: objective (what they came to loot and why); the vice as limitation (what it stops them from doing right); the rule the vice makes them break (the tear's rule); the irreversible consequence (their death or a loss that doomed others). Because secondary characters "demonstrate consequence," the **first scavenger death should teach the rule to the audience** and each later death should show it tighter. The book does not discuss a protagonist who is the one *causing* the consequences; see chapters 7 and 8.

### Chapter 7. The antagonistic force
**Core claims.** "The antagonist is not defined by appearance. It is defined by how it operates." (ch 7) It frightens only if its behavior forms a system that applies consistent pressure. Backstory is optional; structure is not. Fear grows when the antagonist acts, not when it is explained (ch 7).

**Taxonomy (ch 7).** Four functional types of horror antagonist:
- **Persistent:** never stops advancing.
- **Conditional:** responds to specific behavior.
- **Territorial:** controls a space.
- **Contagious:** spreads by contact or knowledge.
The category matters less than **consistency**: the audience learns the behavior through consequence (ch 7).

**Rules and techniques.**
- **Behavior over backstory:** showing what it does repeatedly and without exception beats explaining its origin; mystery sustains pressure longer than explanation (ch 7).
- **Threat, presence, control:** the antagonist need not be on screen; fear rises when it is felt before seen, controls access to safety, or limits movement and options. "Presence is often strongest when indirect." (ch 7)
- **Power imbalance:** the antagonist needs greater endurance, superior access to space, control over the rules, and resistance to normal solutions. If the protagonist can easily confront it, fear collapses (ch 7).
- **The antagonist is the enforcement mechanism of the rules:** each violation must trigger a consequence (ch 7).

**Design before writing scenes (ch 7).** Define how the antagonist advances; what triggers its response; what it controls; what cannot stop it. Exercise: design an antagonist: how it advances, what triggers it, what it controls, one rule it enforces, one consequence it delivers (ch 7).

**Techniques.** Define behavior before origin; enforce rules consistently; maintain power imbalance; use absence to suggest presence; let the antagonist control space or time (ch 7).

**Examples.** *It Follows* (persistence: speed never changes, "inevitability replaces surprise"). *The Strangers* (no motive, escalating knocks). *Blair Witch* (unseen, controls through environment). *The Descent* (surrounded in the dark, knowledge favors the threat). *Candyman* (a rule: say the name and the mirror waits) (ch 7).

**Exercise fit:** yes, strongly (see application).

**Application (PROPOSED).** This is the chapter the film should test itself against, because the antagonist and the lead are the same person plus a place.
- **The antagonist-as-system is a good fit:** the film's threat is two things operating together, **Garcelle (territorial, conditional) and the tear (territorial, and arguably persistent)**. Under the book's taxonomy she is **territorial** (she has held the station alone for years) and **conditional** (she responds to a human's specific vice). Write both as one system and answer the book's four questions for it: *how it advances* (Garcelle moves through the wheel and the tear places her, or the humans, where she needs them); *what triggers it* (a scavenger indulging a vice); *what it controls* (space, via the tear, and, if the ship is cut off, the exit); *what cannot stop it* (humans' weapons, doors, distance).
- **Power imbalance** is already built in: she is a machine with years of habitation and a tear on her side; the book says the threat must resist "normal solutions." Watch the converse, since she is also the audience's center: if the humans are too weak she is not frightening; if she is too invincible the scavengers' track has no hope. The book only lists the imbalance as necessary; it does not say how to calibrate it when the antagonist is sympathetic.
- **Behavior over backstory vs. the audience center:** the book says explaining the antagonist's origin weakens fear. For a lead, the opposite pressure applies (the audience needs her interior). The reconciliation to try (PROPOSED): reveal her **history by behavior and by marks on her body** (the black eyes, the torn hand) rather than by exposition, and keep **the tear's origin** unexplained. This keeps the book's mystery on the physical threat while giving the lead emotional access.
- **"Felt before seen"** suits the wheel: the first sign of her on a scavenger's side of the station could be the tear, not her body.
- The book never considers an antagonist the audience might be rooting for; the author must decide how the audience should feel (dread with her, or for the humans). See the Questions section.

### Chapter 8. Rules, cause and effect, and escalation
**Core claims.** "Horror works when danger behaves predictably." Rules do not limit fear, they create it: when the audience understands them, **anticipation replaces confusion** (ch 8). Fear grows when rules are enforced without exception (ch 8).

**Frameworks.**
- **What a rule must be:** simple, observable, consistent. The audience learns rules **through consequence, not explanation**; once demonstrated, a rule must hold (ch 8).
- **Teaching without explaining:** show the rule through repeated cause and effect, with each trigger escalating slightly. "The audience should understand the danger before the characters do." (ch 8) Exposition weakens horror.
- **Cause and effect as inevitability:** every action must produce a reaction; testing boundaries gets a response; ignoring danger raises cost. Random events reduce fear; inevitable consequence strengthens it. "Cause and effect turn choices into traps." (ch 8)
- **Permanent consequences:** if characters recover easily, fear resets; if mistakes permanently remove safety, pressure accumulates and options narrow (ch 8).
- **Escalation through consistency:** no new rules needed; instead **tighter spaces, shorter response times, higher cost for the same mistake** (ch 8).
- **When rules break, fear breaks:** rules change for convenience; exceptions appear without cost; consequences disappear (ch 8).

**Design before writing scenes (ch 8).** Define the primary rule; how it is triggered; the first consequence; how that consequence escalates; what cannot be reversed. "Rules should guide scene design, not the other way around."

**Techniques.** Keep rules simple and visible; demonstrate through consequence; enforce permanence; escalate cost, not complexity; never allow exceptions (ch 8).

**Examples.** *Oculus* (2013): looking into the mirror, the reflection does not match; a rule about perception, immediate and repeatable. *The Invisible Man* (2020): a chair moves with nobody there, rule shown visually. *The Lodge* (2019): leave her alone, the temperature drops (isolation triggers danger, environment responds). *Martyrs* (2008): sealed door, final consequence removes alternatives. *The Platform* (2019): the same rule (a descending platform) with less food each time = scarcity escalates under an unchanged rule (ch 8).

**Exercise.** Write a rule, the triggering behavior, the initial consequence, the escalated consequence, what makes it irreversible; ask "does repeating this rule make things worse every time?" (ch 8). **Fits: yes. This is the most important exercise in the book for the film.**

**Application (PROPOSED): rules of the space-time tear.**
1. **Write the tear as a rule with five lines, exactly as the exercise asks:** *the rule* (for example, "the rip opens toward what a person reaches for"); *the trigger* (the vice acted on); *first consequence* (the geometry shifts around them: a corridor they have to walk twice); *escalated consequence* (the tear moves the person where Garcelle is; later, takes something from them); *irreversible* (what they lose can never be returned; the tear never "undoes").
2. **Simple and observable.** The book's test is stringent: the audience must be able to **see** the rule work, so avoid explaining the physics. The theoretical-physics research (separate staging file) can be kept as the author's private bible; on screen the rule should be visible as cause and effect only.
3. **Teach the audience before the characters (ch 8).** The cold open and the opening victim are an unusually good tool here: the audience can watch Garcelle use the tear in the first minutes, and then watch the optimistic scavengers walk in blind. The first scavenger death then **confirms** the rule (the book's word for what a scare does, ch 14) instead of introducing it.
4. **Escalate cost, not complexity.** Do not add new tear powers in Act Three. Use the book's three dials on the same rule: *tighter space* (the wheel's usable section shrinks or loops); *shorter response time* (the tear answers the vice faster); *higher cost for the same mistake* (the first indulgence costs a bruise or a tool, the last costs a life).
5. **Rules apply to everyone, including Garcelle (ch 6, 8).** "Horror relies on fairness, not mercy." If she can use the tear without cost, it may read as a convenience. Try giving her a **limit or a price** (she cannot cross at will; the tear reaches the humans because of their vice, not her wishes; she is also caught in its geometry). The book does not require this, but its fairness rule suggests it.
6. **No exceptions or the whole thing fails (ch 8, 16).** Any time the tear lets someone escape or reverses a death for convenience, the audience stops trusting it. This is the most direct risk of a metaphysical premise: it can do anything, so the script must say what it cannot.
7. **Liminal fit.** The book's escalation dials (space, time, cost) happen to match a wheel station well: a loop where the rim does not end is "tighter space"; a delay or repetition of events is "shorter response time"; the book does not discuss time loops, and loops that reset would violate its non-resetting principle, so any loop must **cost the characters something they cannot recover**.

## Part III. The structure of a horror film (chapters 9 to 12)
Part III opening: structure "is the system that controls when fear is introduced, when it intensifies, and when it becomes unavoidable." Without it, escalation peaks early or collapses into repetition. "Acts are not equal. They do not do the same work." Promise: map escalation across a feature, identify turning points, control pacing through page count, design an ending that resolves consequence.

### Chapter 9. Three-act structure for horror films
**Core claims.** The three-act structure "is not a formula. It is a pressure map." Acts describe **function**, not length (ch 9).

**Frameworks.**
- **Act functions:** Act One introduces vulnerability and unease; Act Two removes safety and escalates consequence; Act Three forces confrontation and resolution (ch 9).
- **Turning points over beats.** A turning point is a moment after which the story cannot return to its previous state; acts are divided by consequence, not time (ch 9). Common horror turning points: exposure to the threat, violation of a rule, loss of safety, irreversible commitment (ch 9).
- **Page ranges (feature horror):** Act One pages 1 to 25, Act Two pages 26 to 75, Act Three pages 76 to 100. Given as pacing expectations, not rules (ch 9). (Proportions: about 25, 50, 25.)
- **Escalation across acts:** information increases, safety decreases, cost rises, time compresses. Escalation means **fewer options**, not louder scares (ch 9).
- **When structure fails:** Act One explains too much; Act Two repeats the same danger; Act Three overstays; turning points feel optional (ch 9).

**Design before writing scenes.** Know what removes safety at the end of Act One, what makes survival harder at the midpoint, what forces confrontation in Act Three (ch 9).

**Techniques.** A clear function per act; turning points enforce change; escalate consequence not noise; control pacing through page economy; end acts with irreversible shifts (ch 9).

**Examples.** *The Ring* (the tape ends, phone rings: indirect threat). *It Follows* ("She sees it walking": denial removed). *The Descent* (the tunnel collapses, no way back: the move into Act Two). *A Quiet Place* (the same rule in every act, with rising consequence) (ch 9).

**Exercise.** Outline a three-act film with one Act One turning point, one irreversible loss in Act Two, one forced confrontation in Act Three. Check: does each act remove options rather than add information? (ch 9) **Fits: yes.**

**Application (PROPOSED).** At 85 to 120 minutes (roughly 85 to 120 pages by the usual rule of a page a minute, which the book does not state), the book's proportions give a first outline frame: Act One about the first 22 to 30 minutes, Act Two about the middle 45 to 60, Act Three about the last 20 to 30. For this film Act One's closing turning point is a ship-side one (loss of the ship or exit), Act Two's irreversible losses are the deaths and the shrinking of safe space, and Act Three's confrontation is the last human against Garcelle, plus whatever her own arc forces. Note that **the film's lead is not the person the book's structure was written for** (the book assumes a victim-protagonist); Garcelle needs her own structural line (see chapters 10 to 12).

### Chapter 10. Act One
**Core claims.** "Act One does not try to scare the audience. It prepares them to be scared." (ch 10) It sets up vulnerability, limitation and imbalance and answers: **what will be lost when things go wrong?** (ch 10).

**Frameworks (the elements of Act One, ch 10).**
- **Normal world and unease:** the normal world in horror is never fully safe; Act One should feel "slightly misaligned"; the environment, relationships or routines suggest instability.
- **Limitation and setup:** limitations may be geographic, social, emotional, economic. They decide how trapped the characters will become. Without limitation horror lacks consequence.
- **Indirect threat introduction:** the threat is rarely shown directly in Act One. Show traces, rumors, after-effects, disturbances to build anticipation without commitment.
- **The inciting incident:** the moment the story cannot remain passive; need not reveal the full threat, must force engagement; in horror often involves exposure.
- **Commitment without full understanding:** Act One ends when the protagonist is committed, often unknowingly. Full understanding comes later; early clarity weakens fear.
- **How Act One fails:** it explains the threat too clearly; delays the inciting incident; lacks limitation; confuses unease with fear (ch 10).

**Design before writing (ch 10).** Define what feels unstable, what limits escape, what introduces exposure, what commits the protagonist.

**Techniques.** Establish unease visually; introduce limitation early; delay direct threat; use exposure as the inciting incident; end with irreversible commitment (ch 10).

**Examples.** *The Witch* (woods "closer than before": encroachment shown visually). *The Descent* (no map, no signal: options removed early). *It Comes at Night* (open door that was locked). *The Ring* (the phone call: "Seven days" introduces the rule). *Hereditary* (a ritual with no immediate consequence: the act ends on false safety) (ch 10).

**Exercise.** Outline Act One: one unease element, one limitation, one indirect sign of threat, one inciting incident, one commitment ending the act. If fear has not yet appeared, Act One is doing its job (ch 10). **Fits: yes, for the scavenger crew.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **The cold open conflicts with the book, in a way you can use.** The author's opening shows the threat directly and graphically (the eye scene), whereas the book wants the threat delayed and indirect in Act One. The reconciliation: treat the cold open as a **prologue that sets the audience ahead of the characters** (chapter 8's "understand the danger before the characters do") and make the scavengers' Act One obey the book's rule: the optimistic crew must feel the station's **unease** (misaligned geometry, a sign of the earlier victim, a sound), not its horror, and Garcelle should not appear on their side until the Act One turning point.
- **Limitation (geographic, economic, emotional):** the crew's optimism is the "normal world"; the book's definition suggests making that normality *slightly off*: their economic desperation, their leader's deadline, their ship's fragility. These are the vices' seeds and the limitations that will trap them.
- **Commitment without understanding:** the crew's inciting incident is boarding and the first act of looting. The audience knows what they do not; the film's own dramatic irony is the engine of Act One.
- **Act One failure to avoid:** explaining the tear early.
- The book gives no help on **setting up a lead who is already committed and already informed** (Garcelle starts alone, knows the station). Her Act One cannot be "commitment without understanding." Use her Act One for something else (a pressure on her: a prior, a limit, an unmet need). Open.

### Chapter 11. Act Two
**Core claims.** "Act Two is where horror takes control." It dismantles Act One's vulnerability in stages; the story stops asking what is happening and asks how bad it will get. Question: **what happens when survival becomes difficult instead of possible?** (ch 11) Act Two is "not about chaos. It is about tightening systems." (ch 11)

**Frameworks.**
- **Escalation cycles (four steps):** the threat appears; characters respond; the response fails; the cost increases. Each cycle removes a layer of protection (ch 11). The threat may retreat without resolving, and temporary safety deepens unease.
- **Information without safety:** clues and discoveries bring new danger; knowledge in horror is rarely empowering; understanding increases exposure (ch 11).
- **The midpoint:** "not the biggest scare" but the moment avoidance ends. Before it characters react, after it they are targeted (ch 11).
- **Loss of control:** in the second half, characters lose control of environment, allies, time, perception. Horror peaks when escape stays theoretically possible but practically unreachable (ch 11).
- **Escalation through consequence:** no resets; every mistake leaves permanent damage (ch 11).
- **When Act Two fails:** scares repeat without consequence; information resolves tension; characters regain safety; the midpoint changes nothing. A weak Act Two yields a rushed or bloated ending (ch 11).

**Design before writing (ch 11).** How safety will be removed in stages; what changes at the midpoint; what control is permanently lost; how mistakes increase cost.

**Techniques.** Escalate through repeated failure; reveal information that worsens danger; midpoint ends avoidance; remove safety permanently; compress time and space gradually (ch 11).

**Examples.** *The Night House* (2020): light on, shape gone, room still wrong. *The Empty Man* (2020): the symbol appears again elsewhere; information confirms persistence. *The Invisible Man* (2020): "she realizes no one believes her" = social safety collapses at the midpoint. *Green Room* (2015): the door locks, control shifts. *The Lodge* (2019): the storm worsens, options vanish (ch 11).

**Exercise.** Three escalation cycles, one midpoint that changes the nature of danger, one irreversible loss of control. Check: does Act Two feel restrictive rather than chaotic? (ch 11) **Fits: yes. Priority exercise.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **Escalation cycles map directly onto the vice structure.** Use the book's four steps as the unit of Act Two: *the threat appears* (the tear opens toward a scavenger's vice); *they respond* (they indulge it, or try to fight it); *the response fails* (the vice costs them; the group's fix does not work); *the cost increases* (a death, a loss of ship, power, air or an ally). Three or four such cycles (one per scavenger, in increasing severity) is a plausible Act Two spine. The author's three-beat vice structure (shown early, tested in the middle, used against them at the end) fits inside one cycle each.
- **Information without safety:** every discovery about the station should raise exposure. The scavengers learn the tear exists (a clue), and that knowledge puts them closer to Garcelle.
- **Midpoint = "from reacting to being targeted."** For the scavengers it is the moment they understand someone is hunting them and the station is not just broken. For Garcelle, the midpoint should alter *her* situation too (the book's logic: the midpoint changes the nature of danger). PROPOSED options: a human she cannot read as a vice-victim; a cost the tear charges her; discovering one of them shares her injury. The author decides.
- **Loss of control of perception** (ch 11) is the book's own phrase for the liminal register: the characters lose control over *environment, allies, time, perception*; that is the tear's job in Act Two.
- **Failure to avoid:** the scavengers never regain a safe place (a "safe room" that is later breached is fine; one that stays safe resets tension).

### Chapter 12. Act Three
**Core claims.** "Act Three does not introduce new fear. It resolves the fear already created." Question: **what happens now that escape is no longer possible?** "Act Three is not about winning. It is about consequence." (ch 12)

**Frameworks.**
- **Confrontation is inevitable, not brave:** forced because alternatives are gone, delay is fatal, the system has closed. Confrontation can be psychological rather than physical (ch 12).
- **The climax resolves the engine:** a rule is fulfilled, broken, or enforced permanently; the pressure either consumes the characters or ends them; it should feel like the only possible outcome (ch 12).
- **Cost over victory:** horror endings focus on what is lost; survival is altered; the ending confirms the core fear instead of relieving it. "Comfort weakens horror. Cost strengthens it." (ch 12)
- **Resolution can be brief:** extending the ending reduces impact; abrupt endings work when earned (ch 12).
- **Final images over explanation:** the last image visually confirms the cost, the transformation, the inevitability; explanation after the resolution weakens fear retroactively (ch 12).

**Design before writing (ch 12).** What forces confrontation; how the engine resolves; what is permanently lost; what final image ends the story.

**Techniques.** Force confrontation through collapse; resolve the engine decisively; cost over victory; keep the act short and relentless; end with an image (ch 12).

**Examples.** *Saint Maud* (2019): quiet, psychological confrontation, no hesitation. *REC* (2007): the light fails, the camera drops; the engine resolves through containment and chaos. *Midsommar* (2019): she watches the fire and smiles; threat resolves through acceptance, cost is moral. *The Wailing* (2016): delay itself is the last mistake, a sudden ending. *Texas Chain Saw Massacre*: the survivor laughs, the sun rises, trauma persists (ch 12).

**Exercise.** One unavoidable confrontation, one rule that resolves, one irreversible cost, one final image; if it confirms the core fear rather than relieving it, Act Three is complete (ch 12). **Fits: yes.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **The film's ending is the hardest structural problem, and the book's frame helps in one specific way:** it says the climax should **resolve the engine and confirm the core fear**. So choose the core fear first (chapter 4), then ask what ending confirms it for Garcelle. If the fear is isolation, the cost may be that her revenge leaves her more alone; if inevitability, her own rule catches her. This is a design prompt, not an answer.
- **Cost over victory** meets the author's lead: "winning" for Garcelle cannot be only a body count; the book would require a permanent loss for her.
- **The confrontation with the last scavenger** can be psychological (the *Saint Maud* example), which suits the film's no-body-horror brief.
- **Short Act Three:** after the tear's last rule fires, do not explain it.
- **Final image:** the film begins with the station as a dot against the Moon; an ending that returns to that image with a change (a second dot, a missing dot, the station's rotation altered) would be a structural rhyme. This is a suggestion only.
- The book assumes the protagonist is the one confronted. When she is the confronter and the audience's center, **who is forced to confront whom** must be made clear.

## Part IV. Scenes, scares, and execution (chapters 13 to 15)
Part IV opening: "Structure defines the path, but scenes define the experience." The part moves from planning to execution; it "assumes the structure already works." Promise: design scenes that escalate internally, place scares without weakening tension, build setpieces that obey rules, control violence so it leaves lasting impact. "Actions shorten. Silence matters. Space becomes dangerous."

### Chapter 13. Writing effective horror scenes
**Core claims.** "A horror scene is not defined by what happens. It is defined by what changes." Every scene applies pressure, delays resolution and ends with a shift in control; a scene that ends in the same state it began in weakens the film. Scenes are "the smallest units of escalation" (ch 13).

**Frameworks (the anatomy of a scene, ch 13).**
1. **Entry defines intention.** A character enters a space for a reason: investigate, hide, escape, confirm something. Intention determines how tension builds. Scenes without intention drift.
2. **Delay is where fear lives.** Delay is created through silence, empty space, hesitation, interruption. The longer resolution is delayed "within reason," the stronger the release.
3. **Spatial clarity.** Fear comes from "clarity inside danger," not confusion. The reader must know where the character is, what surrounds them, where threat could appear. Clear space allows anticipation.
4. **Sound and silence control rhythm.** Sound initiates fear, silence sustains it; scenes often begin with sound and escalate through its absence; line length and white space set the rhythm.
5. **Outcomes must alter the situation.** Possible outcomes: safety reduced, a rule confirmed, a character exposed, control shifts. If nothing changes, the scene is redundant.
6. **Do not fully resolve fear.** Partial resolution keeps pressure; answers should introduce new danger, not relief.

**Design before writing (ch 13).** Why the character enters; what delays resolution; what changes by the end.

**Techniques.** Clear intention; delay as the main tool; spatial clarity; rhythm by sound and silence; end on irreversible change (ch 13).

**Examples.** *The Night House*: deliberate entry, lights off, waiting. *Lake Mungo* (2008): the camera keeps recording, nothing moves; time itself becomes threatening. *Session 9* (2001): a long hallway, doors both sides, one open; a readable space with a focal point. *The Orphanage*: footsteps, pause, silence. *The Taking of Deborah Logan* (2014): "normal again," then a cut: normality revealed as temporary (ch 13).

**Exercise.** Design a scene with intention, one delaying element, one situation-changing outcome; remove all dialogue; if still tense it is sound (ch 13). **Fits: yes.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **Spatial clarity vs. liminal confusion is the key tension between this book and the film.** The book says to build fear on clarity, not confusion. A liminal film is built on spaces that stop making sense. The way to satisfy both: **establish the real geometry of the wheel clearly first** (the curvature, the loop, the spokes, the airlocks), so that when the tear breaks it, the audience can *feel* the violation. This also matches the Graymatter reference (the author's staging notes). Without a clear baseline map, the tear is just confusion.
- **Each scene needs a character's intention** (ch 13): the scavengers' reasons to enter a space (loot, a repair, a search for a missing crewmate) give the tear something to act on; Garcelle's scenes need an intention too (she is never "just waiting").
- **Delay as the main tool** suits a patient, territorial lead (the "Bear"): scenes where she lets the scavenger finish indulging the vice before acting.
- **Sound and silence:** a space station has natural quiet (no air, only hull noises and machinery); the Lake Mungo "nothing moves, the camera keeps recording" idea fits a surveillance-camera angle on a station.
- The scene test ("what changes?") is the best cheap check for every scavenger scene: each must end with something removed (air, a tool, a person, a route).

### Chapter 14. Scares and setpieces
**Core claims.** "A scare is not an effect. It is a structural event." Scares **confirm rules, shift control and escalate consequence**; a scare that only startles resets tension (ch 14). Scares work when prepared, not when random.

**Frameworks.**
- **Designing scares:** a scare emerges from pressure already active; before it, the audience should feel expectation, vulnerability, anticipation; the scare releases tension that already exists (ch 14).
- **What a jump scare really is:** not volume but "sudden confirmation" of something the audience already believes; prepared, it feels inevitable; unprepared, cheap (ch 14).
- **Jump scares vs. sustained tension:** the first releases, the second builds; alternate them; overuse exhausts, avoidance prevents release. "Balance is structural, not stylistic." (ch 14)
- **Setpieces as escalation systems:** a controlled sequence of escalating moments combining **space, rules, antagonist behavior**, each beat increasing restriction or proximity (ch 14).
- **Escalation tools inside a scene (list):** narrowing space; shortening response time; removing tools or allies; increasing exposure (ch 14). Repetition without escalation weakens impact.
- **When scares fail:** they interrupt logic; do not change the situation; reset tension; violate established rules. "A scare must leave damage behind." (ch 14)

**Design a setpiece (ch 14).** Define the space, the governing rule, the escalation path, the moment control shifts. Setpieces are planned, not improvised.

**Techniques.** Prepare scares through anticipation; jump scares as confirmation; alternate release and buildup; escalate through restriction and proximity; every scare alters the situation (ch 14).

**Examples.** *Exorcist III* (1990): an empty corridor, a nurse crosses, silence (loaded tension). *Insidious* (2010): confirmation of proximity. *The Strangers* (2008): a figure appears behind her, no sound cue; scare from realization. *Alien* (1979): vents, tracker beeping faster, each beat tightens distance. *Lights Out* (2016): a simple repeated rule increases danger (ch 14).

**Exercise.** A setpiece: location, governing rule, three escalating beats, one scare that permanently changes the situation; ask whether the scare releases tension, increases it, or both (ch 14). **Fits: yes, one per scavenger.**

**Application (PROPOSED).**
- **A vice-death setpiece template from the book:** *space* (a section of the wheel or a tear-connected room), *rule* (the vice is the trigger), *three escalating beats* (the lure shows itself; the tear brings the person closer/removes a way back; the last tool or ally is lost), *the scare that permanently changes the situation* (the death, plus a loss for the survivors).
- **The *Alien* tracker-and-vent example is the most relevant sci-fi model in the book:** distance becomes the clock; a station with a wheel and a tracker-like device (a proximity readout, a mass sensor) could do the same job while the tear makes the readout lie.
- **Her "multiple angles" ability is a setpiece tool:** the book's escalation tools (narrowing space, removing allies, increasing exposure) can be literalized: each tear mouth is a new angle, the audience sees more angles as the setpiece progresses.
- **Jump scares as "sudden confirmation":** in a film of psychological and liminal dread, the book's definition keeps jump scares rare and always confirming a suspicion the audience already has (for example, the tear delivering Garcelle into a room the camera showed empty).
- The book says nothing about scares in which **the monster is the audience's center**; confirmation may need to play as dread and also as satisfaction.

### Chapter 15. Violence, suggestion, and restraint
**Core claims.** "Violence in horror is not a default. It is a decision with consequences." Too early or too often, it replaces tension; delayed, implied or limited, it gains weight (ch 15).

**Frameworks.**
- **When to show violence:** only when it changes the balance of power: it removes a character permanently, confirms the antagonist's dominance, or closes off an escape. Otherwise it becomes noise (ch 15).
- **When to suggest:** suggestion often frightens more than display; withhold the act and the reader imagines the consequence; it works best once rules and threat are understood (ch 15).
- **Restraint builds anticipation:** restraint is controlled delay; when violence arrives it feels inevitable rather than gratuitous (ch 15).
- **Violence must have lasting consequences:** characters change behavior, safety is reduced, options disappear; quick recovery dissolves fear (ch 15).
- **Suggestion can replace gore:** white space, abrupt cuts and sensory absence do more than description (ch 15).
- **Balance across the film:** early violence signals danger, later violence confirms inevitability, final violence resolves the engine; "if the most extreme moment arrives too early, escalation collapses" (ch 15).

**Design before including violence (ch 15).** What rule is enforced, what safety is removed, what consequence cannot be undone. Violence should feel "necessary, never indulgent."

**Techniques.** Show violence only to shift power; suggest to engage imagination; restraint to stretch anticipation; permanent consequences; escalate severity gradually (ch 15).

**Examples.** *Bone Tomahawk*: brief, irreversible violence; witnessing as punishment. *Funny Games*: the wait, imminent but unseen. *The House of the Devil*: a woman waiting; anticipation replaces action. *Eden Lake*: a final fall with no recovery beat. *Kill List*: a scream, then silence; the act omitted (ch 15).

**Exercise.** One violent moment: the rule that leads to it, what is shown, what is withheld, the permanent consequence; then rewrite it with less description; if it becomes more disturbing, restraint is working (ch 15). **Fits: yes, and tests the opening.**

**Application (PROPOSED).** This chapter bears directly on the author's "no or minimal body horror" rule and also on the opening scene.
- **The book's grammar already matches the constraint:** show only when it changes power; suggest otherwise; sound can carry the act (the *Kill List* "a scream, then silence").
- **Potential conflict to raise with the author:** the opening (peeled face, fingers into the eyes, blood on her face) is the most graphic moment, and it comes first. The book's warning that the most extreme moment arriving too early collapses escalation applies; the book's "show violence when it confirms the antagonist's dominance" supports it as a one-time dominance statement. A workable reading: the opening is the **one shown act**, justified because it establishes Garcelle's dominance and the cost of the station; every later death is **suggested or implied**, mediated through the tear (the person steps through and does not return; a sound; the camera stays with Garcelle's eyes or the witnesses). Escalation then comes from *what is withheld*, not from more graphic acts. This is a proposal; the author may choose otherwise and also decide the rating.
- **The eye imagery and the book's "witnessing becomes part of the punishment" (Bone Tomahawk)** could combine: scavengers made to watch a death through the tear from another angle. PROPOSED.
- **Each vice death must change behavior and remove options (permanence):** no death should be a pure body count; each must close a route, a tool or an ally.

## Part V. Revision and professional preparation (chapters 16 to 18)
Part V opening: "Revision in horror is an act of subtraction." Most scripts weaken during revision, not from bad ideas but from eroding clarity, logic, rhythm. Fear survives when rules remain consistent, consequences stay permanent, scenes shorten as danger increases, explanations are removed not added (Part V opening).

### Chapter 16. Common structural mistakes in horror scripts
**Core claims.** Most horror scripts "do not fail because of bad ideas"; structure erodes unnoticed and softens tension until fear stops accumulating (ch 16).

**Taxonomy: the six structural mistakes (ch 16).**
1. **Weak escalation:** repetition without progression: disturbance, reaction, no lasting consequence. The most frequent mistake.
2. **Over-explanation:** explaining rules, motives or mythology too early replaces anticipation with comprehension.
3. **Structural imbalance between acts:** Act One too long, Act Two repeating, Act Three overstaying; the jobs blur.
4. **Broken rules:** a rule that applies once but not later breaks trust.
5. **Characters who do not affect the outcome:** danger advances regardless of choices; fear becomes passive.
6. **Mistaking shock for escalation:** shock is temporary, escalation cumulative; scares that change nothing reset fear.

**Diagnostic questions (ch 16).** Does each act remove safety permanently? Do rules behave consistently? Does every scare leave damage? Do character choices trigger consequence? If not, revise structure, not dialogue.

**Corrections.** Replace repetition with escalation; remove explanation before adding scenes; rebalance act functions; enforce rules without exception; cut scares that do not change the story (ch 16).

**Examples.** *The Woman in Black* (2012): noise, investigation, nothing changes. *The Nun* (2018): explaining the demon's origin. *Annabelle* (2014): another scare, same location, same outcome. *The Bye Bye Man* (2017): the rule changes. *Truth or Dare* (2018): hesitating changes nothing. *The Unborn* (2009): loud moment, silence returns (ch 16).

**Exercise.** Review a draft and find one repeated scene outcome, one over-explanation, one rule inconsistency, one scare that resets tension; fix structure only (ch 16). **Fits the film at the outline stage and again after a draft.**

**Application (PROPOSED).** The most vulnerable mistakes for this film: **#1 and #4 (a tear that can do anything and a vice-per-victim structure that repeats the same beat five times)**, **#2 (explaining the tear or Garcelle's history)**, and **#5 (characters whose vices are shown but whose choices do not matter; the book's rule is that choices must trigger consequence, so the vice must be a decision, not a trap sprung on a passive person)**. The repetition risk is real: five deaths that follow the same pattern is exactly "same location, same outcome." The fix, in the book's words, is escalation of cost (see chapter 8).

### Chapter 17. Rewriting for fear, logic, and rhythm
**Core claims.** Most horror scripts collapse in revision, not in the first draft; scenes expand, explanations creep in, rhythm slows. "Rewriting in horror is not about improvement — it is about protection." (ch 17)

**Frameworks.**
- **Three-pass order:** (1) structure and escalation, (2) logic and rule consistency, (3) rhythm and readability (ch 17). Do not fix everything at once.
- **Fear pass:** remove emotional explanation, clarifying dialogue, unnecessary reaction shots (ch 17).
- **Logic pass:** "Logic in horror is not realism. It is consistency." The same rule produces the same price for the same mistake; convenience destroys inevitability (ch 17).
- **Rhythm pass:** long action blocks slow reading; short lines accelerate; "rhythm is emotional timing" (ch 17); lines shorten as danger peaks.
- **Cutting:** a scene that does not escalate danger, confirm a rule or remove safety weakens the script, however well written (ch 17).
- **Ending:** rewriting the ending usually means removing, not adding (ch 17).
- **"Done" criteria:** rules consistent; escalation never resets; scenes shorten as danger increases; the ending feels inevitable (ch 17).

**Techniques.** Focused passes; remove explanation before adding scares; enforce rule consistency; shape rhythm with white space; cut non-escalating scenes (ch 17).

**Examples.** *The Babadook* (simplifying action), *It Comes at Night* (contrast over explanation), *Drag Me to Hell* (2009): break the rule, the curse responds, no exception. *REC*: lines shorten as danger peaks. *The Descent*: the collapse arrives with no buildup on the page. *Saint Maud*: brief decisive ending (ch 17).

**Exercise.** Rewrite one scene three times: remove explanation, enforce cause and effect, shorten action lines; compare (ch 17). **Fits: later, at draft stage.**

**Application (PROPOSED).** The "scenes shorten as danger increases" rule gives a measurable structural target: early scavenger scenes can be longer and warmer (the vices established), and later scenes shorter and sparer. The logic pass is where the physics of the tear is policed: same trigger, same price. The "remove explanation before adding" rule is a counterweight to the author's own plan to research theoretical physics; the research feeds the writer, not the script.

### Chapter 18. Formatting and preparing the script for professional reading
**Core claims.** Formatting is functional communication; a script is judged before it is read (ch 18). Deviating from standard format makes a script harder to read, not more artistic (ch 18).

**Lists.** Standard format exists to control pacing, standardize reading speed, reduce cognitive load, highlight action. Readable horror uses short action blocks, frequent white space, controlled line length. Sluglines must define location, interior/exterior, time of day. Action stays visual; avoid internal thought, emotional explanation, camera direction unless essential. Dialogue minimal and purposeful: it reveals denial, fear, misunderstanding, and explaining rules or reassuring weakens tension (ch 18). Final polish: remove redundant action, cut explanatory dialogue, check rule consistency, confirm escalation never resets, verify page count. Export checklist: standard screenplay font, correct margins, consistent formatting, clear act progression, strong opening pages, decisive ending (ch 18).

**Examples.** *Halloween*, *Get Out* (2017) (layout mirrors the sinking experience), *Texas Chain Saw Massacre*, *Blair Witch* (simple format, uncontrolled moment) (ch 18).

**Final exercise.** A formatting-only pass: shorten action, add white space, remove explanation, no story changes (ch 18). **Fits: at the end only.**

**Application.** No direct application until a script exists. One note: the book says "strong opening pages" (ch 18); the film's cold open is already designed as that.

### Conclusion, Author Notes, Next Steps (summary)
The conclusion restates the thesis: horror is "engineering inevitability"; control comes from restraint over excess, clarity over confusion, consequence over shock; "when in doubt, remove" (concl.). Eight takeaways listed: visual functioning, concepts that escalate, characters that activate structure, antagonists that enforce rules, fear organized across acts, scenes and scares executed, revision that protects tension, format that supports reading (concl.). Next steps: outline using Parts II and III, draft scenes with Part IV, revise with Part V before showing the script to anyone, and "do not rush feedback. Fix structure first" (next steps). ## (c) Glossary of the book's own terms
- **Concept:** a system that sustains escalation over a whole film, as opposed to an idea (ch 4).
- **Core fear:** the anxiety the monster activates; not the monster (ch 4).
- **Central situation:** the condition that traps characters and cannot reset (ch 4).
- **Story engine:** the mechanism that makes the situation worse if nothing is done (ch 5).
- **Logline (as diagnostic):** a one-sentence test showing who is under pressure, what rule prevents escape, what consequence escalates (ch 5).
- **Structural character:** a character defined by what they trigger, not by backstory (ch 6).
- **Antagonistic force / system:** the threat defined by how it operates (ch 7).
- **Rule:** a simple, observable, consistent statement of how the world responds to behavior (ch 8).
- **Permanent consequence:** an effect that cannot be undone and so keeps pressure accumulating (ch 8).
- **Pressure map:** the book's name for three-act structure (ch 9).
- **Turning point:** a moment after which the story cannot return to its prior state (ch 9).
- **Inciting incident (horror):** the moment of forced engagement, often exposure (ch 10).
- **Escalation cycle:** threat, response, failure, increased cost (ch 11).
- **Midpoint (horror):** the end of avoidance; characters become targets (ch 11).
- **Setpiece:** a controlled sequence of escalating moments combining space, rules, antagonist behavior (ch 14).
- **Jump scare:** sudden confirmation of something already suspected (ch 14).
- **Restraint:** controlled delay of violence (ch 15).
- **Rewrite as subtraction / protection:** the book's model of revision (Part V, ch 17).
- **Visual rules / visual consistency:** the established way darkness, light and space behave (ch 3).
- **Negative space:** absence that becomes information (ch 3).

## (d) Checklist for the Garcelle script (PROPOSED; restated from chapters 16 and 17 and the design lists)
**Concept and engine**
1. Can I state the one dominant core fear in a sentence? (ch 4)
2. Does the situation cut off retreat and fail to reset? (ch 4)
3. What gets worse for the scavengers if they do nothing? What gets worse for Garcelle if she does nothing? (ch 5)
4. Does the film escalate without introducing a new rule in Act Three? (ch 4, 8)

**Rules of the tear**
5. Can the tear's rule be stated in one sentence and seen working on screen? (ch 8)
6. Does the audience understand the rule before the scavengers do? (ch 8)
7. Is there any scene where the tear helps or spares someone for convenience? (ch 8, 16)
8. Does each use of the tear cost something that stays lost? Does it cost Garcelle too? (ch 6, 8)
9. Does each repetition tighten space, shorten time, or raise cost? (ch 8)
10. Does the tear have a fixed visual/sound signature? (ch 3)

**Characters and the vice structure**
11. Does each scavenger's vice limit their options under stress rather than make them foolish? (ch 6)
12. Is the vice a choice the character makes, so that the death is caused by their own action? (ch 6, 16)
13. Does each death show the rule more tightly than the last (not the same beat five times)? (ch 8, 16)
14. Does the first death demonstrate consequence for the audience? (ch 6)
15. Do the scavengers' choices change the outcome of the film? (ch 16)

**Antagonist**
16. Can I answer: how she advances, what triggers her, what she controls, what cannot stop her? (ch 7)
17. Is her power imbalance enough to threaten humans, and is it bounded so there is still tension? (ch 7)
18. Is the tear's origin left unexplained while her behavior is fully consistent? (ch 7, 16)

**Structure**
19. Does each act remove safety permanently, and does Act One end with an irreversible commitment? (ch 9, 10)
20. Does the midpoint end avoidance for the scavengers and change something for Garcelle? (ch 11)
21. Is there a stage where nothing is regained (no permanent safe place)? (ch 11)
22. Does the climax resolve the engine and confirm the core fear, and is the ending short, with a final image rather than an explanation? (ch 12)

**Scenes and scares**
23. Does every scene end in a different state than it began, with a clear intention at entry? (ch 13)
24. Is the real geometry of the wheel clear enough that the tear's distortions read as violations? (ch 13, my reading)
25. Does every scare confirm a rule and leave damage? (ch 14)
26. Is there a balance of jump-scare release and sustained tension? (ch 14)

**Violence**
27. Is each shown act one that changes the balance of power? (ch 15)
28. Is the opening the only fully shown act, and does later violence rely on suggestion? (ch 15; the author's no-body-horror brief)
29. Does the most extreme moment arrive too early? (ch 15)

**Revision**

## Questions this book raises for the film
1. **Which one core fear?** The book wants one. Isolation, inevitability, loss of control (the candidates above) each lead to a different ending.
2. **What is the engine for each side?** Scavengers: what pressure rises as they stall? Garcelle: what pressure rises for her (her "need," still open)?
3. **Whose rules are they?** If the tear's rules are discovered by the audience through the scavengers, who teaches the audience? The cold open is the obvious teacher; how much of the rule does it show?
4. **Does Garcelle pay a price for using the tear?** (the book's fairness principle).
5. **How does the audience feel about the kills?** The book assumes the audience fears the antagonist. With Garcelle as center, the film must choose between dread for the humans, complicity with her, or both, and the book gives no guidance.
6. **Is the opening too extreme to open on?** (ch 15's warning about extremity arriving early; the author decides).
7. **How is a "vice" made a decision rather than a trap?** (ch 6, 16)
8. **Can a looping or folding space avoid "resetting"?** The book's principle of non-resetting is hard for time-bending premises; each fold must have a lasting consequence.
9. **How much of Garcelle's history is shown?** The book prefers behavior over backstory; the lead needs some interiority.
10. **What is the cost for the survivor, if there is one?** Chapter 12's cost-over-victory.

## Points the book handles weakly or not at all
- **Liminal, uncanny or eerie horror:** not mentioned by name. The only adjacent material is ch 3's environment-based unease (the stretching hallway, the door that was closed, stillness) and ch 13's insistence on spatial clarity, which pulls the opposite way from liminal confusion. No guidance on dread without a clear threat, or on eerie atmosphere without rules.
- **A monster who is the protagonist, or a sympathetic antagonist:** not addressed. The protagonist is always "the character under pressure"; the antagonist is a force. No discussion of audience alignment with the threat, of revenge as a want, or of a lead whose cruelty must be both frightening and understood.
- **Robots, artificial minds, sci-fi settings:** not addressed at all; no sci-fi film is cited except *Alien* and arguably *The Platform*. No guidance on exposition for invented technology, or on how a machine's affect appears in a script.
- **Dialogue and exposition for a scavenger ensemble:** only that horror dialogue should misdirect, delay and reveal pressure (ch 2, 18); no treatment of introducing a group of several characters quickly (the book's secondary characters are treated as functions).
- **Ensembles and kill order:** no discussion of how to order deaths; only that violence escalates (ch 15) and secondary characters demonstrate consequence (ch 6).
- **Theme, ending types, twists:** theme is absent; endings are covered only by "cost over victory" (ch 12); twists are discouraged in passing ("rules instead of twists," ch 5).
- **Sound design, music and visual style** are touched only as page-level devices (ch 3, 13); the book says explicitly it is not about directing (intro).
- **Depth overall:** each chapter is summary-level. The book's repeated principles (rules, permanence, restraint, subtraction) are useful as a checklist, but it offers few worked examples beyond two- or three-line snippets of uncertain provenance, no full scene analysis, and nothing like the psychology of fear or monster design found in longer treatments.

## Three most useful things for this film
1. **The ch 8 rule framework** (simple, observable, consistent; taught by consequence; escalate cost not complexity; no exceptions) plus its five-line rule exercise, applied to the tear.
2. **The ch 7 "antagonist as operating system" questions** (how it advances, what triggers it, what it controls, what cannot stop it), applied to Garcelle-plus-tear, with the ch 6 "which rule will each character break" line applied to each scavenger's vice.
3. **The ch 11 escalation cycle and ch 15 show-versus-suggest rule**, which together give an Act Two spine (one cycle per scavenger) and a way to keep the film's violence to the one shown act in the opening plus suggestion afterward.
