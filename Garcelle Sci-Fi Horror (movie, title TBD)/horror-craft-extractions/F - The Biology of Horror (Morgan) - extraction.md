# F. The Biology of Horror (Morgan) - extraction for the Garcelle film

**Book:** *The Biology of Horror: Gothic Literature and Film*, Jack Morgan, Southern Illinois University Press, 2002 (about 231 pages of text plus notes and index).

## 1. The book at a glance

**Thesis.** Morgan argues that horror, which he treats as one broad tradition running from medieval death art and plague chronicles through the gothic novel to modern film, is *essentially bio-horror* (Intro, p. 3). Its subject is the organism: a mind that is built out of a body and cannot admit it, an animal that rots, and the dread of "the body's insides showing up." Where comedy celebrates the upward, fertile, recovering side of the life process, horror enacts the downward side: decay, languor, sterility, contamination, the failure of rebound. He calls this the comic-horror "double helix" (ch 1). Drawing on Langer, Bakhtin, Barber, Frye, Kristeva, Paglia and Freud, he treats houses, ships, cities, illness and monsters as all part of one "community of the life principle" (ch 7, p. 159): when a body is sick, its place is sick.

**Method and reader.** It is academic literary criticism, inductive, heavily quotation-based, and wide-ranging (Poe, Melville, Hemingway, King, Lovecraft, Barker, Ligotti, plus slasher and 1980s crime-horror films). Its target reader is a literature scholar or a serious fan who wants a theory of why the genre exists and what it does for readers; it is not a craft manual and gives almost no advice on how to write or direct anything.

**How it fits the film.** *Clashes:* the film deliberately keeps body horror minimal, its lead is a machine, and its dread is meant to be liminal and metaphysical. Morgan insists the bodily is primary and the "psychological" is secondary. On the premise alone he is an adversary. *Helps:* three things transfer well from bodies to a machine and a place. (1) Chapters 7 and 8 treat the **place as the extension of the afflicted figure**, which suits a station that has been "wounded" by a tear in space-time and a robot who has been wounded in the eyes. (2) Chapter 9 gives the best account in the book of **bewilderment, clashing frames of reality and the derelict ship** as the engine of dread, which is close to the liminal horror the film wants and needs no gore. (3) Chapter 8 includes real treatments of **thresholds, wrong geometry, non-places and the unheimlich**. The book is weakest on exactly the film's premises: artificial minds, a monster who is the protagonist, and science-fictional settings (section 6).

## 2. Detailed extraction

### 2.1 Introduction: "The Body Is His Book" (pp. 1-13)

**Core claims.**
- "Life" means two things: the story we tell about ourselves (the psychological self) and the automatic biological process that carries us (the organism). Thought tends to treat the second as an embarrassing support system and to regard itself as above it (Intro, pp. 1-2).
- Morgan sides with thinkers (Bergson, Barfield, Lakoff and Johnson, Langer) who say mind is body-shaped: our concepts and metaphors grow out of how we move and feel. He therefore claims horror "is essentially bio-horror," a negotiation between rationality and a "biological plenum" that reason cannot map (Intro, p. 3).
- The gothic is usually read as psychological. Morgan says that over-psychologizes it; its fears are somatic. Evidence: Stephen King's character Sue, whose reasoning rejects vampires but whose dread rises "from her spinal cord," the older nerve network (Intro, p. 6). Words like *revulsion*, *panic*, *gross* still carry body meaning (Intro, pp. 6-7).
- He also warns against the opposite error, reifying the body as if it were the end of the story; the body extends into the whole natural order (Intro, p. 3).
- Horror is the dark foil of comedy. C. L. Barber's phrase about "the mortality implicit in vitality" is the seed of the whole book; the comic line goes up (ripening) and the horror line goes down (decay) (Intro, pp. 7-9).
- Examples chosen to show how far "bio" is meant: Clive Barker's sewage-and-fetus passage in *The Damnation Game* (a deliberately extreme case of cloacal horror, pp. 9-10); *Frankenstein* read via Ellen Moers as birth anxiety; *The Yellow Wallpaper*; Moers' remark that gothic aims "at the body itself" rather than the soul (Intro, p. 10).
- He promises to range outside the canon, even touching the Nazi film *The Eternal Jew*, to show how the same visceral triggers (rats, contamination, pestilence) can be turned to political ends (Intro, pp. 11-12). He flags that this is the dangerous side of the genre.
- Closing note: the final chapter asks whether horror is therapeutic; he withholds that question on purpose (Intro, pp. 10-11).

**Frameworks and terms.** *Bio-horror / biomorphic imagination*; *embodied mind*; *anxiety of organism* (developed in ch 4); *comic-horror polarity*; *Dark Romantic*; the epigraph "the body is his book" from Donne.

**Works used and what for.** *Frankenstein* (birth dread, a creator who studies physiology); *'Salem's Lot* (body-level fear); Barker's *The Damnation Game* (extreme cloacal horror); *The Yellow Wallpaper* (postpartum imagery); Melville's "Benito Cereno" (the book's origin: Morgan compared Melville's gothicized text with the factual source, Delano's narrative); *The Exorcist, Rosemary's Baby, Seven* (assaults on the body of a young woman); *Nosferatu* and *The Eternal Jew* (the rat/contagion trigger); Lovecraft's *Supernatural Horror in Literature* (a "biological heritage" in the genre).

**Application to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED).**
- *Where Morgan's thesis does not help:* his thesis says that without a vulnerable body there is no deep horror. Garcelle is a robot and the human characters are intentionally spared graphic suffering. But his own point that the *threat to the body is a threat to the self* can be translated: for the humans, what is threatened is their sense of a stable, orderly world; for Garcelle, what has already happened is the violation of her optics and memory (her "body" is her sensory apparatus and her name).
- *Where it does help:* the "mind cannot accept the body" idea has a mirror image for a machine mind: she may not accept (or cannot reconcile) what has been done to her hardware. The film could let the audience feel her damaged eyes as a *loss of reliable perception* rather than as gore (see the apotropaion idea in 2.5).
- Morgan's warning that horror's visceral triggers can be used cheaply or politically is worth keeping: a lead who hates humans as a class invites the question of what the film thinks of that hatred (the author already has this as an open question).

### 2.2 Chapter 7: "Languishment: The Wounded Hero" (pp. 158-178)

**Important caution about the title.** Morgan's "wounded hero" is **not** an action hero who is hurt and fights on. It is the *languishing figure* of the Grail and fertility myth (the Fisher King, the Adonis of the spring festivals): a master or host who is sick, enfeebled or entranced, whose sickness is shared by his land, house or city, and who is visited by a would-be healer. If the healer succeeds, the story is comic; if the healer fails, the story is horror. So the chapter gives Garcelle a pattern, but it is the pattern of the *host she will be visited in*, not of a hero's journey.

**Core claims.**
1. **Horror inherits the Waste Land version of the quest.** Frye says every genre derives from the quest myth; the comic quest ends with fertility defeating the waste land, horror's inverted version ends with the waste land winning (ch 7, pp. 158-161). Chrétien's Perceval finds a town that is decayed, silent, with no baking, no milling, no art (ch 7, p. 159). Morgan reads this as the first "Bad Place": the dissolution of the person and of the human-made environment as one event.
2. **The "community of the life principle."** (Jessie Weston's phrase, ch 7, p. 159.) A sick body and a sick place are the same fact. In *The Exorcist* the evil is not only in the girl's body but in the community's decay. In the Waste Land pattern, the wound is "uncanny," in the sense of an uncomfortable continuity, between the wounded protagonist, the wasted place and the torn society (ch 7, p. 159; this is where Morgan's use of "uncanny" is clearest).
3. **The pattern in the gothic tale.** A visitor on a journey arrives at the domain of an afflicted host and tries to discover and cure the malady. The visitor's crossing of the threshold is deliberately marked, even ritualized (ch 7, pp. 159-161, 169). Examples: Poe's "Fall of the House of Usher" (the narrator and Roderick); James's *Turn of the Screw* (the governess as healer-exorcist, pp. 172-175); Melville's "Bartleby" (the lawyer tries repeatedly, even sends him the best dinner, pp. 160-163); Melville's "Benito Cereno" (Delano brings food to the sick Spanish captain, p. 163); the 1956 *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* (the doctor finds the town apathetic and cannot cure it, p. 161).
4. **Failed healing is the horror.** Hemingway's "A Pursuit Race" ends with the drug-addicted Campbell refusing his manager's offer of a cure: "They haven't got a cure for anything" (ch 7, p. 170, a quotation worth keeping; Morgan echoes it as the book's slogan for the "true dark tale" at the end of ch 9, p. 223).
5. **Languishment as a trait of people.** Torpor, entrancement and lassitude (the wizard's castle in Thomson's "Castle of Indolence," opium, Usher's family, Bartleby) are horror because they are the organism giving up (ch 7, pp. 159-161). A related type is the person who has been emptied or "translated into an It": pod people, the zombies of *Night of the Living Dead*, *The Howling*'s cult, Ligotti's townspeople of Mirocaw, Freeman's vampiric Luella Miller (pp. 175-178). Their horror is **vagueness and untidiness**, not monstrosity. Stephen King (via Morgan, here p. 175 and again in ch 8, p. 186) says the pod people show no outstanding grotesqueness, only that they are "a little vague, a little messy."
6. **The healer's character matters.** The lawyer in "Bartleby" is a comic, adaptable, good-natured figure (the comic spirit surviving inside horror) and that contrast is part of what hurts (ch 7, pp. 162-163). The governess is a "girl-knight" who describes herself in chivalric terms and expressly sees her task as "fighting with a demon for a human soul" (ch 7, p. 174).
7. **Variants.** The languishing figure can be a woman (Faye Dunaway's Mrs. Delaney in *The First Deadly Sin*, whose kidney is "rotting" while the city rots; the film crosscuts her surgery with the killer's murders so that the two seem one event, pp. 163-165). In *Misery* (1990) the writer Paul Sheldon is the captive wounded figure but the link between his state and the outside world is weak; Annie Wilkes is the "hag" and a perverse healer, who thinks she is ministering to him while rebreaking his legs (pp. 171-172). Carlos Baker's phrase for Hemingway's dark stories, "the vast circumambient realm of nothingness and night," is quoted for the same wider outlook (p. 171).

**Frameworks and terms.** *Fisher King / Waste Land pattern*; *community of the life principle*; *visitor and healer*; *failed healing / restoration agon*; *entering the host's domain across a marked threshold*; *languishment, lethargy, indolence*; *pod-people vagueness*; *double-voiced discourse* (Bakhtin; Hemingway's narration slides into Campbell's confused mind, pp. 167-168); the opposition *Sliding Billy / Billy who "catches"* (adaptive, comic life versus stalled life, pp. 170-171).

**Works analyzed.** See section 5. Main ones: Chrétien's Grail story; "Usher"; *Turn of the Screw*; "Bartleby"; "Benito Cereno"; *Body Snatchers* (1956); *The First Deadly Sin*; *Misery*; Hemingway's "A Pursuit Race"; Ligotti's "Harlequin"; Freeman's "Luella Miller".

**Application to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED).**
- **Garcelle as the afflicted host, the scavengers as the visitors.** This is the closest reading. They cross a threshold (the docking, the airlock, the first corridor) optimistic, like Delano boarding the *San Dominick* or the narrator approaching Usher; they carry a purpose (loot), a rationalizing frame ("it is just a derelict"), and, in Morgan's pattern, a misplaced healer's confidence that things can be put right. The film has the unusual feature that the audience has already seen what the host does to a visitor (the cold open), so the visit plays as dramatic irony rather than mystery. Morgan's chapter shows the pattern's beats: the marked threshold, the host's strangeness being rationalized, the offer of help or trade being refused, the cure failing.
- **Wound-and-place continuity.** Morgan's rule that a wounded figure and her place wound each other suggests the station should hold a *visible echo of Garcelle's injury*: not blood but eyes and sight (windows that are blacked or "eyeless," lights that fail in pairs, cameras and sensors that give wrong images, a corridor that looks back). Morgan himself supplies eye-imagery to borrow: Usher's "vacant, eye-like windows" (ch 7, pp. 159-160) and Dickens's houses "with their eyes stoned out" (ch 8, p. 188). The eyes theme is further supported by Freud's remark in ch 9, p. 202 that injury to the eye is the most dreaded of bodily injuries.
- **Her name.** The "uncanny continuity from wounded protagonist to wasted place" (p. 159) can carry the lost name: a station whose own name has been erased or half-erased (a plate scratched, a designation that no longer matches the registry) while she carries a name that is only a detention-cell code. This is a PROPOSED design, not Morgan's example.
- **Failed healing, turned.** Each scavenger could arrive with an offer of "help" (to repair, to rescue, to save the lone figure they think they have found: the optimistic crew might first take her for a stranded survivor). A scene of a scavenger bringing her something (food, a repair, a kind word), as Delano and the lawyer do, and being refused with a quiet line, would use this chapter's pattern directly. Her refusal in the spirit of Campbell's "no cure for anything" could be her stance toward all human offers.
- **Vice as languishment.** In Morgan, indolence, intoxication and entrancement are the languishing figure's marks (Campbell and heroin; Carys; Stanton in the asylum, ch 3). That gives a literary precedent for the author's vice-driven deaths: a scavenger with a drug, drink, or comfort habit can be shown to *languish* before dying, with the dead-eyed vagueness of Ligotti's townspeople as the visible state. The book's lesson is that vice in horror is rarely "bad choices"; it is the organism's loss of will to resist. (PROPOSED reading; Morgan does not discuss vice-driven death as a plot engine. For that see his ch 6 reading of the slasher films in 3.6.)
- **A caution.** Morgan's wounded figure is passive. Garcelle is wounded and active, an avenger, and the book has almost nothing on a wounded figure who *hunts* (nearest cases are in ch 6: Poe's Montressor, Hop Frog and Mrs. Voorhees, see 3.6). *No direct application:* the chapter's central mood of lethargy. Garcelle's patience (Enneagram 8w9, per her file) is endurance and waiting, not languor.

### 2.3 Chapter 8: "Sinister Loci: The Properties of Terror" (pp. 179-199)

This is the most directly useful chapter for the station as a place. Morgan's "properties of terror" are the sites where we expect horrible things to happen, and he argues that they are never neutral backdrops: they are projections and extensions of the body-psyche.

**Core claims, in order of the chapter.**
1. **Fear is spatialized, and entering a place is a ritual.** The gothic is full of real-estate deals: a house that is too cheap and has stood long untenanted (Gilman's "Yellow Wallpaper," Wharton's "Afterward," Freeman's "The Vacant Lot," Jackson's *Hill House*, King's Marsten house), a realtor or caretaker who holds the key and is "the unwitting enabler," and a deliberately delayed entry (five pages of Eleanor held at the gate in *Hill House*; three pages of pause before the Usher causeway; "Knock," the agent in *Nosferatu*). Taking possession becomes being taken possession of (ch 8, pp. 179-182). Janus, the Roman gate-god, is the model of the threshold that governs transition. The remote, privileged, ungoverned estate (Dracula's castle, Gilles de Rais's castle) is the model of the place beyond law (pp. 180-181).
2. **Public/private reversals and the problem of another's lived-in space.** The private space can be the uncanny place entered from sane public life (Usher), or the safe home invaded from outside (*In Cold Blood*, *Beowulf*'s hall Heorot). Either way "a verge is crossed," and the body's skin is the same line (pp. 182-184). Moving into a house that "retains the personality of its former owners" leaves it possessed; scrubbing, painting and refurnishing are a minor exorcism (p. 182). Dracula, tellingly, wants old houses and says a new one would kill him (pp. 182-183).
3. **We anthropomorphize our space; Freud's *heimlich/unheimlich*.** A house "speaks" in human scale; Morgan cites Kauffman's idea that artifacts evolve like organisms and Langer's that a place articulated by human life "must seem organic" (p. 184). *Heimlich* means belonging to the house; *unheimlich*, translated "uncanny," is the unhomely, the anomalous (p. 183). Twain's drifting house with a dead man in it is the model of the domestic site subverted (p. 183).
4. **Neglect and untidiness are what horror reads as evil.** Marginal, degenerated, "soulless" places are horror's favorites (p. 184-185). Architecture of failure (potholes, unmown lawns, cobwebs, newspapers on a porch, the Marsten house that the town thought was being repaired but is unchanged) is the visible sign that someone has stopped *warding off abjection*; "housekeeping" is a primal imperative, and a lack of aesthetic care is read as a lack of will to live (pp. 185-188). The word "bad" conflates the moral *evil* and the organic *rotten* (pp. 188-189). Ligotti's Mirocaw has "another coldness within its cold": a second set of back-alley streets behind the visible town (p. 185).
5. **Houses as psychological diagrams; inside and outside blur.** Bachelard: we "read" a house and a room (p. 189). In the gothic, the outside world, which we normally use to check our impressions, fails to reassure and instead confirms and exceeds a character's fear (pp. 189-190). Eleanor in *Hill House* thinks "Hill House is vile" and the words come "freely into her mind," as if thought to her by the place (p. 190). Hardy's firelit heath; the Overlook Hotel's sympathy with Jack; Clarice Starling's plane cabin merging with the crime-scene photos (pp. 190-191). In the gothic framework, Morgan says, human structures are potentials that change strangely depending on who is looking, not fixed givens (p. 192). Lévy's rule, which he quotes: the truly fantastic needs the impossible to break into an *objectively familiar* place (p. 192).
6. **"Terror in sunlight."** A place need not look gothic. *Rosemary's Baby* is chilling because the horror sits beside the utterly ordinary (pp. 192-193); the house that is an organism (*The House Next Door*) is the unnatural side of architecture-as-organism (p. 192). The house-body identification means violating one violates the other (Le Fanu's *Uncle Silas* and *Carmilla*, p. 193).
7. **Wrong geometry.** Morgan cites King on Lovecraft's horror of "wrong geometry": interfering with someone's sense of the shape of their physical surroundings interferes with what may be the pivot of the whole human mind (p. 194; King's point, relayed by Morgan, and the chapter's most direct statement on non-Euclidean-style horror). Examples: the Yellow Wallpaper's incoherent pattern; Eleanor's room in *Hill House* whose walls are always "a fraction less than the barest tolerable length"; the Bly house that is old and newer parts "half-displaced and half-utilized" ; Ligotti's buildings that "did not adhere" to one another, seeming either suspended or too tall for their width (p. 194).
8. **Places that are not places: interstices, non-places, liminal service zones.** Unplanned gaps between planned structures are "unheimlich" junctures (p. 194-195). J. G. Ballard's *Concrete Island*: a driver crashes into a pocket of intersecting highways within sight of London and is marooned in "a non-place that has fallen between the cracks" of ordinary life (p. 195). Alleys, laundry rooms, stairs made marginal by elevators, the back sides of buildings (Hanagan: look at the back lanes "and you will be looking into the European soul," p. 196). *Fatal Attraction*'s grimy cage elevator to the heroine's loft is "a liminal, expressionist scene" in which the polished front gives way to a tomb-like service zone (pp. 195-196); the open elevator shaft in *Rosemary's Baby* does the same (p. 195). Cellars (Bachelard: the "dark entity of the house," p. 196), Roethke's root cellar, and the **ditch** as "a liminal point in landscape, a kind of anti-space" off the road (p. 197).
9. **Film has found new sinister places.** Loading docks at night, parking garages, industrial parks, after-hours offices (noir), Quonset huts, boiler rooms (*Nightmare on Elm Street*, *Coma*); film is good at "interstices where no one is meant to live or linger" (pp. 198-199). And **lostness**: a detour or turn off a highway that leads into progressively stranger ground, "the seven miles" in Ligotti's story as the line between ordinary and demonic place (p. 199).

**Frameworks and terms.** *Sinister loci / genius loci*; *spatialization of fear*; *gatekeeper and threshold ritual*; *psychic carryover* (a place keeps its previous occupants); *heimlich/unheimlich*; *anthropomorphized space*; *house = body*; *place articulated by human life* (Langer); *reading a house* (Bachelard); *objective/subjective slippage*; *terror in sunlight*; *wrong geometry*; *non-place*; *interstice*; *liminal point*; *front/back (facade versus service zone)*; *lostness / bewilderment* (picked up in ch 9).

**Works analyzed.** About thirty, listed with chapter and relevance in section 5. Main ones: "The Yellow Wallpaper"; *The Haunting of Hill House*; *'Salem's Lot*; Poe's "Usher"; Lovecraft's *Shadow over Innsmouth*; Ligotti's "Last Feast of Harlequin"; Ballard's *Concrete Island*; *Nosferatu*; *Rosemary's Baby*; *Fatal Attraction*; *Invasion of the Body Snatchers*; *Nightmare on Elm Street*.

**Application to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED).**
- **The station as a "bad place" with the same pieces Morgan lists.** *The offer too good to be true:* the crew's optimistic talk of a free derelict is exactly the cheap house long untenanted. *A gatekeeper and a delayed entry:* a long, ritualized docking that the audience feels (a beacon that answers in the wrong language, a hail that is acknowledged late, an airlock cycle that repeats). *The ungoverned estate:* a wheel near the Moon is beyond any law or rescue (Morgan's Gilles de Rais, Dracula's castle). Space itself is the "cut off by marshes and creeks" of Innsmouth.
- **A wheel is the heimlich-unheimlich machine.** A rotating habitat is a *house* that is meant to feel like ground; its floor curves up in both directions and one can walk the rim and return to the start (Author Statements, design implications). Morgan's definition of the unheimlich as a violation of what is "at home" makes the wheel's built-in feature (artificial gravity pretending to be a floor) a ready source of unease. Lost-and-looping rim corridors map onto his "lostness" motif (p. 199).
- **The tear as wrong geometry, in Morgan's graded way.** Morgan's best examples (Eleanor's room; the Bly house; Mirocaw's buildings) are *small, almost deniable* distortions, a wall a fraction too short, buildings that seem not to adhere. This is a different register from a spectacular portal. **PROPOSED:** let the audience first sense the station is "off" through dimension (a corridor that reads longer on the plan than the wheel's circumference allows, doors a hand's width too wide, a cabin that is a fraction too short in the direction of rotation) before anyone sees the rip. This matches the Graymatter geometry-that-does-not-fit idea in the author's reference notes, and it uses Morgan's point (via King) that tampering with a person's sense of spatial perspective strikes at the pivot of the mind.
- **Spokes, hubs and cage elevators as the "service zone."** A Von Braun wheel has spoke elevators and a weightless hub. Morgan's *Fatal Attraction* and *Rosemary's Baby* examples (the bare, oily shaft behind the charming facade) argue that the **utility routes behind the habitable ring** are where liminal dread concentrates. **PROPOSED:** the tear's mouths open in service zones, crawlways, the hub and spoke shafts rather than in the living rim, and the crew's discovery that the "polished" area is only a front fits Hanagan's front/back idea.
- **Interstices and non-places.** *Concrete Island* is a person stuck in a pocket no one planned. The rip is by nature an unplanned junction; the survivors in it are in a non-place. A scavenger who finds himself in a bay that appears on no schematic and cannot be reached the same way twice is the *Concrete Island* situation.
- **Neglect or order?** Morgan reads neglect (cobwebs, a house gone to seed) as the visible sign of evil, and so the default derelict is dusty and dim. His "terror in sunlight" passage gives the opposite option: **PROPOSED**, a derelict station that is *too well kept in the places Garcelle uses* (clean decks, ordered tools, a made bed in a cabin) would be eerie in his terms, because it signals a will operating where there should be none. The cold open's "walls and stretches of highly advanced tech" could turn out to be in perfect repair, and the disrepair elsewhere to be the zone the tear has taken.
- **Psychic carryover.** Occupying another's lived-in space leaves it "possessed" (p. 182); looters who take the former crew's objects are doing the reverse of exorcism. **PROPOSED:** the personal effects of the original crew (and of the earlier victim) are the film's version of the unexorcised house; a scavenger's vice can attach to a particular object he cannot leave.
- **Inside/outside slippage as a cinematic device.** In *Hill House* the line between Eleanor's thought and the house's is blurred (p. 190). **PROPOSED:** the crew's instruments are their "outside check" and Morgan's point is that in gothic space the check *fails to reassure*; sensors that confirm the oddness and overshoot it would be consistent with the book. For Garcelle, whose senses are damaged, the inversion is neat: she may trust the station's readings more than her own eyes.
- **Time.** Morgan notes horror has its derelict hours as well as places (the dark night at 3 a.m., p. 185). **PROPOSED:** a wheel with a ship's clock and a "night shift" lets the film assign its worst events to the hour the station's own schedule calls the dead of night, and the tear could be the thing that makes a clock disagree with itself.
- *No direct application:* the real-estate, caretaker and cellar-as-womb parts are mostly gothic furniture; use only the gatekeeper and cheap-price beats.

### 2.4 Chapter 10: "The Soul at Zero: Dark Epiphanies" (pp. 224-231)

**Caution about the title.** The "dark epiphany" here is mainly **what horror does for the audience and for a culture**, with one extended character example. It is a theory of horror's therapeutic effect, not a template for a protagonist's climax. This chapter is short (eight pages). Morgan takes the phrase "the soul at zero" from Clive Barker, who paraphrases Emily Dickinson's "zero at the bone" (ch 1, p. 36).

**Core claims.**
1. Horror is art, not imitation: a symbolic form that gives shape to the pattern of life (a "discovery of reality," via Cassirer, p. 224). It enacts something not found elsewhere (pp. 224-225).
2. **Kristeva (with Mary Douglas): horror ritually conjures and purifies the abject.** Horror literature is "rooted in the abject it utters and by the same token purifies" (p. 225, a short exact quotation of Kristeva). The consolation comes from the bodily reaction to horror itself, **not** from the genre teaching us to master fear (p. 225). Morgan adds Morris West's idea that literature's two themes are the presence of God or the terror of his absence, and suggests the sacred may emerge from the dark power of absence (p. 225).
3. **Stephen King's version.** Terror comes from "things in the unmaking," and the ritual venting of that feeling seems to restore a "more stable and constructive state" (p. 226). Images of what lies beneath the finish: the bare wall showing where wallpaper has been torn (the "Yellow Wallpaper," Bartleby's wall, the wall against which ministers are shot in Hemingway) and Snowden's insides in *Catch-22*. Morgan calls what shows through the wallpaper **"the uncanny"** (**UNSURE** whether he means Freud's technical sense or the general label he uses elsewhere): "the negation of all enhancement, art, culture and civilization" (p. 226). A corpse with no God and no science is "the utmost of abjection" (Kristeva, p. 226).
4. **Abjection triggers art, civilization and the self.** An individual or society defines itself by rejecting what is "rank and vile" and drawing a line against it (p. 226). Deterioration can be therapeutic: addicts and drinkers sometimes reform just because their own decline becomes undeniable (p. 226).
5. **The one character-level dark epiphany: Carys in Barker's *The Damnation Game*.** Held in Mamoulian's lair on heroin and drowsiness, she is made "world-weary" (every possibility appears already dissolving: "Why stand up at all"). She will die unless an epiphany occurs. The catalyst is seeing the man who comes to rescue her, Marty, fall into the same demoralization and lie down, which horrifies her into reviving (pp. 226-227). Morgan states the principle: this is "the point where one disintegrates or pulls oneself together" (p. 227; paraphrase of his sentence).
6. **Homeopathic model.** A small dose of the morbid stimulates the body's healthy energies. Horror is "not resistance to but an unveiling of the abject" (Kristeva, p. 227). The virtual claustrophobia of a gothic tale revives our awareness of space and freedom; Radcliffe's *The Italian* ends in joyous images of liberty (pp. 227-228). Gallows humor is a "flash of self-assertion" (Langer); Dickinson's "I lived on Dread"; we crave emergency and cannot bear monotony (p. 227).
7. **Reintegration.** Disorientation then reorientation clarifies the boundary between the life-giving and the morbid (p. 228). Harker in Dracula's castle: no one knows how sweet morning is until they have suffered the night; Seward's relief at the fresh air after leaving Lucy's vault; King on the "magic moment of reintegration and safety" at the end of 1950s horror films (p. 228).
8. **Sacrilege and the sacred.** Assaults on a young woman's body (*The Exorcist*, *Rosemary's Baby*, *The Stand*, *Stigmata*) produce an aversion to the sacrilege, which can awaken awareness of the sacramental. Art's virtual space is a "sacred precinct" where contemplation, not action, is called for (p. 229, via Paglia).
9. **Real horrors provoke civilization.** The 1665 London plague orders on street cleaning, the Black Death's effect on medicine; civilization is a reactive creativity responding to defilement, "the place where all ladders start" (Yeats, via Anne Rice, p. 230). Morgan speculates that the infant lying in its own waste is the primal sinister locus and that being picked up and cleaned is the origin of order (p. 230). He closes by saying the genre will keep drawing us back to "Desolation Row" (p. 231).
10. **A limit he sets elsewhere (ch 9, p. 222).** He argues against critics who say horror gives readers "coping skills" or moral education; he says any therapeutic effect lives in the physical reaction and not above it.

**Frameworks and terms.** *Abjection* (Kristeva); *apotropaion* (carried over from ch 9); *soul at zero*; *reintegration*; *homeopathic model*; *sacred precinct*; *abject → civilization*; *dark epiphany* (a use of Joyce's "epiphany," never formally defined here).

**Works.** *The Damnation Game* (Carys); *Dracula*; *The Italian*; *The Exorcist* (Karras revived by horror); *Catch-22*; "The Yellow Wallpaper"; *Bartleby*; Dickinson's poems; Defoe's plague orders; *Bone Games* (epigraph).

**Application to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED).**
- **Her climax.** Morgan's one character epiphany gives a three-step shape: (a) complete despair or numbness, (b) seeing one's own condition mirrored in another person, (c) a choice, to disintegrate or to pull oneself together. For Garcelle (who has been at "zero" since she lost her name and sight) the mirror could be a scavenger whose vice is a kind of withdrawal or addiction, or someone who offers her no fear and no hatred. Whether she breaks or reconstitutes is the **author's open question**; Morgan supports both outcomes. His model implies that the epiphany happens *because* she sees the abject (the thing she has been pushing away) in another, which is a fresh way to think about revenge as a reaction against her own abjection (her ruined eyes, her lost name).
- **For the audience.** The reintegration beat (King; Harker's morning) suggests the film's final image should offer relief to the *viewer* even if the lead's fate is grim: a long shot of the Moon and empty space (echoing the cold open) after the claustrophobic station would be the "fresh air after the vault." That is a design suggestion, not a requirement.
- **The uncanny as the bare wall.** Morgan's definition (the negation of art, culture and civilization showing through the finish) maps well onto a space station: stripped panels revealing bare structure, an environment whose decoration has been removed. It also applies to Garcelle, a figure who has had her cosmetic "finish" (name, ordinary optics) burned off.
- **Vices as the thing the rip shows.** If the tear shows each scavenger what they want, Kristeva's abject (what we push aside to remain ourselves) is a literary precedent: each is confronted by what they have been rejecting. PROPOSED, not Morgan.
- *No direct application:* the plague-ordinance, infant-waste and homeopathy material, except as an explanation of why audiences want to watch a film like this.

### 2.5 Chapter 9: "Apotropaion and the Hideous Obscure" (pp. 200-223): read in full, extracted in detail

*The brief asked for a light summary of this chapter, but it contains the book's best material on bewilderment, the derelict ship and clashing realities, so it is treated here at length.*

**Core claims.**
1. **Obscurity is essential to horror (Burke).** Knowing the full extent of a danger takes away much of the fear (pp. 200-201, Burke quoted). Humans are poorly equipped for night; horror reproduces the loss of bearings in its form: nested reports, static, garbled signals, flawed transmissions. Morgan calls clear seeing the culture's **apotropaion**, its ward against fear (Paglia: the Westerner "knows by seeing," p. 201). Horror withholds it.
2. **Technique list drawn from his examples.** *The Howling*'s jittery credit sequence and the wire that fails during a sting (p. 201); *Night of the Living Dead*'s crude production making the viewer feel the film itself is shaky (pp. 200-201); the *Blair Witch Project*'s handheld camera and lurching flashlight (pp. 202-205); *Blowout*'s dying wire (pp. 204); James's evasive dialogue in *The Turn of the Screw* that raises apprehension by never stating the danger (pp. 203-204); Poe's non-specific settings (p. 204). He proposes that a "horror communication model" would encourage static, interruption, deception and flawed transmission (pp. 204-205), and ties this to Wiener's cybernetics and "cybernetic anxiety" when attention is interfered with in a crisis (pp. 201, 209).
3. **Rationality fails.** Medicine and psychiatry fall short in the face of the preternatural and eventually withdraw (Usher, *The Yellow Wallpaper*, *The Exorcist*, *Stigmata*) (p. 202).
4. **Eyes.** Putting out the eyes and the flood of darkness is an especially dreaded horror; Morgan cites Freud for the claim that no bodily injury is feared as much as an injury to the eye (p. 202).
5. **The derelict ship is "a recurrent image of failing apotropaion" (p. 205).** The unmaintained ship (the *San Dominick*, Coleridge's mariner's ship, Dracula's *Demeter*, Poe's *Pym*, *Nosferatu*) inverts the sailing ship's ideal as high art and "the clean and proper body," and is also a microcosm of society (p. 205). "Benito Cereno" is Morgan's long case: Delano boards a ship that has not shown its colors, finds rust, mold, a dolorous bell, a crew whose true situation is hidden from him (the enslaved men have in fact seized control and are staging a charade), and an enfeebled master (pp. 205-209). Morgan calls the boarding experience queer and unheimlich, like entering a strange house with strange inmates (p. 206, partly Melville's wording). The visitor struggles to rationalize his forebodings as an overactive imagination, as Usher's visitor does (pp. 206-207).
6. **Clashing frames.** Using Mary Douglas and Milner: a frame marks off one kind of reality, and when two frames overlap or collide, the result is a vertigo of category-clash (p. 208). Delano's frame is the orderly, functioning American ship; the *San Dominick* is the unheimlich frame; he looks back at his own ship, *Rover*, hoping its clarity will break the spell (pp. 208-212). The disinformation he gathers is not a puzzle but a disturbance of his whole mapping of reality: "All this is very queer now" and a feeling of incipient seasickness (p. 209). Further examples: the "Mad Trist" read aloud as noise at the end of Usher (p. 209); Faulkner's "That Evening Sun," where Nancy's terror is conveyed through the irrelevant chatter of the children (pp. 210-211).
7. **Mess and rust.** Morgan notes that organic oxidation and the rusting of metal are not different in kind, so our own deterioration is "not different in kind from that of the things around us" (p. 212). This is the book's one clear bridge from body to machine and structure. **UNSURE:** it is a passing observation inside the *Benito Cereno* reading, not a developed argument, so do not lean on it as Morgan's position on machines.
8. **Horror is amoral and refuses a comforting voice.** Citing Langer, Aiken and Barbauld, and Fiedler's "analytical versus projective" distinction, he argues that "Benito Cereno" gives the reader no authoritative moral voice to settle things, much as nightmare offers none (pp. 212-215). Horror's conservatism is an Apollonian demand for restraint, not a political view (pp. 215-217). Realistic place names and detail make the later slide into the hallucinatory more disturbing (Lovecraft's Innsmouth, King, Barker, p. 217). Ligotti on *Night of the Living Dead*: "its only news is nightmare" (p. 217). The strongest horror disallows the apotropaion of social progress: no police, no social solution (p. 221-223).
9. **A technical lesson.** *Stigmata* ends with a real-world message about a hidden scroll; Morgan calls that an error because it pulls the viewer out of the hallucinatory frame (p. 222).
10. **Tartarus of Maids** (pp. 218-221) is the book at its most gynecological and is not relevant to this film.

**Application to the Garcelle film (PROPOSED).**
- **The station is the *San Dominick*.** The crew's optimism and their ship as the "good frame" (named, ordered, maybe a Newfoundland-dog name like *Rover*) against the station as the "unheimlich frame." A scene in which a scavenger looks out at their ship for reassurance and the view is slightly wrong (the ship too near, too small, with the wrong light) uses Morgan's *Rover* beat exactly.
- **Garcelle's damaged sight as the film's form.** Morgan says the loss of clear seeing is the loss of the apotropaion. Her black corneas and mismatched irises can be the film's visual grammar for degraded, unreliable perception: the audience sees through noise, dropouts, halos, a doubled image. Use only a few shots, but make them consistent. Note that Freud's eye remark (p. 202) is the book's only direct statement about eye injury and that Morgan treats it as bodily horror; the film can take the same dread without gore.
- **Interference as an audio-visual rule.** The sensor and comms failures in *The Howling* and *Blowout* are cheap and unrepeatable; use them on the crew's link to their own ship, so that the one lifeline is garbled at the key moment.
- **Rust equals wounds.** Morgan's own sentence lets a station's corrosion and a robot's damage stand in for a body's decay. This is how the book's bio-horror can be translated into a machine-and-place register.
- **Science fails.** The author's physics research is for a film where the rip is "possible"; Morgan's pattern is that characters who explain it rationally are the ones that fail, and the explanation is the apotropaion that will not hold.
- **Do not moralize at the end.** *Stigmata* shows the cost of a real-world epilogue. The vice-driven deaths should not be framed by a moral voice (an analytical, "they deserved it" narrator); Morgan's amoral nightmare mode points the other way.

## 3. Light summaries: chapters 1 to 6 (chapter 9 is in 2.5)

### 3.1 Chapter 1, "Mortal Coils: The Comic-Horror Double Helix" (pp. 14-36)
*Argument.* Horror and comedy are twin strands: comedy dramatizes the life process's upswing and rebound, horror its decline and the failure to rebound. Morgan starts with Edith Wharton's childhood typhoid (the body "overrun by an alien force," pp. 14-15), argues that horror is driven by the recognition that biology is fate (p. 15), and says literature of horror reaches the body by symbolic enactment, not by real peril (pp. 17-18). He sketches a long tradition of informal grotesque writing (pp. 20-25), argues that tragedy is a poor opposite for comedy and horror is the better one (pp. 22-25), and uses Langer, Bakhtin and Barber to define the comic "sense of life" (pp. 25-34). *Catch-22*'s Snowden scene shows horror as the dark ground of a comic novel (pp. 26-27). He ends with Ligotti's altar of the gothic: an abandoned, decayed structure filled with fetishes of disuse, including **dolls with broken faces**, and Barker's phrase "the soul at zero" (pp. 35-36). *Bearing on the film.* One passage (p. 21) contrasts horror's return of the body with the "information age's" disembodied intelligence, the closest Morgan comes to AI. The Ligotti altar is a visual cousin to a station full of damaged things (the author's project calls its robots "Dolls"; I make no claim beyond the resemblance).

### 3.2 Chapter 2, "The Muse of Horror: Traditions of Dreadful Imagining" (pp. 37-66)
*Argument.* Horror existed long before the gothic novel: in plague chronicles (Dekker, Thucydides, the Black Death), martyrologies and execution accounts (Foxe, Damiens), medieval death art and the *danse macabre*, Christian terror teaching, the cult of the charnel house, and the Catholic/Protestant tension that gave the gothic novel its lurid physicality (pp. 40-66). The supernatural tale on its own is "airy"; it needs bodily grounding (pp. 38-40). *Bearing on the film.* (1) **Vice-fitted punishment is an old horror form**: Bosch and Bruegel imagined torments suited to Avarice, Gluttony, Vanity (p. 48); *Seven* revives it; and Morgan relays King's description of a Bradbury undertaker who performs "curiously moral atrocities" on his clients (pp. 48-49). This is the nearest thing in the book to the author's vice-driven deaths. (2) Plague quarantine as a "segmented, immobile, frozen space" under surveillance (Foucault, pp. 52-53) is a model for a locked wheel under watch. (3) The death ship *Demeter* (p. 63). (4) St. Lucy and her eyes on a dish (p. 60), later echoed in *Angel Heart* (ch 5, p. 129): eyes as a martyr's attribute.

### 3.3 Chapter 3, "Macabre Aesthetics" (pp. 67-89)
*Argument.* Horror's aesthetic is the *arhythm* of life: disestablishment, things "in the unmaking" (King), and exposure to harm. It works by making readers feel their own vulnerability through small bodily details (cold, wet shoes, a cough), by inverting the vital elements (stagnant water, artificial light, stale air), by presenting monsters as analogs of disease with "a motive without a mind," and by the **malevolent hover**: a persecutor who is always watching (pp. 80-85). Case studies: the *Blair Witch Project* (dread without a gross-out climax, a map that no longer works), Poe's "Cask of Amontillado," Douglass's account of slavery, "Benito Cereno," Hawthorne's "Rappaccini's Daughter." *Bearing on the film.* (1) King's distinction between sustained dread and sudden eruption (p. 70) and the *Blair Witch* example support a film that withholds gore. (2) Small physical discomforts as the entry point to terror (cold, damp, breath) can be done with a crew in a failing habitat. (3) The hovering persecutor (Covey, Babo, Madame de la Rougiere, Annie Wilkes) is the best model in the book for a hunter present from "multiple angles" (see 2.6).

### 3.4 Chapter 4, "The Anxiety of Organism" (pp. 90-112)
*Argument.* The mind, proud of its reason, is unsettled by the organic: the skin as fragile container, the body's inside showing up, drives without personality (the sea creatures of Melville, Lovecraft's amphibians, stalkers like Alex in *Fatal Attraction*), proliferating life, the Faustian denial of mortality, and the "information age" repression of the body (Hayles on Turing, pp. 107-108). Science is the intellect's "militant wing" and is shown as a violator (Frankenstein, Rappaccini, Captain Obed). Morgan also confronts Lovecraft's and Céline's racism as projected bio-anxiety (pp. 100-101). Flannery O'Connor and *Fargo* show characters who are cartoons until a moment of physical injury makes them real (pp. 111-112). *Bearing on the film.* (1) The Jentsch/Freud automaton passage and the "dim mechanical livingness" examples (pp. 100-103) are the closest the book gets to a machine's dread. (2) O'Connor's technique is a hint for the scavengers' characterization: introduce each as a flat type, then let one physical vulnerability make them real. (3) The Hayles passage frames what a gynoid means in Morgan's argument (see section 6).

### 3.5 Chapter 5, "Acquaintance with the Night: America and the Muse of Horror" (pp. 113-131)
*Argument.* American writers did build a gothic tradition: Freneau's "House of Night," Brockden Brown, Poe, the Puritan preachers, Southern gothic, Lovecraft's New England, and true crime (*Fall River*, *In Cold Blood*). Any setting that shows decline can serve (a faded mansion or a hotel gone to seed). A 1980s family of crime-horror films (*The First Deadly Sin*, *Blowout*, *Angel Heart*, *Fatal Attraction*) melds noir and gothic, and in them the investigator is dragged into the case rather than staying detached. *Bearing on the film.* Mostly low. Two small points: the idea that **any decaying setting works** (so a decaying future station is legitimate), and the noir-gothic point that the "detached investigator" fails and is pulled in (the scavengers' prospecting is detached at first).

### 3.6 Chapter 6, "Dark Carnival: The Esoterics of Celebration" (pp. 132-157)
*Argument.* Carnival suspends ordinary rules; in horror this becomes **festive horror**, where costume, parade, laughter and fair become vehicles of menace (Poe's "Cask," "Hop Frog," "Masque of the Red Death," Hawthorne's Merry Mount, *Black Orpheus*, Ligotti, *Tightrope*). The book then reads *Halloween* and *Friday the 13th* as conservative: careless, indulgent teens are killed and the disciplined girl survives, which Morgan reads as an "Apollonian" demand for restraint rather than mere prudishness (pp. 150-157). *Bearing on the film.* (1) **The slasher logic is the closest published model for the author's vice-driven deaths**: neglected duty and indulgence kill; but Morgan's list is broad (sex, neglect, mockery, laxity), not a catalogue of individual vices, and his survivors are rewarded for discipline. (2) **Revenge figures**: Montressor kills at carnival with a patient plan and a private "execution ritual" and then feels flat (pp. 138-141, 86); Hop Frog, a maimed and powerless jester, turns a masquerade into a fire that kills his king and councilors and escapes through the roof (pp. 144-145); Mrs. Voorhees is "a conservative avenging angel" (p. 153). None is a robot or a hunter in a space station, but they are the book's avengers. (3) The "peace of the fair" breach (p. 137) is a model for a salvage crew that arrives under a truce and breaks it.

## 4. Master list (a): frameworks and terms

- **Abject / abjection** (Kristeva): what must be rejected to remain a self or a society (corpse, filth, the body's insides); horror stages it and so purifies it. Ch 4, 10.
- **Anxiety of organism:** dread at being an organism, meat in a fragile container; the book's organizing fear. Ch 4.
- **Apotropaion:** a charm that wards off evil; Morgan's term for clear seeing, order, housekeeping and social progress, all of which horror disables. Ch 9, 10.
- **Bio-horror / biomorphic imagination:** the thesis; horror's images and fears rooted in embodied life. Intro.
- **Community of the life principle** (Weston): body, house, land and society share one fate. Ch 7, 8.
- **Comic-horror double helix:** comedy and horror as intertwined strands of ascent and decline. Ch 1.
- **Disestablishment / "things in the unmaking"** (King): the core of dread. Ch 3, 10.
- **Fisher King / Waste Land pattern:** the afflicted host, the failing land, the visiting healer. Ch 7.
- **Visitor and healer; failed healing:** the horror form of the quest. Ch 7.
- **Languishment, lethargy, indolence; pod-people vagueness:** the organism giving up; emptied people; untidiness as symptom. Ch 7, 8.
- **Sinister loci / genius loci:** places that carry the evil. Ch 8.
- **Heimlich / unheimlich** (Freud): homelike versus the "unhomely" uncanny. Ch 8, 9.
- **Gatekeeper and threshold ritual:** a marked, delayed entry. Ch 8.
- **Psychic carryover:** a place retains its former occupants. Ch 8.
- **Terror in sunlight:** horror beside the ordinary. Ch 8.
- **Wrong geometry:** distortion of dimension as an attack on the mind (King on Lovecraft). Ch 8.
- **Non-place, interstice, liminal point:** unplanned gaps and service zones, the ditch. Ch 8.
- **Lostness / bewilderment; "the hideous obscure":** loss of bearings as embodied vertigo. Ch 9.
- **Frames** (Douglas, Milner): colliding realities cause vertigo. Ch 9.
- **Analytical versus projective** (Fiedler): realistic characters versus symbolic projections; mixing confuses. Ch 9.
- **Apollonian / Dionysian:** restraint versus indulgence; horror's "conservatism." Ch 6, 9.
- **Festive horror / dark carnival / masquerade:** carnival forms turned toward menace. Ch 6.
- **Grotesque realism; material bodily principle; degradation** (Bakhtin): the comic body that renews; in horror, degradation has no renewal. Ch 1, 3, 6.
- **Malevolent hovering:** the persecutor who is felt everywhere. Ch 3.
- **Surmounted** (Freud): beliefs we think we have outgrown that return as the uncanny. Ch 2.
- **Soul at zero; dark epiphany; reintegration; homeopathic model:** Ch 10.
- **Cybernetic anxiety** (Wiener): fear when information flow is interfered with in a crisis. Ch 9.

## 6. Questions this book raises for the film, and what it handles weakly or not at all

### Questions raised (for the author)
1. If the film's dread is not bodily, what stands in for the body? Candidates from Morgan: the station (wound-and-place continuity), Garcelle's optics and name, the crew's sense of an ordered world.
2. Are the scavengers the visitors and healers of the Waste Land pattern (ch 7), and does the film want the "failed healing" beat in which an offer of help or trade is refused?
3. Is the station neglected (Morgan's default) or immaculate in the places she uses ("terror in sunlight")? Or both?
4. How does the audience get any "reintegration" (ch 10) when the lead is the threat, and does the film want one?
5. Is the film an amoral nightmare (ch 9: no authoritative moral voice) or a slasher-style discipline story (ch 6: indulgence punished)? What does the film think of the vices that kill?
6. Who is Garcelle's double, if anyone: her torturer, the earlier victim, a scavenger who mirrors her isolation?
7. How much of the rip's rules does the audience learn (Burke: fear needs obscurity)? What plays the part of the "good frame" the crew carries, and when does it break?
8. Whose eyes do we see through, and does the picture degrade when we are in hers?
9. Does the crew feel watched before the first death (malevolent hover)?
10. Where do the rip's mouths open: in living quarters or in service zones, spokes and the hub?

### Handled weakly or not at all
- **Robots and artificial minds.** The only passages are Hayles on Turing (ch 4, pp. 107-108; also ch 1, p. 21) and the automaton/"mechanical livingness" lines (ch 4, pp. 100-103). Morgan treats disembodied intelligence as the thing horror reacts against; a gynoid is outside his model. His own sentence on rust (ch 9, p. 212) is the only bridge from organism to machine. He never considers a machine that suffers, has memory, or hates.
- **A monster who is the protagonist.** Not discussed. His monsters are drives with no psychology and "no charm, humor, flair" (ch 4, pp. 103-104); his focal figures are visitors and heroines. Closest: narrators who are avengers (Montressor, ch 6), Hop Frog (ch 6) and Mrs. Voorhees (ch 6). A lead with a history and a grievance goes against his grain, so this book cannot guide the key structural choice.
- **Liminal space.** The word is used four times, loosely. Chapter 8 gives non-place examples but no theory. The Graymatter notes and the books the triage proposes (Fisher, Augé) cover this better.
- **Science-fiction settings.** Only a remark that SF combines well with horror (ch 2, p. 38). No spacecraft, isolation in vacuum, or station horror; derelict ships are all sea vessels.
- **Space-time distortion.** Only "wrong geometry" (ch 8, p. 194) and "derelict hours" (ch 8, p. 185). Nothing on time loops or multiple angles of access.
- **Individualized vices as plot engine.** Absent; the nearest are the vice-fitted torments in medieval art and *Seven* (ch 2) and the slasher morality (ch 6).
- **Craft.** None. This is criticism, not instruction; I found no advice on structure, pacing or character.
- **Politics.** The book names the dangers of horror's triggers (the Nazi film, Lovecraft and Céline) but also says horror is "conservative" (ch 6, pp. 153-157; ch 9, pp. 215-217); readers should keep their own judgment.

### Most reusable items for this film (PROPOSED)
1. Ch 9: the derelict ship, clashing frames and "good ship in the distance" beat; interference as form.
2. Ch 8: the tear introduced as small wrong geometry; service zones as liminal; the gatekeeper delay.
3. Ch 7: Garcelle as the afflicted host and the crew as healers whose help fails.
4. Ch 3: the malevolent hover as the way to be everywhere at once.
5. Ch 10: Carys's mirror epiphany, as one possible shape for Garcelle's turn.
