# Bat Semi-Domestication — the Commensal Pathway

2026-09-23. **Author-stated canon**, recorded here rather than inside any one project because it spans
centuries and outlives the work that introduces it.

---

## The canon

**Melinda Barlow** lives in the mountains of **Colorado**, inside the **Colorado Republic**, during the First
Interwar Period. She is **not famous in-universe** — obscure in her own time and after it.

**She is nonetheless one of the most consequential people in the setting.** She begins the rudimentary process
by which bats become, **hundreds of years later, something like "wild pets"** — kept the way someone might
keep an ocelot or a Komodo dragon. **Not domesticated**, which takes tens of thousands of years, but **tame
enough to live as "just-outside-your-home friends."**

Her story is a **short film, roughly 20 minutes.**

---

## Why the framing is exactly right — the commensal pathway

**The author has independently described a real and named thing.** Domestication is understood to have three
pathways, and "just-outside-your-home friends" *is* the first of them:

| Pathway | How it works | Animals |
|---|---|---|
| **Commensal** | The animal **moves into the human niche on its own.** Nobody sets out to domesticate it | **Dogs, cats**, possibly pigs |
| **Prey** | Food animals brought under management | Sheep, goats, cattle, reindeer |
| **Directed** | Deliberate capture for work | Horses, donkeys, camels |

**The decisive line, and it is the story:** *humans did not intend to domesticate mammals from the commensal
pathway.* It happened to them.

**So Melinda is not domesticating bats. She is starting something her descendants finish without meaning to** —
which is a better story than deliberate taming, and it is also what actually happens in the real cases. Dogs
came this way, at least 15,000 years ago, selected for behavior rather than for any product.

**And taming is not domestication.** Taming changes an individual animal's behavior. Domestication is a
multi-generational genetic change across a population. **Melinda can only ever tame.** Everything past that
belongs to people she never meets — which is precisely why she can be obscure and still be one of the most
consequential figures in the setting.

## The starting point is real, and it is humble

**Bat houses already exist.** They are wooden boxes with the opening on the underside, hung where they will
catch enough sun — *"bat boxes that are too shaded will not heat up enough to attract a maternity colony."*
People build them for conservation, and they work: one Australian project's 42 boxes peaked at **280 bats**
over twelve years.

**They also fail constantly** — boxes fall down, the wood rots, and ants, rats, possums and spiders move in.
**And some bats will never use them at all**, because species that roost in foliage or large cavities simply
do not take to boxes.

**That is a gift for a twenty-minute film.** Melinda's work is a wooden box, a south-facing wall, and years of
failure — not a breakthrough. The drama is patience, not invention. And the one species that *does* take to
her boxes is the one whose descendants are still with people centuries later.

---

## What this does for the wider setting

- **It gives the far future a texture that costs nothing to maintain** — bats around human dwellings, tolerated
  and half-tame, in a world that has otherwise lost a great deal.
- **It is a rare thread of continuity across the whole timeline.** Most of this universe's long arcs are about
  things breaking. This one is about something small and good that survives because nobody was guarding it.
- **It makes obscurity thematic rather than incidental.** A world that grants and then revokes robot
  personhood is a world with strong opinions about who counts. Melinda counts enormously and is never noticed.

## Open — do not invent these

- ⚠️ **Which bat species.** Colorado's mountains have several, and the choice matters: only some use boxes at
  all, and the story's species is the one with descendants. Needs a real species, not a generic bat.
- ⚠️ **What happened to Colorado's bat populations across the collapse.** **White-nose syndrome** is a live
  real-world epizootic and would have been running before the war. Whether it burned through, whether isolated
  mountain hibernacula survived it, and whether the collapse of human movement *helped* by halting
  cave-to-cave transmission — all researchable, none researched.
- ⚠️ **Melinda's dates**, and how far "hundreds of years later" reaches. It plausibly lands near the Falkland
  Treaty era or the game's present, but nothing is pinned.
- ⚠️ **Whether the bats appear in any other work.** If they show up as background texture in a later-set
  story, that is the payoff of this one, and it should be placed deliberately.
- ⚠️ **Whether anyone later knows her name.** The canon says she is not famous. Whether that means *forgotten*
  or merely *uncelebrated* is a real choice, and it changes the ending of her film.

## Worth considering, offered not asserted

**Belyaev's silver-fox experiment** is the obvious real-world reference point and is **not** covered in the
source consulted here, so it is named from general knowledge and unverified. The reason to check it: it is the
standard demonstration that **marked tameness can emerge in a few dozen generations**, far faster than
domestication proper. If the figures hold, it supports the author's structure exactly — **fast tameness, slow
domestication** — and would give a defensible answer to how far the bats could plausibly have come in the
centuries available.
