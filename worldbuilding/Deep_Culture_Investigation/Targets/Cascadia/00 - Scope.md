**Flags on the territory (for the author):**
1. **The Panhandle's limits.** The territory file says the Panhandle's "exact inland extent is not" clear at map resolution. This scope uses the ten Southeast Alaska boroughs and census areas, **including Yakutat** (663 people in 2020), whose coast runs north of Glacier Bay. Decision 12.
2. **California east of the Sierra.** The territory file leaves open whether the claim "extends over the whole state or stops at the Sierra crest", and reads the map's whole-state fills as suggesting the whole state. The whole-state rule (2026-09-23) points the same way. This scope uses the whole state. Decision 13.
3. **Two legal families.** Cascadia spans common law (the three U.S. states, Alaska and British Columbia) and Mexican civil law (the Bajas), plus the tribal and First Nations orders. Stage 1 will need all three families [claim: to be confirmed at Stage 1].

## 2. Units

**Proposed: five settler regional cultures (one with a sub-unit) and six Indigenous units, eleven in all.** The settler units follow Woodard's county lines where he covers the ground (the U.S. states), his text where he covers it only loosely (British Columbia, the Panhandle, Baja California), and a proposal of this scope where he does not cover it at all (Baja California Sur). The Indigenous units follow the anthropological culture-area tradition (Kroeber; the Smithsonian *Handbook of North American Indians*), with tier-2 peoples named under each. The unit list is the author's to confirm before Stage 1.

**Cascadia merges three of Woodard's nations, not two.** The cross-reference file and the character profile speak of two (Left Coast and Far West). Woodard's own text puts "southern California and the Imperial Valley" and Baja California in El Norte, and his map shades San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara, Ventura, Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, San Diego and Imperial counties as El Norte [scholarly: Woodard, read]. The Proposed Extrapolations file already found this (its flag 2); this scope confirms it from the book and adds a fourth framework for Baja California Sur, which Woodard leaves outside his eleven (his map shades it in the light "context" tone).

### Disagreements between founders and population (flag for the author)

1. **Which unit is the core.** Founders, the nation's name and the character profile point to the Left Coast with the British Columbia coast. Population points to El Norte: 40.0%, against 37.2% for the Left Coast, the British Columbia coast and the Panhandle together. Decision 7.
2. **California's Far West.** Its founders were the forty-niners, the corporate and federal water works and, later, the Dust Bowl Southerners. Its present population is 42.2% Hispanic, close to El Norte's share. Woodard himself calls El Norte "resurgent", so the seam may be moving north. Not resolved here.
3. **The British Columbia coast's founders.** Woodard: settlers from Seattle, Oregon and northern California; an extension of the Left Coast. Canadian sources: a Hudson's Bay Company and British colonial founding, with people of British background about 60% of the province by 1900. The character profile and the Vancouver file side with the Canadian sources, but they also say Woodard's research does not support folding British Columbia into the Left Coast, and his text does claim it [scholarly: Woodard, read]. Decision 2.
4. **Southern California's founders.** Woodard: El Norte, founded 1769-1782. Against it: the small Californio population (about 8,000 non-Native people in all of Alta California in 1846) and the Anglo-Midwestern re-founding of the 1880s-1920s. Today's Hispanic plurality agrees with Woodard's label, but it comes mainly from 20th-century immigration rather than continuity from the founders [claim: this scope's reasoning]. Decision 6.
5. **Baja California's founders.** Woodard: El Norte. The founding evidence: weak Dominican missions, then late colonization through foreign land companies and a U.S. agricultural company working with Chinese labor at Mexicali, closer to the Far West's corporate pattern than to norteño ranching; then the 1937 *ejido* seizure; today 38.8% of residents were born in another state. Decision 5.
6. **The Panhandle.** The DCI frameworks index reads Alaska as Indigenous-majority; Woodard puts Juneau on the Left Coast; the census shows a population 23.3% Native overall, 45-49% in Prince of Wales-Hyder, Yakutat and Hoonah-Angoon, and 19.4% in Juneau. Decision 4.
7. **Santa Rosa and Sonoma** (the setting of *Streetside Harmonies*). The Proposed Extrapolations file says the area's "first effective institutions are Hispanic" (the Sonoma mission, 1823), with the Yankee wave second. Woodard maps Sonoma as Left Coast and says Spanish influence "ceased altogether at San Francisco". Both are recorded here. Decision 19.
8. **The population total.** The Material Base gives about 62 million, "order of magnitude only". This count is 61,141,109 (mixed years). Not a conflict with canon; it supplies the present-day figure the character profile lists as its largest quantitative gap (open item 6).

## 4. Seams

Where units meet inside Cascadia, and what divides them. Stage 5 must keep each of these as a named outlier, never averaged away.

1. **Left Coast and Far West: the crest of the Cascades, the Coast Mountains and the Coast Ranges.** In Washington and Oregon the line runs along the Cascade crest (the eastern edges of Whatcom to Skamania, and of Lane, Douglas and Jackson); in California it runs between the coast counties and the Central Valley, so Sacramento is Far West while Yolo and Solano are Left Coast on the map's reading; in British Columbia it runs between the coast and the interior. **What divides them:** wet against dry, maritime trade against extraction and irrigation, and, in Woodard, opposed politics: the Left Coast "battles constantly against the libertarian-corporate agenda" of the Far West [scholarly: Woodard, read]. **The project already records this as Cascadia's main internal weakness:** the "ladder, not a chain" finding, in which the Columbia Gorge, the Cascade passes, the Fraser Canyon and the Coquihalla all cut along this line, and the interior holds the eastern end of each [project: Industry supplement B.3.9; Material Base §0].
2. **Left Coast and El Norte: Monterey and San Luis Obispo.** The map's line falls between Monterey and San Luis Obispo counties. Woodard dates it early: El Norte's culture was "well rooted south of Monterey", and "California's north-south split was already apparent by 1845" [scholarly: Woodard, read]. **What divides them:** a Yankee civic founding against a Spanish and Mexican one; the Gold Rush north against the rancho south [scholarly: Woodard, read].
3. **Far West and El Norte: the Transverse Ranges and the Mojave edge.** Kern and San Bernardino counties against Los Angeles, Ventura and Riverside. **The seam splits one metropolitan area:** San Bernardino County is Far West and Riverside County is El Norte, although both form the Inland Empire. At present this seam is weak in the data (both sides about 42-44% Hispanic in California; disagreement 2). The project's chokepoints Tejon, Tehachapi and Cajon sit on it (Industry supplement B.3.9).
4. **El Norte and Sudcalifornia: the 28th parallel (the state line), across the Vizcaíno desert.** Woodard's El Norte stops at the state line. **What divides them:** a late, border-oriented, migrant society in the north against a small, older, mission-and-ranch society in the south; the Jesuit province in the south and the Dominican one in the north [claim: the mission provinces from search summaries; their split not checked]. The project records the Vizcaíno as a north-south cut with only a sea bypass (Industry supplement B.3.9).
5. **The Left Coast and the British Columbia coast: the international line on the water** (the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Haro Strait) and the 49th parallel. Both are common-law and coastal, but they differ in founding (Yankee against British colonial), constitution (presidential and federal against Westminster and federal), and the many laws that differ between the two countries [claim, not checked; Stage 1]. Woodard says the coast identifies across the line; the Canadian founding evidence says the line runs deeper (disagreement 3).
6. **California and Baja California: the U.S.-Mexican border, inside El Norte.** Woodard calls the borderlands "a single norteño culture" split by "a large wall" [scholarly: Woodard, read]. For law it is the sharpest seam in Cascadia: common law against Mexican civil law [claim: to confirm at Stage 1]. Tijuana-San Diego and Mexicali-Imperial are the paired cities.
7. **The settler coast and the Indigenous north.** Haida Gwaii, the Nass and Skeena, Prince of Wales Island, Hoonah-Angoon and Yakutat are places where Native people are close to half the population or more and where treaty, title or self-government is strongest. This is the edge of Woodard's First Nation zone (decision 9).
8. **Peoples split by borders.** The Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian straddle the Alaska-British Columbia line (Metlakatla's people came from British Columbia [claim, not checked]); the Kumeyaay/Kumiai and the Cocopah/Cucapá straddle the U.S.-Mexican line; the Ktunaxa, Syilx and Sinixt straddle the 49th parallel [claim, not checked].
9. **Cascadia's outer seams** (for the library, not for this target's composite): the Far West continues into the Colorado target, El Norte into the Sonora target, the northern Dene into the Alaska target, and the Plateau and Great Basin peoples into Colorado.

## 5. Scope statement

Following Woodard's own practice of saying what he leaves out ("peeling the onion"; American Nations Extraction, Part Two), these distinctions are deliberately not units. Each can still appear in Stage 4's **Variation** answers.

- **Sub-regions inside each unit.** Inside the Left Coast: the Bay Area, Puget Sound, the Willamette Valley and the North Coast. Inside the Far West: the Central Valley, the Sierra foothills, the Columbia Plateau and the Palouse, eastern Oregon's ranch country, the Mojave, and British Columbia's Okanagan, Kootenays and Cariboo. Inside El Norte: Los Angeles, San Diego, the Imperial Valley, Tijuana, Mexicali and San Quintín. **Why:** the unit is the founding culture, and eleven units are already a heavy load.
- **Regional separatist identities** such as the "State of Jefferson" in northern California and southern Oregon [claim, not checked]. **Why:** sub-regional identity inside the Far West and the North Coast; it belongs under Variation and the seams.
- **Settlements that did not found a lasting society:** Russian Fort Ross, the Spanish post at Nootka Sound, the French-Canadian farms of the Willamette's French Prairie, the Mormon colony at San Bernardino [claim, not checked]. **Why:** the Doctrine of First Effective Settlement counts only lasting, self-perpetuating founders; these are recorded as prior layers where they matter.
- **Immigrant and diaspora identities as units:** Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Korean, Vietnamese, Hmong, Armenian, Iranian, Azorean Portuguese, Italian, Scandinavian, Russian sectarian (Molokan and Doukhobor), Jewish, African American and Central American communities, and Mexican-Americans as distinct from El Norte. **Why:** Woodard's doctrine treats later arrivals as assimilating to the founders' template, and the method treats tier-2 identities as evidence to compress; their home stocks are other targets' units and can be reused there. Decision 15 asks whether any should become units here.
- **Migrant Indigenous peoples from elsewhere:** Mixtec, Zapotec and Triqui from southern Mexico (in California and San Quintín), the Métis, Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders. **Why:** they are later layers in Cascadia, and their home units belong to other targets.
- **Each tribal nation and First Nation as its own unit.** **Why:** several hundred governments; compressed into six culture-area units with tier-2 peoples named. The legal grouping is separate (section 1).
- **Peoples with no living community:** the Guaycura, Pericú and southern Cochimí of Baja California Sur, who did not survive the mission period. **Why:** no living unit to research; they belong to Sudcalifornia's founding history.
- **Class, generation, gender and religion** as units. **Why:** they are the Variation question's job in every entry.
- **The years 2025-2083.** Present-day data stand in for the pre-war population of 2083; nothing is projected forward. **Why:** projection would be derivation, and Stage 6 is the author's.
- **South Asian communities in present-day British Columbia, California and Washington: excluded under the No Subcontinentals law; nothing further was researched. (Flagged for the author.)**

## Method notes

What in the runbook, the template or the brief did not fit, from this pilot:

1. **Section 8 differs between the runbook and the template.** The runbook's Stage 0 has "the library check" as item 8 and no decisions section; the template puts the library check into two columns and makes section 8 "Decisions for the author". The template was followed, as the brief asks, with a library note under section 2. Suggest aligning the runbook.
2. **STATUS position.** The template puts the title first and the STATUS line second; the runbook and the brief want STATUS on the first line. The brief was followed.
3. **The tag set has no place for official records** (statutes, treaties, court rulings, the Federal Register list) **or for trade-press syntheses** like Woodard's. Official counts and records were tagged [measured] and Woodard [scholarly] with a note. Suggest a `record` tag and a rule for syntheses (decision 20).
4. **"Zone within the target" is ambiguous:** the unit's place in the target, or the unit's own core, domain and sphere? Read here as the former. Suggest the column say so.
5. **"Founding population and when" does not fit Indigenous units.** Used "present at contact", with contact dates. Suggest a separate column set for Indigenous units (contact, dispossession, present legal form).
6. **"Founders' weight" has no scale.** Used high, medium and low with reasons. Suggest a fixed scale and a rule for how it combines with population in Stage 5.
7. **Population shares need unit territories,** and Woodard's lines exist only as a book-scale map. This scope wrote whole-county lists (section 3). Suggest storing the county-to-unit table in `Data/` so every target that shares a unit uses the same lines; the brief kept this job to one file, so it was not created.
8. **The frameworks index says Alaska is "read as Indigenous-majority".** That does not fit the Panhandle (23% Native), and Woodard's text puts Juneau on the Left Coast. Suggest the index distinguish Southeast Alaska.
9. **The jurisdiction definition** ("a tribal nation with its own courts") fits U.S. tribes, fits British Columbia's First Nations poorly (few have courts; their legal forms are treaty, self-government statute and title) and does not fit the Bajas (no Indigenous courts). Suggest "Indigenous governments with their own legal order", grouped by legal form.
10. **The one-line exclusion rule and census aggregates.** The U.S. census's "Asian" aggregate includes the excluded group. It was reported as the census's category, without a breakdown; suggest the rule say whether that is acceptable.
11. **Shared units need a lobe rule.** El Norte and the Far West are mostly outside Cascadia; whichever target writes them first will write from its own lobe. Suggest the unit library record the lobes it has covered.
12. **Workload.** Eleven units mean 143 lineage profiles and 132 sign passes for one target. Suggest a lighter track for sphere units (S1a, S5, I5, I6), such as five axes and the tier-1 concepts only.
13. **Data access.** The Census Bureau API now requires a key; the Vintage 2024 county files worked without one. Worth adding to `Shared/Sources_and_Datasets.md` (not edited here).
14. **The search budget** (about 40) was enough for scope, but most findings came from search summaries; Stage 1 should budget for reading primary sources in full.
