## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Kumyks**, whose language long served neighbors in the plains as a bridge, a brokering role.
- **Avars**, whose khans and imams dealt most often with the empires.
- **Dargins of the Akusha union**, for collective diplomacy by an assembly of villages.
- **Laks of Kazikumukh**, a khanate that bargained between Russia and its neighbors.
- **Lezgins**, for border talks with Baku.
- **Tabasarans and Aguls**, for small-people tactics between larger neighbors.
- **Derbent's Azerbaijanis**, for Persianate etiquette and the elçi tradition.
- **Mountain Jews**, often merchants and interpreters between communities.
- **Nogais**, with a steppe history of dealing with hordes, Kalmyks and Russia.
- **Akkin Chechens**, mediating between Dagestan and Chechnya.
- **Kizlyar Russians and Cossacks**, frontier go-betweens of the old line.
- **Sufi sheikhs and Salafi preachers**, as rival religious diplomats.
- **Young urban speakers of Russian only**, who may lose the old multilingual brokering.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for diplomatic history from the khanates to Gunib; mixed for everyday tact and family diplomacy; thin for current interethnic talks beyond press.
- **Languages needed:** Russian; Arabic and Persian for pre-modern letters; Kumyk and Avar for oral memory; Azerbaijani for Derbent.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the Caucasian War's memory is contested between Moscow and local narratives; border and Lezgin issues touch interstate relations; religious negotiations involve people later jailed or killed.
- **Held for review:** none.
