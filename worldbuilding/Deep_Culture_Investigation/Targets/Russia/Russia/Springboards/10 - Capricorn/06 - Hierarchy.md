## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Moscow elites and the regions:** capital privilege as a rank of place.
- **Descendants of nobility, clergy, merchants and peasants,** where families keep that memory.
- **Ukrainian regions:** the former serf provinces of the Dnieper against Habsburg Galicia and Transcarpathia; Cossack officer families.
- **Crimean Tatars,** with khanate ranks behind them.
- **Old Believers** (priestly and priestless) against the main church's hierarchy.
- **Nenets and Kola Sami herders:** herd ownership and kin rank, as each people describes it.
- **Roma communities;** labor migrants placed low in city hierarchies.
- **Prison and army subcultures** with their own informal ranks.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for imperial and Soviet history; mixed for present class data; thin for Arctic rank structures in English.
- **Languages needed:** Russian, Ukrainian, Crimean Tatar; Old Novgorodian for birchbark texts.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** prison caste terms include slurs and refer to abuse; quote sparingly. Serfdom and colonial rank in the Ukrainian lands are read differently by Russian and Ukrainian historians: keep both. Avoid any single "Russian servility" thesis.
- **Held for review:** none.

## 5. First queries
1. On gramoty.ru, search letters by addressee rank («господин», «челом бьет»).
2. Pull the 1897 census volumes for Moscow, Tver, Poltava and Kyiv governorates; tabulate soslovie.
3. Search the Russian National Corpus for «быдло», «холоп» and «мажор», by decade.
4. Read Freeze (1986) before any other history of estates.
5. Download ISSP inequality data for Russia; find the society-shape item.
