# Russia (core state): Reform (Aquarius): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Russia (core state), region Russia. **Language(s) for searching:** Russian and Ukrainian; Church Slavonic for the 17th-century church reform; Crimean Tatar (Ottoman-era script and modern Latin) for school reform. **Concept in one line:** deliberate change meant to improve a society's institutions, practices or people, short of overturning the whole; and the figure of the reformer.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Old Believers** (priestly and priestless) against the official church: the first great split over reform.
- **Former *zemstvo* provinces of left-bank Ukraine and central Russia** against provinces that had none.
- **Perestroika-era intelligentsia, 1990s "losers" (single-industry towns, pensioners) and winners.**
- **Western Ukraine, Kyiv and the east** on post-2014 reforms.
- **Crimean Tatars,** with their own reform current.
- **Nenets, Sami, Komi and Karelians,** as objects of Soviet reforms and as authors of revival programs.
- **Recovering drinkers and drug users;** former prisoners.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on reform from above and its histories; mixed on attitudes measured over time; thin on how northern peoples speak of reforms done to them.
- **Languages needed:** Russian, Ukrainian; Church Slavonic for the Schism; Crimean Tatar and Ottoman Turkish for the Jadids.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** 1990s reforms are bitterly contested; avoid either camp's framing as a finding. The Belomor book is propaganda; read it as such. Wartime Ukraine's reform debates are fast-moving.
- **Held for review:** none.
