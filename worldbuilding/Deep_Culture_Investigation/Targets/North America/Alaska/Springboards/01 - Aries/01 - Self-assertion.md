## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Peoples with formal speaking orders (Tlingit and Haida feasts; elders' councils in villages) against peoples with other turn-taking.
- Village (bush) communities against Anchorage, Fairbanks and Juneau.
- Long-resident settlers and "sourdough" families against newcomers, military families and seasonal workers.
- Fishing, oil, mining and tourism workplaces.
- Women and men; elders and youth; Orthodox and Old Believer communities; immigrant groups with their own norms (Filipino, Samoan, Hmong, Korean, Latino).
- Canadian lands, if held, treated separately.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Public minutes and newspapers are accessible; direct data on assertion norms by group is likely thin.
- **Languages needed:** English; Native-language dictionaries for terms; Russian for some communities.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** feast oratory and ceremonial speech can be owned or governed by clan; use only what holders have shared publicly. Avoid reading village silence as a lack of assertion.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.
- **Law:** none researched here; for what law says about speech and claims, see Libra and Capricorn.

## 5. First queries
1. Search the Alaska Native Language Center catalog for dictionary entries on "speak," "refuse," "boast," "argue."
2. List the Alaska Federation of Natives convention keynotes of recent decades and note the speaking order.
3. Sample public-comment minutes from three municipal assemblies (one urban, one regional hub, one village).
4. Search Alaska newspapers' letters pages for "speak up" and "stand up" to catch local idiom.
5. Look for oral histories where people describe disputes with bosses, neighbors or officials.
