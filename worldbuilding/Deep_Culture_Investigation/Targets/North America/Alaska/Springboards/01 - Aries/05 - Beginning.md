## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Native peoples with their own origin accounts and seasonal rounds (Iñupiat, Yup'ik/Cup'ik, Alutiiq, Unangax̂, Tlingit, Haida, Dene peoples): each separate.
- Descendants of Russian-America and Orthodox villages (Julian calendar, Lent and Easter as beginnings).
- Gold-rush, homestead and statehood-era settlers' descendants against recent arrivals.
- Military families and seasonal workers who start and end in cycles.
- Refugees and immigrants (Filipino, Samoan, Hmong, Korean, Latino).
- People in recovery, those leaving the state, and those who "came for a summer."
- Canadian lands, if held.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Settler beginnings are well documented; community-held origin material is not for general use; direct data on attitudes to starting over is likely thin.
- **Languages needed:** English; Native-language dictionaries; Russian and Church Slavonic for parish records.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** origin stories and first-catch ceremonies are owned or governed by their peoples; do not retell them. Use only what holders have published or shared. Naming at birth is covered in the domain sheet.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Search the Alaska State Library catalog for diaries and letters of first arrivals, by decade.
2. Get the dictionary words for "begin," "first" and "new" in each Native language from the language center.
3. Identify the year-opening events by region (ice, fish, whale, freeze-up) in local media.
4. Search the state museums and historical society for how they introduce Alaska's beginnings.
5. List recovery and resettlement services and read their public stories of fresh starts.
