*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America (pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada). **Language(s) for searching:** English; Russian; the Native languages (Yup'ik, Iñupiaq, Alutiiq, Tlingit, Gwich'in and other Athabaskan languages); Tagalog; Samoan; Spanish. **Concept in one line:** cover and refuge from danger and the elements: a roof, a safe place for those who have none, and the duty to give it.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Urban unsheltered people in Anchorage, Fairbanks and Juneau, against rural households in overcrowded or unfinished houses.
- Coastal villages facing flood and erosion (Yup'ik, Iñupiaq), against interior Athabaskan communities.
- Trail and bush travelers, for whom shelter is a cabin or a stranger's door.
- Women and children leaving violence; youth in foster care; elders.
- Military and seasonal workers; immigrant households that double up.
- Old Believer and Orthodox communities that give lodging through the church.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on counts and agency reports; mixed on how shelter is understood; thin on informal sheltering of kin and strangers.
- **Languages needed:** English; Native languages for shelter and guest words.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** shelter locations for violence survivors are confidential; village relocation is the villages' own matter. Sanctuary and tenancy law goes to Libra.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Pull the latest point-in-time counts for Anchorage and the rural regions.
2. Read the villages' own relocation pages and the Denali Commission's planning notes.
3. Search Project Jukebox for "roadhouse", "sod house", "winter camp" and "took us in".
4. Look up public-use cabin rules and the stories told about them.
5. Search the ANLC dictionaries for "shelter", "windbreak" and "guest".
