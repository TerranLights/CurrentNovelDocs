# Alaska: Work (Virgo): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America (pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada; Yukon and adjoining northwestern lands assumed). **Language(s) for searching:** English; Alaska Native languages (Iñupiaq, Yup'ik, Alutiiq, Dena languages, Tlingit and others); Yukon First Nations languages. **Concept in one line:** purposeful effort that produces, keeps up or provides something of value, paid or unpaid, and the place that effort holds in a life.

## 1. Search terms

| Tagalog | alaskero | Filipino cannery worker in Alaska | for the cannery workforce's own term, *check* |

English keywords: subsistence and cash economy, seasonal work, cannery, pipeline construction, oilfield rotation, gold rush labor, homesteading, "bush" jobs, fishing crew, unemployment in villages, work ethic, self-reliance.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Native subsistence households (by language area) against wage workers in the same village.
- Commercial fishing families and processors, including Filipino, Latin American and Pacific Islander crews (*check* which are large).
- Oilfield and pipeline rotation workers who live elsewhere against residents.
- Miners and gold-rush descendants; homesteaders; military families.
- Native regional corporations as employers against federal, state and borough jobs.
- Yukon First Nations workers against Whitehorse's government economy.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Rich on subsistence and on oral history; labor statistics are good but say little on attitudes; attitude surveys thin for Alaska alone.
- **Languages needed:** English; Native languages for the words; Tagalog for the cannery record.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** some oral histories are restricted by speaker or community; the mixing of "subsistence" with cash work is a politically charged topic in the state, so read each source's position as a claim.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Read two Division of Subsistence community reports and list how the households describe their year of work.
2. Search the state labor department's site for "seasonality" and "nonresident workers".
3. Look up the Yup'ik and Iñupiaq words glossed "work" and "help" in the Alaska Native Language Center's dictionaries.
4. Search oral-history catalogs for "pipeline", "cannery", "fish camp" and "gold".
5. Search Alaska obituaries for "hard worker" to see whether the phrase is used and by whom.
