# Alaska: Dissolution (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America (pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada). **Language(s) for searching:** English; the Indigenous languages of its lands (Yup'ik, Iñupiaq, Alutiiq, Unangam Tunuu, Dena'ina, Gwich'in, Tlingit, Eyak records and others); Russian; Tagalog; Spanish; French and Inuktitut on the Canadian side. **Concept in one line (proposed):** the coming apart of a fixed form: the loss of the self's edges in something larger, or the ending of bonds, groups and identities.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Native peoples with strongly retained languages against those with few remaining speakers; villages facing relocation against stable ones; Orthodox and Old Believer settlements, where assimilation is debated; urban Native residents in Anchorage; gold-rush and mining town descendants; evangelical and Pentecostal congregations; military families on short postings; Canadian-side Yukon towns.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Language, relocation and institutional records are rich; mystical and ecstatic experience is thin and mostly in worship communities' own words.
- **Languages needed:** English; Indigenous languages for first-voice material; Russian for church history.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** survivor testimony; ceremonial knowledge and anything about a language's last speakers; relocation debates still affect living communities. Use what peoples themselves publish.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Search the Alaska Native Language Archive for language-loss histories and revitalization accounts, by language.
2. Pull relocation case files and community statements for threatened villages.
3. Read the boarding-school inquiry volumes for assimilation language, survivors first.
4. Search church and congregation archives for ecstatic worship and church-closure records.
5. Search dementia-care and divorce-recovery materials from Alaska organizations for how families describe loss.
