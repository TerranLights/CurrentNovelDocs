# Alaska: Boundary (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America (pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada). **Language(s) for searching:** English; Indigenous languages of its lands (Yup'ik, Iñupiaq, Alutiiq, Unangam Tunuu, Dena'ina, Gwich'in, Tlingit, Haida and others); Russian; Tagalog; Spanish; Korean; French and Inuktitut on the Canadian side. **Concept in one line:** the lines a person or group draws between self and others, and between groups, places and categories, and how firmly they are held.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Native peoples with homelands crossing the Alaska-Canada border (Gwich'in, Tlingit, Han and others), kept apart by people; villages with outsider rules against open cities; Southeast Alaska ferry towns; homesteaders and off-grid residents; unhoused campers; Russian Old Believer settlements; military families and base communities; seasonal workers; Filipino, Korean and Latino households.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Boundary history and agency documents are rich; personal-space and privacy norms are thin and best found in guides, newspapers and oral accounts.
- **Languages needed:** English; Indigenous languages for territory and permission words.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** abuse and assault data; territorial knowledge, place names and sacred sites that peoples do not publish. Land, property and jurisdiction rules belong to Libra (letter) and Capricorn (spirit), not here.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Search the Inuit Circumpolar Council and Gwich'in sources for their statements on borders.
2. Gather public-cabin and wilderness etiquette pages and compare their written rules.
3. Search village council sites for visitor policies, noting who writes them.
4. Pull the sensitive victimization and violence-prevention reports with care, and read their method notes first.
5. Search Alaska newspapers for "trespass", "privacy" and "neighbors" letters to see how lines are argued.
