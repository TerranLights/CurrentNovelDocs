# Alaska: Fear (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America (pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada). **Language(s) for searching:** English; Indigenous languages of its lands (Yup'ik, Iñupiaq, Alutiiq, Unangam Tunuu, Dena'ina, Gwich'in, Tlingit and others); Russian; Tagalog; Korean; Spanish; French and Inuktitut on the Canadian side. **Concept in one line:** the emotion felt at danger, real or imagined, and a culture's ways of naming, showing, managing and using it, worry and anxiety included.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Native peoples and villages (relations to danger, ghosts and dark seasons differ by people), against cities; coastal hazard communities and Interior wildfire communities; fishing, aviation and oil-field workers; military families; Russian Orthodox communities; recent arrivals (often less trained for bears, cold and distance); Filipino, Korean and Latino households; Canadian-side northern towns.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on hazards and public messaging; mixed on anxiety measures; thin on folk fear, which sits in oral history and local storytelling.
- **Languages needed:** English; Indigenous languages for elders' accounts; Russian for church sources.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** stories of spirits and fear beings may be restricted by those who tell them; epidemic, nuclear and disaster memories involve living families; suicide and distress data are sensitive.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Pull the hazard histories and drill descriptions from the emergency and geophysical agencies.
2. Search oral-history archives for the 1964 earthquake and for epidemic memories, by community.
3. Compare bear-safety messaging with accounts from hunters and elders.
4. Search ANTHC and state public-health reports for anxiety and climate-worry measures.
5. Read Project Chariot and Amchitka histories for the nuclear-era fear told by Native voices first.
