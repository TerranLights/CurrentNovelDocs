# Alaska: Death (Scorpio): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

**Target:** Alaska, region North America; pre-war lands carried: USA, Canada. **Language(s) for searching:** English; Russian; Iñupiaq, Yup'ik, Alutiiq, Unangam Tunuu, Dena'ina and other Athabascan languages, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian; Spanish and Tagalog. **Concept in one line:** the end of a life, and how a culture understands dying, the dead and what death means (grief and mourning here; funeral practice sits in the domain sheet).

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Each Native people, kept separate (Iñupiat, Yup'ik and Cup'ik, Alutiiq, Unangax̂, Athabascan peoples, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian): their own beliefs about the dead and about dying.
- Russian Orthodox Natives, Old Believers, other Christians (Presbyterian, Catholic, Lutheran, Pentecostal, Latter-day Saint): different teaching on the dead.
- Village against city: a death in a village of a few hundred is known to everyone.
- Occupations with daily mortal risk: commercial fishing, bush aviation, mining, oil, military.
- Newer Filipino, Hmong, Samoan, Korean, Hispanic and Russian-speaking communities, and Canadian-side peoples carried in (check the tracker's territory entry).

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Rich on counts of death (health and safety agencies) and on oral history; thin on beliefs about death in plain survey form.
- **Languages needed:** English; Russian for church sources; Native languages for the best terms and for recorded elders.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** speaking the names of the dead; ghost and spirit teachings some peoples keep within the community; photographs of the dead; suicide and the families affected; mass graves from epidemics. Use what each people has published or approved.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Look up "die", "the dead" and "ghost" in the Alaska Native Language Center catalogs for each language, noting euphemisms.
2. Search Project Jukebox for "death", "funeral", "flu" and "ghost", and list which recordings are open.
3. Pull the vital-statistics and violent-death reporting pages for the causes, and see how Native health bodies break them down.
4. Read the Diocese of Alaska's published funeral and memorial material for the Orthodox view.
5. Search the Tundra Times and the Anchorage Daily News for obituaries and "in memory" notices, then compare wording across decades.
