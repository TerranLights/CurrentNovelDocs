## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Southeast clan peoples (Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian), where moiety shapes who partners with whom, against Dene, Yup'ik and Iñupiaq kin systems.
- Russian-descended Creole communities (Kodiak, Sitka) and Old Believer villages.
- Fishing, logging and mining crews, where "partner" is a work term.
- Road-system cities against off-road villages in couple formation.
- Military families and rotating workers; Filipino, Samoan, Korean, Latino and Hmong communities.
- Native corporations and tribes in joint ventures and compacts with agencies.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on marriage law, vital statistics and co-management agreements; mixed on Native marriage custom; thin on couples' daily division of labor for Alaska alone.
- **Languages needed:** English; Indigenous dictionaries; Russian for church records.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** clan rules and ceremonial marriage belong to each nation; use only what a people publishes. Church registers may be closed.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Read Alaska Statutes Title 25 on marriage, common-law status and customary adoption, and the Community Property Act.
2. Pull ACS marital-status and unmarried-partner tables for Alaska census areas.
3. Open the Whaling Commission and Bird Co-Management Council agreements.
4. Search Sealaska Heritage and the Alaska Native Language Center for "marriage" and moiety terms.
5. Ask the Alaska State Library what Russian-era and territorial marriage records it holds.
