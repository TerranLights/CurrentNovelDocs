## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Native nations: each holds its own account of past blame and of outsiders; do not merge them. Southeast, Yup'ik, Iñupiaq, Dene, Alutiiq, Unangax.
- "Locals" and "outsiders": long-term residents against newcomers and seasonal workers; the road system against the Bush.
- Subsistence, commercial and sport users in fish-and-game fights.
- Descendants of those evacuated in 1942: Aleuts and Japanese Americans.
- Russian-descended and Old Believer communities in their own image-making.
- Filipino, Samoan, Korean, Latino and Black communities, and military families, each blamed or praised in local talk.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for hearings, statutes and agency comment records; mixed for everyday blame and for conspiracy belief; thin for private projection in families.
- **Languages needed:** English; Indigenous dictionaries; Russian for church and older sources.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** beliefs about harm, ill will and healing belong to each nation and are often not published; use only what a nation shares. Hate-crime data involve living victims. Avoid treating any group's image of others as a national trait.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Read the 1945 Anti-Discrimination Act debate record and the Aleut Restitution Act hearings.
2. Search the Alaska State Library for newspapers that name "outsiders", "cheechakos" or groups blamed in a crisis.
3. Pull FBI hate-crime data and Human Rights Commission complaints for Alaska.
4. Read Board of Game public comments on predator control.
5. Ask each Native nation's culture body what it publishes on its own history of blame and outsiders.
