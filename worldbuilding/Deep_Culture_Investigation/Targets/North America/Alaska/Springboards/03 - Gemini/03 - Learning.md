*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Peoples whose elders have written down their teaching ways (Yup'ik and Cup'ik, Iñupiat, Koyukon and other Athabaskans, Tlingit, Haida, Alutiiq, Unangax); villages with immersion schools against those without; road-system districts against off-road ones; Anchorage's large, diverse schools; correspondence-school families; military families; fishing-season households with moving children; newcomer groups (Hmong, Filipino, Samoan, Korean, Hispanic); Yukon schools if carried.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on Native educators' writing and on test and graduation figures; mixed on informal learning at home; thin on newcomers.
- **Languages needed:** English; Native languages for program names; Russian for old mission schools.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** boarding-school accounts belong first to survivors and their nations. Student-level school data is private. Elders' teachings in print may be cleared only for named purposes. Disputes over school funding and curriculum are heated: record variation. Law goes to Libra and Capricorn.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Read the culturally responsive schools standards and note who wrote each section.
2. List immersion programs by language and village.
3. Pull state graduation and teacher-turnover data by district.
4. Search boarding-school testimony in the nations' own publications before outside reports.
5. Check Extension and 4-H records for learning-by-doing programs.
