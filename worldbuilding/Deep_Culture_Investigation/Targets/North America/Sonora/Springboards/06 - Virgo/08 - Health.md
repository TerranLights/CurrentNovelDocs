## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Yaqui, Mayo, O'odham, Seri, Guarijío and Cucapá communities with their own healing and health services; Mexican American families in Tucson who use both clinics and folk healers; ranching and farm-valley families; border-town residents who cross for care; migrants and the uninsured; Latter-day Saint colonies with church health teachings; urban professionals and retirees using private care; people who rely on pharmacies next to doctors' offices.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for surveys and clinical statistics; thin for folk and Indigenous healing; mixed for mental health.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish, English; Indigenous languages for healing terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** healing practice and knowledge belong to the nations; some health research has abused trust (see the Havasupai case), so ask tribal review boards. Mental illness, addiction and death are sensitive. The nation's exact lands are still to be settled.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Pull ENSANUT Sonora and BRFSS Arizona tables on self-rated health and care use.
2. Search "curandero," "sobador" and "partera" with Sonora for studies.
3. Read the tribal health departments' public pages for how they define wellness.
4. Search Teresa Urrea sources for how her healing is remembered.
5. Ask tribal review offices what health research is allowed before reading further.
