*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
Border-city residents (Nogales, Douglas/Agua Prieta, San Luis, Mexicali) against interior Sonoran and Arizonan communities; dual-citizen and single-citizen families; Mexican-heritage, Anglo and mixed families; Native nations divided by the border (Tohono O'odham, Cucapá, Kumeyaay), each in its own words; Yaqui and Mayo communities holding Catholic and older practice together, as shared; Mennonite and Mormon colonists living between countries; maquiladora workers and their managers; U.S.-raised returnees against those who stayed.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for border studies, identity polls and census items; mixed for how paired worldviews are held; thin for private two-facedness.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish, English.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** labels like "pocho" can wound; ceremonial pairings (religion, masks) belong to their communities. The nation's exact lands are still to be settled; this sheet is written for the Sonoran Desert core.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.

## 5. First queries
1. Read Martínez and Ríos together, noting what each says about living on both sides.
2. Pull ACS and INEGI identity and language tables for border counties and municipalities.
3. Search "doble moral" in Sonoran opinion columns for how it is used.
4. Ask tribal offices what they share about the border's effect on their people.
5. Search "Spicer Yaqui" for secondary readings and compare with the community's own materials.
