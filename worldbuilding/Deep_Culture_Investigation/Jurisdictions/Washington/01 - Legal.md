## 1. Part A: legal system

| Field | Washington |
|---|---|
| **Legal family** | **Common law** (Anglo-American), with **one civil-law transplant: community property** in marriage, from Spanish law by way of California (Family and property below), and a heavy layer of **direct legislation** since 1912 |
| **How it got there** | **Founders first.** The area north of the Columbia was part of Oregon's settler government and territory until Washington Territory was created in 1853, so Oregon's statute law came first [claim: from general knowledge, not checked]. **The 1889 constitution** was framed by 75 delegates at Olympia from July 4 to August 22, 1889, ratified on October 1, and took effect with statehood on November 11, 1889 [official: Washington State Legislature, the constitution's preface, *(read)*]. The Code Reviser's table of sources says provisions from **23 state constitutions** were copied into it; the main donors were **Lair Hill's model draft** (51 identical sections and 46 similar), the **California constitution of 1879** (45 and 45) and the **Oregon constitution of 1857** (23 and 37), with Wisconsin's next [official: Washington State Legislature, "Sources of the Constitution", *(read)*]. Hill was a Portland lawyer and former territorial judge commissioned by *The Oregonian*, and much of his draft came from Oregon, which had copied Indiana [scholarly: law-review summary, *(search summary)*]. So the chain of transplant runs **Indiana (1851) to Oregon (1857) to Hill (1889) to Washington**, with California supplying the corporations article (Article XII, mostly "identical" to California's) [official: Sources table, *(read)*]; the anti-monopoly sections (XII, 21-22) are marked "probably original" [official: same, *(read)*]. **Later layers:** the **Seventh Amendment (1912)** reserved the initiative and referendum to the people [practitioner: Ballotpedia, *(search summary)*]; the recall came in the same year [claim: from general knowledge, not checked] |
| **Precedent** | **Binding.** Supreme Court opinions "are then published and set precedent for subsequent cases" [official: Washington Courts, Supreme Court information sheet, *(read)*]. A 2007 study by the Supreme Court of California found the Washington Supreme Court's decisions the **second most widely followed** by other states' appellate courts from 1940 to 2005 [official: same sheet, citing that study, *(read)*] |
| **Main sources of law** | The constitution; the **Revised Code of Washington (RCW)**, much of it enacted by initiative; administrative rules (WAC); case law; county and city ordinances. Court procedure is set by **court rules adopted by the Supreme Court** (civil and criminal procedure, evidence) [official: Supreme Court information sheet, *(read)*] |
| **Trials** | **Adversarial.** Jury trial in criminal and civil cases; the constitution's jury clause (Article I, section 21) keeps the right "inviolate" and lets the legislature allow non-unanimous **civil** verdicts [claim: from general knowledge, not checked]. Criminal verdicts are unanimous, so Washington had nothing like Oregon's 1934-2020 rule [claim, not checked] |
| **Judges** | **Elected from practicing lawyers**, not a career judiciary. **Supreme Court:** nine justices, "nonpartisan", six-year terms; "elected by Washington voters, or appointed by the Governor when a vacancy occurs"; the court chooses its cases and hears petitions in departments of five [official: Supreme Court information sheet, *(read)*]. **Court of Appeals:** three divisions, judges elected to six-year terms; **superior courts** in each of the 39 counties, judges elected to four-year terms; **district and municipal courts** of limited jurisdiction [practitioner: encyclopedia summaries, *(search summary)*]. The Supreme Court also runs the court system, admits and disciplines lawyers, and has televised all its arguments since 1995 [official: information sheet, *(read)*] |
| **Constitutional review** | **The ordinary courts**, with the Supreme Court on top; no constitutional court. A party may go straight to the Supreme Court when a trial court holds a statute or ordinance unconstitutional [official: information sheet, *(read)*]. Like Oregon, Washington reads its own constitution independently where it differs: ***State v. Gunwall*** **(1986)** set six criteria for doing so, and **Article I, section 7** ("No person shall be disturbed in his private affairs, or his home invaded, without authority of law") is held broader than the Fourth Amendment, recognizing a right to privacy "with no express limitations" [official: rulings as summarized in case commentaries, *(search summary)*; the clause's wording from general knowledge]. Review has decided the state's largest questions: the income tax (1933, 2023), the death penalty (2018) and drug possession (2021) (Part B) |
| **Pluralism** | **Tribal law and courts** of the 29 federally recognized tribes. Washington took **partial** criminal and civil jurisdiction on reservations as an optional Public Law 280 state; the Tulalip Tribes were among the first three tribes (2014) to prosecute non-Native people for domestic violence under the Violence Against Women Act [practitioner and official: the Cascadia scope, section 1]. **Treaty rights limit state law** more here than in any other Cascadian state: the Stevens treaties (1854-1856) reserved fishing "at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations"; the **Boldt decision** (*United States v. Washington*, 1974) read "in common with" as an equal share, up to half the harvestable fish, with the tribes as co-managers; the U.S. Supreme Court upheld it in 1979, after years of state resistance; and the **culvert case** (Ninth Circuit 2016, affirmed 4-4 by the Supreme Court in 2018) held that state road culverts blocking salmon violate the treaties [practitioner: encyclopedia article, *(read)*]. The **Centennial Accord** (1989) set a government-to-government relationship [claim: the date from general knowledge]; since 2012 it is statute: RCW 43.376 requires agencies to "make reasonable efforts to collaborate with Indian tribes" and the Office of Indian Affairs to convene tribal leaders "annually to implement the Centennial Accords" [official: RCW 43.376, *(read)*; enacted 2012 c 122, last amended 2026 c 245]. Details belong to the T1 profile (job L6) |
| **Family and property law** | **Community property.** Washington's first community-property statute (**1869**, territorial) was modeled on an **1850 California statute**, and the system derives from **Spanish civil law**; why the territory borrowed it is not known, and Washington courts treat the Spanish antecedent as persuasive authority [scholarly: law-review summaries, *(search summary)*]. Under RCW 26.16.030 neither spouse may will away more than half the community property or give it away without the other's consent [official: the statute, *(search summary)*]. **Unmarried couples:** the courts extend an equitable division to a "committed intimate relationship", a "stable, marital-like relationship" (*Connell v. Francisco*, 1995) [official: the ruling as quoted, *(search summary)*]. On divorce all property, community and separate, is divided as is "just and equitable" (RCW 26.09.080) [claim: from general knowledge, not checked]. **Inheritance:** free testation over one's separate property and half the community; the surviving spouse keeps their own half [claim: the general rule, not checked]. **The contrast with Oregon** (separate property, equitable division, an elective share) is the sharpest Part A difference between the two states |
| **Levels** | **Federal:** the U.S. Constitution as a floor, federal crimes, the Controlled Substances Act, federal law on Native nations and the treaties (which limit the state directly, above), the U.S. District Courts for the Eastern and Western Districts and the **Ninth Circuit** (see the federal profile). **State:** almost all criminal law and sentencing, family and property law, growth management, taxation, and gun law, which the state **preempts**: RCW 9.41.290 bars local firearm rules stricter than state law, with narrow exceptions [practitioner: encyclopedia article, *(read)*]. **Counties and cities:** charters and ordinances, local minimum wages above the state's [official: Department of Labor and Industries, *(read)*]; elected **sheriffs** and county **prosecuting attorneys** [claim: from general knowledge, not checked]. **The voters:** initiatives to the people and to the legislature, used heavily (Part B) |

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading:** **left on social policy, personal conduct and guns; left on labor but long right of center on the tax base; libertarian on privacy and public records** [claim: this profile's reading of the rows above]. Caughey and Warshaw place Washington's policy **12th most liberal of 50 in 2014** (estimate 1.31, 95% interval 0.83 to 1.74), in the top 14 in every year checked from 1936, and statistically indistinguishable from Oregon (11th; the intervals overlap almost entirely) [measured: computed this session]. The two dimensions split: **9th on social policy, 22nd on economic policy** in 2014, against 3rd on economic policy in 1960 [measured: computed this session]. Three things set Washington apart: a **regressive revenue base** in a left-voting state, held in place for ninety years by a court ruling and the voters and only now changing (2021, 2026); **privacy and open government** written into its constitution (1889) and a 1972 initiative; and, in Cascadia's north, the **strictest gun laws actually in force**, alongside low incarceration and no death penalty. As in Oregon, much of this was made by initiative, which here pulled left on personal conduct and guns and right on taxes (and on punishment in 1993) [claim: this profile's reading].

**The author's reading:** none given for Washington (brief, 2026-11-03).

**Where the measures and the author agree or differ:** no author's reading to compare. **Between the measures:** all three economic measures put Washington well to the right of Oregon on the economy (Caughey and Warshaw 22nd against 10th; EFNA 32nd against 46th; Cato 37th against 46th overall), and close to it on social policy (9th against 14th). They agree in direction with each other and with the laws in each row. EFNA and Cato are think tanks with a declared orientation, tagged practitioner.

**Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The **Cascade crest** divides Washington's 19 **Left Coast** counties (Clallam, Clark, Cowlitz, Grays Harbor, Island, Jefferson, King, Kitsap, Lewis, Mason, Pacific, Pierce, San Juan, Skagit, Skamania, Snohomish, Thurston, Wahkiakum, Whatcom) from its 20 **Far West** counties (Adams, Asotin, Benton, Chelan, Columbia, Douglas, Ferry, Franklin, Garfield, Grant, Kittitas, Klickitat, Lincoln, Okanogan, Pend Oreille, Spokane, Stevens, Walla Walla, Whitman, Yakima) (`Data/Unit_Territories.md`). Because the state **preempts local gun law and sets growth rules statewide**, county-level variation shows up mainly in **votes and enforcement**, not in local ordinances:
- **Votes on state law.** On Initiative 2117 (2024, repeal of the climate law), King County voted 74.76% no, Snohomish 60.71% and Pierce 57.94% (Left Coast), while Benton voted 54.51% yes and Spokane 46.09% yes (Far West) [practitioner: encyclopedia article citing county results, *(read)*]. The same pattern, a Puget Sound majority outvoting the interior, recurs on the tax and gun initiatives [claim: not checked county by county].
- **Sheriffs.** At least 20 county sheriffs said they would not enforce parts of I-1639 (2018) [practitioner: encyclopedia article, *(read)*]; which counties was not checked here.
- **Planning.** Under the Growth Management Act 28 counties plan fully and 11 only partly [practitioner: MRSC, *(read)*]. The partial-planning counties are, from general knowledge, rural counties on **both** sides of the crest (Adams, Asotin, Klickitat, Lincoln, Okanogan and Pend Oreille in the Far West; Cowlitz, Grays Harbor, Pacific, Skamania and Wahkiakum in the Left Coast) [claim, not checked]. So, as with Oregon's gun ordinances, the legal seam runs between Puget Sound and the rural west as well as along the crest.
- **Cities.** Several cities set minimum wages above the state's [official: Department of Labor and Industries, *(read)*], Seattle among them [claim: the city list not checked]; Seattle's combined sales tax is the state's highest (10.55%, October 2026) [practitioner: encyclopedia article, *(read)*].
- **Treaty country.** The Boldt decision and the culvert case bind the state across western Washington's salmon country, and treaty fishing rights also reach the Columbia and its tributaries [practitioner: encyclopedia article, *(read)*; the Columbia cases not checked]; reservations hold partial state jurisdiction under Public Law 280. Tribal law is the T1 profile's subject (job L6).
- **Woodard's reading of the seam:** the Left Coast "battles constantly against the libertarian-corporate agenda" of the Far West [synthesis: Woodard, as quoted in the Cascadia scope, section 4]. The county votes fit it; the state's own preemption and statewide planning mean the Left Coast majority usually sets the law the Far West lives under.
