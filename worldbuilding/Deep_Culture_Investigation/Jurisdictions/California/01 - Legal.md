## 1. Part A: legal system

| Field | California |
|---|---|
| **Legal family** | **Common law, of the American state branch, written largely into codes:** "a common-law state with codes." The base rule is statutory: "The common law of England, so far as it is not repugnant to or inconsistent with the Constitution of the United States, or the Constitution or laws of this State, is the rule of decision in all the courts of this State" (Civil Code § 22.2, first enacted April 13, 1850) [official: code text, search summary]. **A civil-law residue survives in three places:** marital property (community property, from Spanish and Mexican law), water (pueblo rights) and land titles (the Spanish and Mexican grants) [official and scholarly, below]. Comparative classifications list the United States as common law with Louisiana as its mixed jurisdiction; California is not classed as mixed [claim: JuriGlobe and the CIA World Factbook, not consulted]. |
| **How it got there** | **Founders' layer, 1769-1846: Castilian law, then Mexican law.** Alta California was governed under the Spanish colonial legal order (the Castilian tradition carried to the Indies), then under Mexican law after 1821 [scholarly: Robbins Collection, UC Berkeley, "California's Legal Heritage" exhibit (read)]. Ordinary disputes were heard by the *alcalde*, who was both mayor and judge; the American military governors left the alcalde system in place after 1846 until the legislature created district, county and sessions courts on March 30, 1850 [practitioner and scholarly: county court histories and Wikipedia's "Alcalde," search summary]. **The break, 1849-1850.** The Monterey convention of 1849 debated keeping "civil law principles inherited from Spain and Mexico, which offered community property rights not conferred by the common law" [scholarly: Robbins Collection (read)], and in the end kept only that: the 1849 constitution made a wife's property owned before marriage or received by gift, devise or descent "her separate property" and ordered laws defining her rights in property "held in common with her husband" (Art. XI, § 14) [official: 1849 constitution, text via search summary]. The delegates who carried it were mostly Northeasterners (Dimmick and Tefft of New York, Norton of Vermont), keeping rights the Californianas already held under Spanish and Mexican law; Dimmick said the separate-property rule "has been found to work well here," and the Virginian Charles Botts led the opposition [scholarly: Erika Pérez, *The Panorama* (SHEAR), 2024 (read)]. The legislature then adopted the common law (April 13, 1850) and an act defining the rights of husband and wife (April 17, 1850) [official: search summary]. The state's courts soon read the 1850 statute as adopting "the common law of England, not the civil law ... nor the Mexican law" [official: an early California Supreme Court opinion quoted in a search summary; case name not identified]. **Titles from the founders' era** were sorted out by the federal Land Act of 1851, which required every holder of a Spanish or Mexican grant to prove it before a commission within two years; Mexican law governed rights that predated the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo [practitioner: Wikipedia, "California Land Act of 1851," search summary]. **The New York layer, 1851-1872.** Procedure came first: the Practice Act of 1851, drafted with Stephen J. Field's help, was based on New York's Field Code of Civil Procedure; in 1872 California enacted four codes (Civil, Code of Civil Procedure, Penal and Political) built on David Dudley Field's New York drafts, most of which New York itself never enacted; only California, North Dakota, South Dakota and Montana enacted "virtually all" of Field's civil code [practitioner: Wikipedia, "California Codes" and "California Civil Code," search summary]. **The second constitution** (1879) replaced the first and is still in force, heavily amended [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. **The Progressive layer, 1911:** see "Main sources of law." |
| **Precedent** | **Binding,** in the common-law way. Civil Code § 22.2 makes the common law the rule of decision where no statute or constitution speaks, and the state Supreme Court has read "common law" there to mean the whole body of English jurisprudence as of 1850, equity included (a 1917 ruling) [official: search summary of § 22.2 commentary]. The codes are construed by courts as continuations of the common law rather than as civilian codes, which is what makes California "a common-law state with codes" [synthesis: this file's reading of the above; claim]. Trial courts are bound by every published appellate decision [claim: *Auto Equity Sales v. Superior Court* (1962), not consulted]. |
| **Main sources of law** | **1.** The U.S. Constitution and federal law (see the federal profile). **2.** The state constitution of 1879, amendable by the legislature with voter approval, by convention, or **by the voters' own initiative** [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. **3.** Statutes, organized in the codes. **4.** **Initiative statutes and constitutional amendments, the referendum and the recall,** adopted at a special election on October 10, 1911, under Governor Hiram Johnson, together with women's suffrage; the voters approved what Ballotpedia and others call the most expansive initiative and recall powers in the nation [official: Secretary of State's 2011 centennial release; practitioner: Ballotpedia, KQED (search summary)]. Initiatives have made some of the state's most consequential law, in both directions: Proposition 13 (1978) on taxes; three-strikes, Propositions 47 and 36 on sentencing; Proposition 64 on cannabis (Part B). Caughey and Warshaw's estimates suggest direct democracy pushes states' **cultural** policy to the right (about 0.6 standard deviations, "perhaps implausibly large"), with little net effect on **economic** policy, citing California's 1978 "tax revolt" as a case where it enabled more conservative policy [scholarly: Caughey and Warshaw, *Dynamic Democracy* (2022), p. 157 (read)]. **5.** Case law, and the common law through § 22.2. **6.** Regulations of state agencies; county and city ordinances and charters. |
| **Trials** | **Adversarial,** with juries in criminal and civil cases and professional judges; no lay judges [claim: general knowledge]. Civil verdicts may be by three-fourths of the jury, criminal verdicts must be unanimous (Constitution, Art. I, § 16) [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. Every county also has a civil grand jury that investigates its local governments [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. |
| **Judges** | **Appointed and then retained for the appellate courts; elected (in practice, mostly appointed first) for the trial courts.** The system has the superior courts of the 58 counties, six districts of the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court. Supreme Court and Court of Appeal justices are nominated by the governor, vetted by the State Bar's Commission on Judicial Nominees Evaluation, confirmed by the Commission on Judicial Appointments (the Chief Justice, the Attorney General and a presiding justice), and then stand in yes-or-no **retention elections**, serving 12-year terms. Superior court judges are elected by county voters on a nonpartisan ballot for six-year terms; vacancies are filled by the governor, and "the vast majority of superior court judges initially reach the bench via gubernatorial appointment." Every judge must have been a California lawyer or judge for at least 10 years: a bench drawn from practicing lawyers, not a career judiciary [official: Judicial Council newsroom, "Judicial Selection" (read)]. The appellate system dates from a 1934 constitutional amendment [practitioner: Ballotpedia, search summary]. The 1849 constitution had made Supreme Court justices elected by the voters for six-year terms [official: 1849 text, search summary]. Retention has bitten once at scale: Chief Justice Rose Bird and two associate justices were voted off the court in 1986, chiefly over the death penalty [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. |
| **Constitutional review** | **By the ordinary courts,** with the California Supreme Court as the last word on the state constitution (and the U.S. Supreme Court above it on federal questions). There is no separate constitutional court [claim: general knowledge]. **The voters' power to amend is itself reviewed:** an initiative may *amend* the constitution but not *revise* it. In *Raven v. Deukmejian* (1990) the court struck part of Proposition 115 as an impermissible "qualitative" revision; in *Strauss v. Horton* (2009) it upheld Proposition 8 as an amendment, using both quantitative and qualitative tests [official: the opinions, via Justia and SCOCAL summaries (search summary)]. The state constitution is often read more broadly than the federal one (privacy, speech; Part B) [claim, with cases in Part B]. |
| **Pluralism** | **Tribal law:** about 109 federally recognized tribes; California is a mandatory Public Law 280 state, so the state holds criminal jurisdiction on reservations, with tribal jurisdiction concurrent in many matters (scope, section 1; profiled as group T3) [practitioner: search summaries, via the scope]. **Founders'-era property law:** pueblo water rights (below) and land titles from the Spanish and Mexican grants. **Local home rule:** charter cities control their "municipal affairs" [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. **No religious courts or religious personal law** [claim: general knowledge]. |
| **Family and property law** | **Community property,** the founders' legal inheritance: all property acquired by a married person during the marriage while domiciled in California is community property (Family Code § 760), and property acquired elsewhere that would have been community property here is **quasi-community property**, divided equally on divorce [official: code text, search summary]. **Inheritance:** free testation (no forced heirship) [claim: general knowledge]; in intestacy the surviving spouse takes the decedent's half of the community and quasi-community property (Probate Code § 6401), so the whole community passes to the spouse [official: code text, search summary]. **Divorce:** the Family Law Act, signed by Governor Ronald Reagan on September 5, 1969, made California the first U.S. state to drop marital fault as the ground for divorce [practitioner and scholarly: search summary, including a Golden Gate University law review note and an Assembly Judiciary report]. **Water**, a property right of its own: a **dual system**, riparian rights (adopted with statehood in 1850) and appropriative rights ("first in time, first in right," from the miners' posted notices; recognized by the legislature a year later), reconciled by a constitutional requirement that every use be "reasonable and beneficial"; the 1913 Water Commission Act created the permit system and the agency that became the State Water Board; pueblo rights "based on Spanish and Mexican law" survive (Los Angeles); groundwater has no state permit system and has been governed by "reasonable use" since *Katz v. Walkinshaw* (1903) [official: State Water Resources Control Board, "History of the Water Boards" (read)]. When Los Angeles was incorporated in 1850 it succeeded to "all the rights, claims, and powers of the Pueblo de Los Angeles in regard to property" [official: the 1850 incorporation act, via search summary]. |
| **Levels** | **Federal** (the federal profile): the U.S. Constitution and Bill of Rights, federal crimes, immigration, the scheduling of drugs (cannabis is still federally illegal), securities, most labor-relations law, federal law on Native nations, and the Ninth Circuit, whose rulings on gun and speech law bind California (Part B). **State:** almost all criminal law, policing and sentencing, family, property, contract and tort law, elections, schools and most regulation. **Counties (58):** each elects its sheriff and district attorney and has its own superior court; counties run jails, probation and much of welfare [claim: general knowledge for the last clause]. **Cities:** zoning and local police; the state has been taking back land-use power through housing law since 2017 (Part B). Variation by county is therefore built into enforcement (section 3). |

## 3. Part B: the overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading** [synthesis: this file's summary of the table; not a measure]. **Left in every domain on the direction of its law, and split on the second axis:** authoritarian on the economy, business regulation, guns, public health and gambling; libertarian on sex, marriage, reproduction, cannabis, speech (in its constitution) and self-defense; libertarian-trending on punishment for a decade (2011-2020) and then partly reversed by the voters (2022-2024); in land use, moving from local restriction to state-mandated building. **Two lawmakers, not one:** the legislature (a Democratic supermajority [claim: general knowledge, not checked]) sits further left than the voters, who make law directly by initiative and have repeatedly pulled back on taxes (1978), gig work (2020), rent control (2018, 2020, 2024), the minimum wage (2024), the death penalty (2016) and theft and drug sentences (2024), while moving left on cannabis, abortion and marriage. Caughey and Warshaw's finding that direct democracy pushes cultural policy to the right [scholarly, read] fits the punitive and some of the moral votes, but not the cannabis, abortion and marriage votes, which went the other way. **On the measures,** California is near the left and the restrictive end but **not at the extreme** of any of them: Caughey and Warshaw's liberal anchor is New York; EFNA has three states below it; Cato has two; BJS has 15 states with lower imprisonment rates; Cato's personal freedom puts it in the middle (26th).

**The author's reading:** "California tends to skew Hard-Left" (author, 2026-11-02), read on the left-right axis only (author, 2026-11-10). The second axis is read from the laws, as above.

No overall measure used here scores California as the single most left-wing or most restrictive state; in one domain, guns, Giffords ranks it first.

**What the table shows** [synthesis: this file's reading of the counts]:
- **The seam that matters most runs between the Far West and the two coastal units** (the scope's seams 1 and 3). The Far West votes against gun control, against repealing the death penalty and against a higher minimum wage by wide margins, and splits on cannabis and same-sex marriage; it is the part of California where the author's "Hard-Left" fits least. Its large metropolitan counties (Sacramento, San Joaquin, San Bernardino) vote with the state, so the seam is also urban against rural inside the unit. This fits Woodard's line that the Left Coast "battles constantly against the libertarian-corporate agenda" of the Far West (scope, section 4) [synthesis: Woodard via the scope].
- **The Left Coast is the most left and the most libertarian on personal conduct and punishment** (death-penalty repeal 57.2%, parole 73.0%, marriage 73.4%), and the most supportive of gun control (72.7%). Its North Coast and rural edges (Del Norte above all; also Napa, San Benito, Solano on the death penalty) vote like the Far West.
- **El Norte sits close to the state average** on almost everything, which is expected: its eight counties hold just over half of the state's people (20.7 of 39.6 million in the 2020 base; scope, section 3). Inside it, Los Angeles and Santa Barbara lean with the Left Coast, Orange and Riverside lean toward the Far West, and **Imperial County** (agricultural, on the Mexican border) voted for the higher minimum wage (55.5%) but against adult-use cannabis (45.3%) and same-sex marriage (48.7%): left on economics and traditional on morals in the same electorate [measured, read].
- **A shared floor across all three units:** the voters everywhere rejected wider rent control (2024) and passed tougher theft and drug penalties (2024). On these two, the seams vanish.

**Local officials whose enforcement differs from Sacramento's:**
- **District attorneys (the decarceral wave and its reversal).** San Francisco (Left Coast) recalled District Attorney Chesa Boudin on June 7, 2022, 55.0% to 45.0%; Brooke Jenkins was appointed in his place [practitioner: Wikipedia, read]. Alameda County (Left Coast) recalled District Attorney Pamela Price, in office since January 3, 2023, on November 5, 2024, with 62.9% [practitioner: Wikipedia, read]. Los Angeles County (El Norte): George Gascón took office on December 7, 2020, announced on his first day that he would not seek cash bail for some minor offenses or "never seek the death penalty", survived two recall drives (the 2021 drive fell short of signatures; in 2022, 195,783 signatures were found invalid), and lost his re-election in November 2024 to Nathan Hochman [practitioner: Wikipedia, read; Hochman ran as an independent per general knowledge, while the summary read calls him a Republican; not resolved]. The two recalls were in the Left Coast, the unit with the most left-leaning electorate in the table, which is a reminder that the unit is not one voice.
- **Sheriffs.** Before *Bruen*, concealed-carry licensing depended on the sheriff: Sacramento and Fresno (Far West) issued permits readily, while Los Angeles and San Francisco (El Norte, Left Coast) were "presumptively *No Issue*" [practitioner: Wikipedia, "Gun laws in California," read]. Riverside County's sheriff Chad Bianco (El Norte; in office since January 7, 2019) said in 2020 that he would not enforce the state's COVID-19 orders, backed Proposition 36, appeared in 2025 for a bill to weaken the state's sanctuary law, and ran for governor in 2026 (10.2% in the primary) [practitioner: Wikipedia, read].
- **Cities.** **Huntington Beach** (Orange County, El Norte) contested its state-assigned share of 13,368 homes for 2021-2029 and stopped processing accessory-dwelling applications; the state sued in March 2023 and won in the Court of Appeal on September 12, 2025, and the city's federal suit was dismissed (November 15, 2023; rehearing denied April 21, 2025) [practitioner: Wikipedia, read]; its voters also adopted a local voter-identification rule in 2024 that the state challenged [claim: general knowledge, not checked]. **Cannabis:** 80% of municipalities banned adult-use stores as of 2019, so the cannabis map is patchwork at the city level [practitioner, read]; which units hold the bans was not mapped this session. **Rent control** exists only where cities adopted it within Costa-Hawkins's limits (for example San Francisco, Berkeley, Oakland, Los Angeles, Santa Monica) [claim: general knowledge, not checked]; most of those cities are in the Left Coast and El Norte.
- **Tribal lands.** On the reservations and rancherias of about 109 tribes (units I3, I4 and I5), state criminal law applies under Public Law 280, but gaming runs under tribal-state compacts and the tribes hold a monopoly on casino gaming (Part B, personal conduct); their own legal orders are profiled as group T3 [practitioner: via the scope].
