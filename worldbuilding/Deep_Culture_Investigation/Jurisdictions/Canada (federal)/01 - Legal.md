## 1. Part A: legal system

**Legal family.** Common law in every province and territory except Quebec, whose private law is civil law, codified in the Civil Code of Québec, while its public and criminal law are common law like the rest of Canada's [claim: general knowledge, not consulted]. JuriGlobe (University of Ottawa) lists "CANADA (minus QUEBEC)" among the common-law monosystems and Quebec among the "mixed systems of civil law and common law" [scholarly: JuriGlobe, read]. Federal statutes have to work in both private-law systems at once (federal bijuralism) [claim, not consulted]. Over both sit the constitutionally protected Aboriginal and treaty rights of section 35 and the partly recognized Indigenous legal orders (see **Pluralism**).

**How it got there (founders first).**
- **The founding layer: English law, received colony by colony, not at Confederation.** Each colony took English law as it stood on a fixed date: Ontario 15 October 1792; the three Prairie provinces 15 July 1870, the day the Hudson's Bay Company's lands passed to Canada; British Columbia 19 November 1858, the day the mainland colony was proclaimed [practitioner: reception-statute summaries, search summary]. British Columbia's own statute was read: the civil and criminal laws of England "as they existed on November 19, 1858" are in force "so far as they are not from local circumstances inapplicable" [official: Law and Equity Act, RSBC 1996, c. 253, s. 2, read]. So the common-law founding came through the British colonial state and, in the West, the Hudson's Bay Company, which governed the land before the colonies [synthesis, from the reception dates].
- **The exception: French civil law in Quebec,** kept after the British conquest by the Quebec Act (1774) [claim, not consulted]; noted here only as the exception the brief asks for.
- **The federal frame (1867).** A British statute, the British North America Act (now the Constitution Act, 1867), created a Westminster federation and divided powers between Parliament (s. 91) and the provinces (s. 92) [official, read]. Final appeals went to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London until 1949, when the Supreme Court of Canada became the final court and grew to nine judges [practitioner: search summary].
- **Later layers.** Patriation in 1982 added the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and section 35 [official: Constitution Act, 1982, read]; the Charter moved constitutional review from federalism disputes toward rights review [synthesis]. Since 2019-2021, a further layer: statutes that recognize Indigenous law or adopt the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (see **Pluralism**).

**Precedent.** Binding: lower courts follow higher courts, and the Supreme Court binds every court [claim, not consulted]. The Supreme Court has allowed trial courts to revisit its precedents when a new legal issue is raised or the circumstances or evidence have changed fundamentally (*Canada v. Bedford*, 2013; *Carter v. Canada*, 2015) [claim, not consulted]. In Quebec's civil law the Code comes first and case law is in theory persuasive only, though followed in practice [claim, not consulted].

**Main sources of law.** The Constitution (the Acts of 1867 and 1982, with their amendments); federal and provincial statutes; regulations and orders in council (the 2020 firearms prohibition was made by order in council: see Part B); case law; the Civil Code of Québec for Quebec's private law; treaties and land-claims agreements, which section 35 protects [official: Constitution Act, 1982, s. 35, read; the rest claim, not consulted].

**Trials.** Adversarial. The Charter gives the accused "the benefit of trial by jury where the maximum punishment for the offence is imprisonment for five years or a more severe punishment" [official: Charter s. 11(f), read]. Most criminal cases are tried by a judge alone in the provincial courts [claim, not consulted]. Parliament abolished peremptory challenges of jurors (Bill C-75, in force September 19, 2019), a change that followed the 2018 Stanley trial, and the Supreme Court upheld it in *R. v. Chouhan*, 2021 SCC 26 [official, via search summary]. Civil juries are a provincial matter (see the British Columbia profile).

**Judges.** Appointed from practicing lawyers; there is no career judiciary [claim, not consulted]. Two appointing authorities:
- **Federal:** "The Governor General shall appoint the Judges of the Superior, District, and County Courts in each Province" [official: Constitution Act, 1867, s. 96, read], in practice the federal cabinet on the Minister of Justice's recommendation, after screening by 17 Judicial Advisory Committees (one or more per province and territory) run by the Office of the Commissioner for Federal Judicial Affairs, set up in 1978; the process was reformed in October 2016 [official: Justice Canada and Commissioner pages, search summary]. Parliament may set up a "General Court of Appeal for Canada" and other federal courts [official: s. 101, read]: the Supreme Court, the Federal Court and the Federal Court of Appeal, and the Tax Court [claim, not consulted]. Supreme Court judges are picked by the Prime Minister from candidates proposed by an Independent Advisory Board (since 2016) [official, search summary]; three of the nine must come from Quebec (Supreme Court Act, s. 6) [practitioner: search summary].
- **Provincial:** each province constitutes and staffs its own provincial courts [official: s. 92(14), read]; their judges are appointed by the province (for British Columbia, see that profile).
- **Tenure:** superior-court judges hold office "during good behaviour", are removable only "on address of the Senate and House of Commons", and retire at 75 [official: s. 99, read].

**Constitutional review.** Diffuse, by the ordinary courts, with no separate constitutional court: any superior court can find a law inconsistent with the Constitution and of no force (Constitution Act, 1982, s. 52(1)) [official, not consulted for the text], and the Supreme Court is final. Two Canadian features:
- **Reference questions.** The federal cabinet can put questions of law straight to the Supreme Court (Supreme Court Act, s. 53) [practitioner: search summary], and provinces can refer questions to their courts of appeal [claim, not consulted]. Examples: the Secession Reference (1998) [claim, not consulted]; the carbon-pricing references, upheld in *References re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act*, 2021 SCC 11 [practitioner: search summary].
- **Limits and override.** Charter rights are subject to "such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society" [official: s. 1, read]. Parliament or a legislature may declare that a law operates "notwithstanding a provision included in section 2 or sections 7 to 15" of the Charter, for five years at a time, renewable [official: s. 33(1) and (3), read]. The override does not reach democratic, mobility or language rights, or section 35 [synthesis: from the text of s. 33(1)]. Its use has been provincial (Quebec most of all; also Saskatchewan, Ontario and Alberta) [claim, not consulted].

**Pluralism.**
- **Quebec's civil law** (above).
- **Indigenous peoples: the constitutional frame.** Parliament holds "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians" [official: Constitution Act, 1867, s. 91(24), read]. "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed", the Aboriginal peoples being "the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples" [official: Constitution Act, 1982, s. 35(1)-(2), read]. Section 35 sits outside the Charter, so the notwithstanding clause cannot override it [synthesis].
- **The Indian Act (1876; now R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5)** [claim, not consulted]: the federal statute that defines registered First Nations status, constitutes bands and their councils, holds reserve land, and gives band councils limited bylaw powers [claim, not consulted]. It is the default legal setting for most First Nations, including the roughly 200 bands of British Columbia (Cascadia scope, group T5). Nations leave parts of it through modern treaties, self-government statutes and agreements, or court-declared or negotiated Aboriginal title (scope groups T6 and T7). **Summarized here only as the frame for job L7,** which profiles those orders.
- **The federal UNDRIP Act** (United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, S.C. 2021, c. 14), assented to June 21, 2021: it affirms the Declaration "as a universal international human rights instrument with application in Canadian law"; the government "must, in consultation and cooperation with Indigenous peoples, take all measures necessary to ensure that the laws of Canada are consistent with the Declaration"; and an action plan was due within two years [official: ss. 4-6, read]. Subsection 2(2), the Act's own clause saying it upholds and does not derogate from section 35 rights, was repealed by S.C. 2024, c. 30, s. 35 [official, read]. That 2024 statute appears to be the one that moved a single non-derogation clause into the Interpretation Act [claim, not checked]. The Act is a framework: unlike British Columbia's courts with that province's act (see the BC profile), no federal court ruling giving it direct legal effect was found in this job [claim: nothing found, not a full search].
- **Indigenous law with the force of federal law.** The 2019 federal child-welfare act (An Act respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children, youth and families) lets Indigenous governing bodies' child and family laws prevail over provincial law, and the Supreme Court upheld it in 2024 (2024 SCC 5) [claim, not consulted].
- **In sentencing,** courts must pay particular attention to the circumstances of Indigenous offenders and consider alternatives to prison (Criminal Code s. 718.2(e); *R. v. Gladue*, 1999) [claim, not consulted]. British Columbia's Indigenous sentencing courts build on this (BC profile).
- **Military justice** under the National Defence Act [claim, not consulted].

**Family and property law.**
- **Federal:** capacity to marry and divorce: "Marriage and Divorce" [official: s. 91(26), read]. Under it, the Divorce Act (1985, amended in 2021 around the child's best interests and family violence) [claim, not consulted] and the Civil Marriage Act (2005), which opened civil marriage to same-sex couples nationwide [claim, not consulted].
- **Provincial:** "The Solemnization of Marriage in the Province" and "Property and Civil Rights in the Province" [official: s. 92(12)-(13), read]. So the marital property regime, wills, intestate succession and land law are provincial: equal division of family property in British Columbia (BC profile); the family patrimony of the Civil Code in Quebec [claim, not consulted].
- **On reserves** the provincial family-property laws did not apply to reserve land, a gap Parliament filled in 2013 with the Family Homes on Reserves and Matrimonial Interests or Rights Act [claim, not consulted].

## 2. Part B: legal orientation, by domain

**What this layer covers.** Only what the federal government sets. Where a domain is set by the provinces (or the municipalities under them), the row says so and names who sets it, as an honest null, and the provincial reading is in the province's profile. **The positions are this job's reading of the laws and measures** [synthesis], placed against a North American frame, since the Cascadia pilot's other jurisdictions are U.S. and Mexican; the same law can read differently against a European frame. Left against right is about whom the law favors (redistribution, collective provision, protected groups, against markets, owners, tradition); libertarian against authoritarian is how far the law reaches into the area and how hard it is enforced.

**Measures available at this level.** *Economic Freedom of North America* (EFNA, Fraser Institute; free-market) has no separate federal score: its "all-government" index scores each province on federal and provincial policy together, so federal policy shows only as a floor shared by all ten provinces [practitioner: search summaries]. In the 2025 edition (data for 2023), Alberta, the highest province, tied for 30th among the 50 states and 10 provinces, British Columbia was 47th, and seven provinces ranked below every U.S. state [practitioner: Fraser Institute via news summaries, search summary]. Statistics Canada's corrections and police-reported crime tables are used in the policing row [measured].

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading** [synthesis]. In a North American frame the federal layer is **center-left**, and it is not one thing on the second axis:
- **libertarian on the body and private life:** abortion without a criminal law, one of the broadest assisted-dying regimes, legal cannabis, same-sex marriage, no death penalty;
- **authoritarian on weapons:** licensing, registration of restricted arms, the 2020 prohibition and the 2022 handgun freeze;
- **moderately authoritarian on hateful speech,** and more so since July 2026;
- **center on markets,** with the welfare state's floor (health transfers and equalization) set federally and most economic regulation left to the provinces;
- **in policing and punishment,** a rehabilitative and decarceral direction to 2022 (mandatory minimums repealed, Gladue sentencing), then a turn toward tougher bail and sentencing in 2023-2026;
- **in government reach,** growing security powers, checked by courts that have twice found the 2022 emergency declaration unreasonable.

Over 2024-2026 the movement on most rows is the same way: toward the right on carbon pricing and on bail and sentencing, and toward restriction on MAID's expansion, drug possession and hate speech. Guns are the exception: tighter since 2020, with the Supreme Court case pending.

**The author's reading:** none given for Canada. The author's examples (2026-11-02) are U.S. states only. Nothing is supplied here in its place.

**Where the measures and the author agree or differ:** nothing to compare. EFNA (practitioner, free-market) is the only cross-jurisdiction orientation measure that covers Canada, and it covers the economy only.

**Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The federal layer is uniform by design (one Criminal Code, one Charter), but the provinces carry most of the enforcement, and the results differ widely:
- **Legal family:** Quebec's civil law against common law everywhere else (the Quebec target).
- **Incarceration,** provincial and territorial custody per 100,000 adults, 2023/24 [measured: Statistics Canada, table 35-10-0154-01]: **British Columbia 39.71** (the lowest), Nova Scotia 62.14, Quebec 67.81, New Brunswick 69.92, Ontario 73.26, Newfoundland and Labrador 73.29, Prince Edward Island 79.97, Alberta 91.05, Manitoba 174.59, Saskatchewan 239.00; Yukon 106.40, Northwest Territories 244.57, Nunavut 470.72.
- **Police-reported crime,** Crime Severity Index, 2025 [measured: Statistics Canada, table 35-10-0026-01]: Canada 75.01; Ontario 57.17, Quebec 61.55, Alberta 90.62, **British Columbia 92.37**, Manitoba 130.04, Saskatchewan 146.64.
- **Policing:** Ontario and Quebec keep provincial police forces; the other provinces contract the RCMP as provincial police, with municipal forces in some cities [claim, not consulted; British Columbia's mix is in its profile].
- **The notwithstanding clause** has been used by provinces (Quebec most), not recently by Parliament [claim, not consulted]; British Columbia has not used it (BC profile).
- **Economic freedom** (EFNA 2025, data for 2023, all-government): Alberta 30th, British Columbia 47th, then Manitoba 54th, Saskatchewan 55th, Quebec 56th, New Brunswick 57th, Prince Edward Island and Nova Scotia 58th, Newfoundland and Labrador 60th, among the 50 states and 10 provinces [practitioner: Fraser Institute, via The Hub, read]. Ontario's rank was not in the summary read.
- **Drug policy:** only British Columbia received a federal exemption for decriminalized possession (2023-2026) [official, search summary].
- **Indigenous orders:** band governments under the Indian Act, modern-treaty nations, self-governing nations and title holders sit inside every province; in Cascadia these are British Columbia's groups T5-T7 (job L7).

**For Cascadia's seams** [synthesis]. On seam 5 (the 49th parallel and the water line), the Left Coast's U.S. lobe and the British Columbia coast share common law, but this federal layer differs from the U.S. federal layer exactly where the U.S. leaves things to the states: criminal law here is national, and guns, abortion, the death penalty and assisted dying are settled nationally, not county by county. Inside British Columbia, the coast against the interior is the seam this layer does *not* create: the federal law is the same on both sides (see the BC profile for where the province and its municipalities differ).
