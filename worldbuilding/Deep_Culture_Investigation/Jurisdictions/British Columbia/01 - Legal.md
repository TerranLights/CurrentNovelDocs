## 1. Part A: legal system

**Legal family.** English common law, under the Canadian federal layer; no civil-law element [scholarly: JuriGlobe lists "CANADA (minus QUEBEC)" as a common-law monosystem, read]. Alongside it, the constitutionally protected rights and the partly recognized legal orders of the province's First Nations (see **Pluralism**).

**How it got there (founders first).**
- **The founding: the Hudson's Bay Company and the British colonial state.** Fort Victoria (1843), the Colony of Vancouver Island (1849, granted to the company) and the Colony of British Columbia, proclaimed November 19, 1858 in answer to the Fraser River gold rush [practitioner: Cascadia scope, section 2, search summary].
- **Reception of English law on the colony's founding day.** "The Civil and Criminal Laws of England, as they existed on November 19, 1858, so far as they are not from local circumstances inapplicable, are in force in British Columbia" [official: Law and Equity Act, RSBC 1996, c. 253, s. 2, read]. The brief's "English Law Act" is the older name of this rule; the present statute is the Law and Equity Act [claim: the older name not checked]. A 1897 statutory review was meant to settle which English statutes applied, yet courts later revived "ancient English statutes" left out of it, and the reception doctrine has been used against Indigenous claims: in one student's reading, the Supreme Court of Canada "has used the doctrine of reception to deny Indigenous nations ownership and jurisdiction over fisheries in rivers that flow through their reserves" [practitioner: Matheson, Allard School of Law blog, 2021, read].
- **Into Canada:** the two colonies united in 1866 and the province joined Confederation on July 20, 1871 [claim, not consulted]. From then on criminal law, marriage and divorce, and "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians" were federal (Canada profile).
- **Bearing on the scope's disagreement 3** (Woodard reads the coast as an extension of the Left Coast; Canadian sources give it a British colonial founding): **the legal record follows the British colonial founding.** The province's law begins on the day the colony was proclaimed, and its institutions (Westminster government, appointed judges, English received law) came from the colonial state, not from settlers out of Oregon or California [synthesis]. This says nothing about where the population came from, which is Woodard's point; both readings stand, and the author's decision 2 (the coast as its own unit) already took the Canadian side.
- **Later layers:** the Charter (1982); the province's Human Rights Code [claim, not consulted]; the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (2019) and the court ruling that gave it force (2025; see **Pluralism**).

**Government.** Westminster: a unicameral Legislative Assembly of 93 members, a premier and cabinet responsible to it, and a Lieutenant Governor [claim: seat count from 2024 election summaries, search summary; the rest not consulted]. The October 19, 2024 election returned the New Democratic Party with 47 seats, the Conservatives with 44 and the Greens with 2 [practitioner: news summaries, search summary]. British Columbia alone among the provinces has citizen-initiated recall and legislative initiative (the Recall and Initiative Act, 1995) [claim, not consulted].

**Precedent.** Binding vertically: the Court of Appeal for British Columbia binds the province's trial courts, and the Supreme Court of Canada binds all [claim, not consulted].

**Main sources of law.** Provincial statutes (consolidated as the Revised Statutes of British Columbia, 1996, plus later annual statutes) and regulations; case law; the federal layer; the received English law of 1858 where nothing replaced it; and, since 2025, the UN Declaration as an aid to interpreting provincial law and a standard courts may test it against (see **Pluralism**) [official: statutes read as cited; the rest synthesis].

**Trials.** Adversarial. Criminal juries follow the federal rules (Canada profile). Civil jury trials are available in the Supreme Court of British Columbia [claim, not consulted].

**Courts and judges.**
- **Court of Appeal for British Columbia** and **Supreme Court of British Columbia** (the superior trial court): judges appointed federally under s. 96, through the federal Judicial Advisory Committees, to serve to 75 (Canada profile) [official: Constitution Act, 1867, ss. 96 and 99, read].
- **Provincial Court of British Columbia** (most criminal cases, small claims, family and youth matters) [claim, not consulted]: judges and judicial justices are appointed by the Lieutenant Governor in Council (the cabinet) on the recommendation of the Judicial Council of British Columbia after a merit-based process. The council (nine members, from the public, the bar and the bench) screens and interviews applicants, with investigations by the Canadian Bar Association's BC branch and the Law Society, and the Attorney General recommends to cabinet from the candidates it approves [official: Provincial Court pages and the Provincial Court Act, search summary].
- **Appointed from the bar, not a career judiciary** (both levels) [synthesis from the above].

**Constitutional review.** By the ordinary courts, as everywhere in Canada; the province can refer questions to its Court of Appeal [claim, not consulted]. British Columbia has not used the notwithstanding clause [claim, not consulted]. **A provincial form of review peculiar to British Columbia:** under the province's UNDRIP statute, courts may now test provincial laws against the UN Declaration (*Gitxaala*, below).

**Pluralism.**
- **The Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act** (DRIPA, SBC 2019, c. 44), the first provincial statute to adopt the UN Declaration [claim: "first" not checked here]: "the government must take all measures necessary to ensure the laws of British Columbia are consistent with the Declaration", "in consultation and cooperation with the Indigenous peoples in British Columbia" (s. 3); ministers may make agreements with Indigenous governing bodies (s. 6); and cabinet may authorize agreements for **joint statutory decision-making** with an Indigenous governing body, or for decisions that **require its consent** (s. 7), effective on publication in the Gazette [official: DRIPA, read; current to October 27, 2026, so no amendment is in force].
- ***Gitxaala v. British Columbia (Chief Gold Commissioner)*, 2025 BCCA 430** (December 5, 2025, 2-1): reversing the 2023 trial ruling, the majority held that DRIPA "incorporates UNDRIP into the positive law of British Columbia with immediate legal effect", that related rights can be litigated, and that the province's mineral-claims regime is inconsistent with Article 32(2) of the Declaration [practitioner: law-firm summaries, search summary; encyclopedia, read]. Premier Eby called amending DRIPA "non-negotiable"; after opposition from the First Nations Leadership Council and others, the government said by April 2026 it would not bring in DRIPA legislation that session [practitioner: news and encyclopedia, read and search summary]. The province appealed, and the Supreme Court of Canada granted leave on May 21, 2026 [practitioner: encyclopedia, read]. **The disagreement stands open:** the province's position is that the ruling created legal uncertainty; the First Nations organizations' is that DRIPA is now doing what it was passed to do [practitioner: as above].
- **Indigenous sentencing courts** in the Provincial Court: the first, the New Westminster First Nations Court, opened in November 2006; there are now ten, including a new one in Chilliwack (the others in North Vancouver, Kamloops, Duncan, the Nicola Valley, Prince George, Williams Lake, Hazelton and Lillooet). They hold no trials: they are for people who identify as Indigenous and intend to plead guilty, and they build a **healing plan** with Elders, the Crown, defense counsel and community agencies, which can include traditional and therapeutic measures [official: Provincial Court pages, search summary].
- **First Nations' own legal orders:** about 200 band governments under the Indian Act (T5), eight modern-treaty nations with three more coming (T6), and sectoral self-government and Aboriginal title (T7: shíshálh, Westbank, Tsilhqot'in, Haida) [official, via the Cascadia scope, section 1]. A Provincial Court notice sets where prosecutions under First Nation agreements and treaties are heard [official: notice title only, search result]. **Profiled in job L7.**
- ***Cowichan Tribes v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2025 BCSC 1490** (August 7, 2025, after a trial begun in 2014): Aboriginal title declared over part of the claimed area in Richmond, **including land held in fee simple** by Canada and the City of Richmond, and over submerged lands before the old village site; Canada and British Columbia have appealed [practitioner: law-firm and news summaries, search summary]. It is the first such declaration over privately titled urban land found in this job [claim: from the summaries' description, not checked against the ruling].

**Family and property law.**
- **Family property: the Family Law Act** (SBC 2011, c. 25; in force March 18, 2013) [official: the Act's table of contents, read; date: search summary]. Spouses are presumptively entitled to an equal share of family property and family debt (s. 81, "Equal entitlement and responsibility"); "excluded property" (s. 85) covers property brought into the relationship, gifts and inheritances, some settlements and trust property, but its growth in value during the relationship is shared. **Unmarried partners count as spouses for property after two years in a "marriage-like relationship"** (s. 3) [practitioner: law-firm summaries, search summary].
- **Inheritance: the Wills, Estates and Succession Act** (WESA) [claim: in force 2014, not consulted]. **Testamentary freedom is limited:** under s. 60 a court may vary a will that does not make adequate provision for the will-maker's spouse (married or marriage-like) or children, including adult children [practitioner: search summary]. On intestacy a spouse whose children are all the deceased's takes a preferential share of $300,000, and the rest divides equally between the spouse and the children [practitioner: search summary].
- **Land:** a Torrens-type title registry (the Land Title Act) [claim, not consulted]; the Agricultural Land Reserve (1973) limits farmland conversion [claim, not consulted]; and, over both, Aboriginal title where declared or agreed (above).

**Levels: what British Columbia sets, and what it can't.**
- **Sets** [official: Constitution Act, 1867, s. 92, read; the examples synthesis]: property and civil rights (family property, succession, land, tenancy, contracts, labor outside federal sectors); the organization of the courts, the prosecution of most criminal offenses, and policing (the Police Act); provincial offenses; health-care delivery within the Canada Health Act's conditions; taxes within the province; securities regulation.
- **Delegates to municipalities:** zoning, building and local bylaws, and the choice to run a municipal police force (see Part B) [claim, not consulted, except as shown in Part B].
- **Can't set:** criminal law (drugs and guns included), capacity to marry and divorce, First Nations status (registration under the Indian Act) and reserve lands (all federal; Canada profile). It can ask Ottawa for exemptions, as with the 2023-2026 drug decriminalization (Part B).

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading** [synthesis]. A **center-left** province inside a center-left federation, with three features of its own:
- **Personal conduct swung further than anywhere else in Canada, in both directions.** British Columbia ran the country's only decriminalization of drug possession (2023), then narrowed it (public use recriminalized in May 2024), let it lapse (January 2026) and widened involuntary care.
- **Housing is the most interventionist domain:** taxes on vacancy, speculation and flipping, a rent cap tied to inflation, and provincial override of municipal zoning. It is left on whom the law favors, and authoritarian toward municipalities and owners of empty homes while deregulating what builders may put on a lot.
- **Decarceral in outcomes but weak in clearance:** the lowest incarceration rate in Canada, alongside a crime-severity index above the national figure and a clearance rate well below it.

On government reach the province leaned toward the executive in 2025 (Bill 7's cabinet powers, Bills 14 and 15's fast-tracking). The legislature pulled part of it back (Part 4 of Bill 7 withdrawn), and the courts and First Nations constrained it (*Gitxaala*; the opposition to amending DRIPA). Since 2024 most domains have moved the same way as the federal layer: right on carbon pricing, toward enforcement on drugs.

**The author's reading:** none given for British Columbia. The author's examples (2026-11-02) are U.S. states only. Nothing is supplied in its place.

**Where the measures and the readings agree or differ.** With no author's reading, the comparison here is between the one measure and the laws:
- **EFNA** (practitioner, free-market) puts British Columbia **second among the provinces** for economic freedom, while the province's labor and housing laws read **center-left**.
- Both can be true in their own frames: EFNA's all-government index carries the federal layer common to every province, and ranks provinces against each other and against the states, while this profile reads the provincial statutes against the U.S. and Mexican jurisdictions of the pilot.
- **Recorded, not resolved.** Caughey and Warshaw, the registry's academic measure of policy liberalism, covers U.S. states only, so there is no academic measure to check EFNA against for this province.

**Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The coast is the S2 British Columbia coast unit and the I1 Northwest Coast peoples' homelands; the interior is the S3 Far West's British Columbia lobe and the I2 Plateau and I6 Dene peoples' homelands; the far north is in no unit (`Data/Unit_Territories.md`).
- **Provincial politics: coast against interior.** In the October 2024 election the NDP took most seats in the Lower Mainland and on Vancouver Island, and the Conservatives dominated the interior and the north, with suburban gains in the Lower Mainland, including Surrey and Langley [scholarly: Resnick, *The Conversation*, read; practitioner: news, search summary]. Resnick ties the NDP side to gun control, environmental protection, Indigenous reconciliation and pandemic policy, and the Conservative side to housing affordability, the health system's failures, the toxic-drug crisis, free enterprise and "unabashed resource development" [scholarly: Resnick, read]. **The coast is not uniform:** the Conservatives' largest gains on the coast came in the Surrey and Langley suburbs [practitioner: news, search summary].
- **Policing: municipal forces on the coast, the RCMP almost everywhere else.** Of the 12 municipal police services, 11 are in the coast unit's census divisions: Abbotsford (Fraser Valley); Central Saanich, Oak Bay, Saanich, and Victoria with Esquimalt (Capital); Delta, New Westminster, Port Moody, Surrey, Vancouver and West Vancouver (Greater Vancouver). The 12th, Nelson, is the only one in the interior [synthesis: the province's list placed on the unit territories]. The interior's cities (Kelowna, Kamloops, Prince George) and the rest of the coast are policed by the RCMP, under municipal contracts or as the provincial police [synthesis: from their absence from the list]. So the coast's two largest metropolitan areas mix municipal police boards with RCMP detachments, while the interior is policed almost entirely by a federal force on contract [synthesis].
- **Crime and clearance by metropolitan area, 2025** [measured: Statistics Canada, table 35-10-0026-01]. The split is not clean:

  | Metropolitan area | Unit | Crime Severity Index | Weighted clearance rate |

  The two core coastal metros have the lowest severity, and the Fraser Valley and interior cities higher. Vancouver has the lowest clearance rate in the province [measured; reading synthesis].
- **Indigenous sentencing courts** sit on both sides. Four are in interior census divisions: Kamloops, the Nicola Valley, Prince George and Williams Lake. Six are in the coast unit's: New Westminster, North Vancouver, Duncan and Chilliwack, and also Hazelton and Lillooet, which are inland towns in the two census divisions that straddle the coast and the interior (Kitimat-Stikine and Squamish-Lillooet) [synthesis: court list placed on the unit territories].
- **Treaty, self-government and title.** The modern-treaty nations are all on the coast side: Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, the Maa-nulth nations, Tla'amin, K'ómoks, and Kitselas and Kitsumkalum to come. So are shíshálh and the Haida title lands. Westbank (Okanagan) and the Tsilhqot'in title area (Cariboo) are in the interior. The *Cowichan* title claim is in Richmond, inside Metro Vancouver [official: the scope's list; claim: the locations from general knowledge, not checked].
- **Municipal politics.** In 2023, during the drug pilot, local governments raised concerns about drug use in child-focused public spaces, which led to the 15-meter buffer and then the provincial and federal restrictions [practitioner: UBCM, read]. Local governments also opposed the 2025 fast-track bill [practitioner: news, search summary]. Vancouver sought its own decriminalization exemption before the provincial one [claim, not consulted]. **Thin:** this job found no measure of municipal ordinances by city, and the brief's "Vancouver against the interior cities" contrast rests on the election and policing evidence above, not on a municipal-law dataset.
- **The far north** (Stikine and Northern Rockies, 5,161 people in 2021): no unit; RCMP policing [measured: population, from `Data/Unit_Territories.md`; policing synthesis: no municipal force there].
