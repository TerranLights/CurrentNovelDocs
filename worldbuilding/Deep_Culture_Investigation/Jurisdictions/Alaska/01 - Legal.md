## 1. Part A: legal system

**Legal family.** Common law, as a state of the U.S. federal union. The reception statute: "So much of the common law not inconsistent with" the two constitutions or state statutes "is the rule of decision in this state" (AS 01.10.010) [official: Alaska Statutes, read].

**How it got there (founders first, then later layers).**
- **Before the state.** Russian America (1799-1867) left no lasting legal layer that this research found [claim: honest null; not researched further]. Under U.S. rule the territory long had a thin federal legal presence [claim, not consulted], and before the turn of the century miners' courts acted on their own: an estimated seven executions under miners' courts before 1899, and eight legal executions between 1900 and 1957 [practitioner: Death Penalty Information Center, Alaska page, read]. The territorial legislature abolished the death penalty in 1957, two years before statehood [practitioner: DPIC, read].
- **The founding: the 1956 constitution.** Ratified by the people on April 24, 1956, and operative with statehood on January 3, 1959 [official: constitution, read]. Its design is the founders' template for the state's law: a unified court system (Art. IV, § 1), merit selection of judges (Art. IV, §§ 5-8), boroughs instead of counties (Art. X), the common use of fish, wildlife and waters (Art. VIII, § 3), and the initiative, referendum and recall (Art. XI) [official: read]. That the framers drew on the National Municipal League's model state constitution and the Missouri Plan is general knowledge [claim, not consulted].
- **Later layers:** the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (1971, below); the 1972 amendments (the right of privacy, § 22, and the civil-rights clause, § 3); the 1976 Permanent Fund amendment (Art. IX, § 15); the 1994 amendments on arms (§ 19) and criminal administration (§ 12); the 1998 marriage amendment (§ 25) [official: constitution with amendment years, read]; ANILCA (1980) and the subsistence conflict (below); voter-made law: marijuana (2014), elections (2020).

**Precedent.** Binding. The Alaska Supreme Court is final on state law, including the state constitution, which it reads independently of the federal one: *Ravin v. State* (1975) found that the state's privacy right covers an adult's possession of a small amount of marijuana at home, the first such ruling by any state or federal court [official: ruling, search summary]. Federal questions arising in Alaska go to the U.S. District Court for Alaska and the Ninth Circuit (federal profile) [official: CRS R48242, read].

**Main sources of law.** The constitution, the Alaska Statutes, regulations, case law, and voter initiatives. The initiative may not "dedicate revenues, make or repeal appropriations, create courts, define the jurisdiction of courts or prescribe their rules" (Art. XI, § 7) [official: read]. All elected officials except judges are subject to recall (Art. XI, § 8) [official: read].

**Trials.** Adversarial. A criminal jury "of twelve", with six to twelve allowed in courts not of record (Art. I, § 11); a civil jury of twelve where more than $250 is at stake, with verdicts by not less than three-fourths allowed by law (Art. I, § 16) [official: read].

**Judges: merit selection with retention elections** (the brief's "check": confirmed).
- **Courts.** "The courts shall constitute a unified judicial system for operation and administration" (Art. IV, § 1) [official: read]: a supreme court, a court of appeals, the superior court (general trial court) and district courts, with magistrates [claim: the court list beyond the constitution's text, not checked]. The constitution set three justices, increasable by law at the court's request (Art. IV, § 2) [official: read]; there are now five [claim, not checked].
- **Selection.** The governor fills a vacancy "by appointing one of two or more persons nominated by the judicial council" (Art. IV, § 5). The Judicial Council has seven members: three attorneys appointed by the Bar's governing body, three non-attorneys appointed by the governor and confirmed by the legislature, and the chief justice as chair; members are appointed "without regard to political affiliation" (Art. IV, § 8) [official: read]. The chief justice votes only when it could change the result [official: Alaska Judicial Council, *Twenty-Eighth Report*, 2015-2016, read].
- **Retention.** A judge faces a yes-or-no nonpartisan vote at the first general election more than three years after appointment, then every tenth year for supreme court justices and every sixth for superior court judges (Art. IV, § 6) [official: read]; the court of appeals and district court terms are set by statute (eight and four years) [claim: from a search summary, not checked]. Since a 1975 statute the Judicial Council evaluates every judge on the ballot and recommends a yes or no vote [official: *Twenty-Eighth Report*, read]. In 2016 voters retained all 33 judges on the ballot, with yes votes from 57.5% to 76.1% [official: same, read].

**Constitutional review.** By the ordinary courts, as in the federal system. Alaska's rights clauses are in places wider than the federal ones: the express privacy right ("The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed", Art. I, § 22, 1972) [official: read], which the state supreme court has read to protect home marijuana use (*Ravin*, 1975) [official, search summary] and abortion (*Valley Hospital Association v. Mat-Su Coalition for Choice*, 1997, still in force after *Dobbs*) [practitioner: Wikipedia, "Abortion in Alaska", read].

**Pluralism: tribes, Native corporations and subsistence.**
- **Many tribes, little Native land in the federal legal sense.** Alaska holds 229 of the country's federally recognized tribes [practitioner: news on HB 123, search summary]. The 1971 settlement (ANCSA) declared all aboriginal titles and claims of title in Alaska, "including any aboriginal hunting or fishing rights that may exist", to be "hereby extinguished" (43 U.S.C. § 1603) [official: read], and Congress meant it to work "without establishing any permanent racially defined institutions" and "without creating a reservation system or lengthy wardship or trusteeship" (§ 1601) [official: read]. The land went to Native corporations, not to tribes (Sealaska in the Southeast) [practitioner: via the Cascadia scope]. In *Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government* (February 25, 1998; 9-0, Thomas) the Supreme Court held that about 1.8 million acres of former reservation land the tribe held under ANCSA is not "Indian country" (the federal statutes' term for Native lands under federal and tribal jurisdiction), because it was neither set apart by the federal government for Native use nor under federal superintendence [official: ruling via Cornell LII, read]. Tribal authority in Alaska therefore rests mainly on membership, not territory: tribal courts hear members' matters such as child custody (*John v. Baker*, Alaska 1999) [official: ruling, not consulted]; the Central Council of the Tlingit & Haida runs a regional tribal court in Juneau [practitioner: via the Cascadia scope, search summary].
- **The exception: Metlakatla.** The Annette Islands Reserve, Alaska's one reservation, is outside Public Law 280's grant of criminal jurisdiction to the state: the Metlakatla Indian Community "may exercise jurisdiction over offenses committed by Indians" there [official: 18 U.S.C. § 1162, read].
- **Public Law 280 is mandatory here** (the brief's "check": confirmed, with a nuance). It was extended to "all Indian country within the Territory of Alaska" in 1958 (Pub. L. 85-615); the 1970 amendment (Pub. L. 91-523) restated it for the state with the excepted area [official: § 1162 amendment notes, read].
- **State recognition of tribes (2022;** "check": confirmed). Governor Dunleavy signed HB 123 on July 28, 2022, recognizing the federally recognized tribes in state law [practitioner: Ballotpedia News, KTOO, search summary]. The Alaska Federation of Natives said the statute "does not impact the existing legal status of Alaska Tribes" or the state's authority; Native leaders had gathered signatures for a ballot measure that the act made unnecessary [practitioner: KTOO, July 28, 2022, read].
- **Federal tribal jurisdiction over non-Native people.** The 2022 VAWA Alaska Pilot Program lets up to five Alaska tribes a year, 30 in all, exercise special criminal jurisdiction over non-Native people for covered crimes in their villages, which are not Native lands in the federal sense; no designations were listed as of August 9, 2024 [official: Justice Department, read].
- **Subsistence: two orders on one land.** The state constitution reserves fish, wildlife and waters "to the people for common use" (Art. VIII, § 3) and requires sustained yield (§ 4) [official: read]; the state statute gives subsistence uses priority outside "nonsubsistence areas" (AS 16.05.258) [official: read]. Federal law (ANILCA, 1980) gives *rural* residents a priority on federal lands; the Alaska Supreme Court held a state rural priority unconstitutional under the common-use clause (*McDowell v. State*, 1989), so the federal government has managed subsistence on federal lands since about 1990, extended to many navigable waters by the Ninth Circuit's *Katie John* rulings [official: rulings and statute, not consulted].
- **The constitution's silence.** The state constitution, as transcribed, does not use the word "Native" anywhere [official: text search of the Wikisource transcription, read]; Native rights in Alaska rest on federal law, state statute and the courts.

**Family and property law.**
- **Marital property: separate property with equitable division,** plus an optional community-property regime (the brief's "check": confirmed). On divorce the court divides property "acquired only during marriage, in a just manner and without regard to which of the parties is in fault", and may reach premarital property when equity requires (AS 25.24.160(a)(4)) [official: read]. Spouses may instead make property community property by a community property agreement or trust (AS 34.77.030, .060) [official: read]; the Alaska Community Property Act dates from 1998 and was the first opt-in system [practitioner: legal-guide sites, search summary].
- **Inheritance:** a Uniform Probate Code-style intestacy scheme (for example, the surviving spouse takes the whole estate when all the decedent's descendants are also the spouse's: AS 13.12.102) [official: read; the Uniform Probate Code attribution a claim, not checked].
- **Marriage:** the constitution still says a marriage "may exist only between one man and one woman" (Art. I, § 25, 1998) [official: read]; it is unenforceable since a 2014 federal district ruling and *Obergefell* (2015) [official: rulings, not consulted].
- **Land and resources:** 60.8% of the state is federal land (222.4 million acres, September 30, 2024) [official: CRS R42346, read]; the rest is mostly state land and ANCSA corporation land, with little private land outside them [claim: shares not checked]. At least 25% of mineral lease rentals, royalties and bonuses go to the Permanent Fund, whose principal may be used only for income-producing investments (Art. IX, § 15, 1976) [official: read].

**Levels: who sets what inside Alaska.**
- **The state** sets criminal law and procedure, family, property and most of everything else in Part B. **Prosecution is a state function:** the attorney general heads the Department of Law (AS 44.23.010) and is to "prosecute all cases involving violation of state law" (AS 44.23.020(b)(4)) [official: read]; with no counties there are no county sheriffs or elected county prosecutors [claim: inference from the statute and Art. X, not checked].
- **Local government:** "maximum local self-government with a minimum of local government units" (Art. X, § 1); all local powers are vested in boroughs and cities (§ 2); the whole state is divided into boroughs, "organized or unorganized" (§ 3) [official: read]; the areas outside organized boroughs form a single unorganized borough (AS 29.03.010) [official: read]. The Census Bureau counts 19 boroughs (including the municipalities of Anchorage and Skagway and the city-boroughs) and divides the unorganized borough into 11 census areas [measured: Census Bureau, Vintage 2024 county estimates, computed here].
- **Local powers that matter for law:** municipalities and "established villages" may vote to ban the sale, the importation, or the sale, importation and possession of alcohol (local option, AS 04.11.491) [official: read]; local gun ordinances are preempted (AS 29.35.145) [official: read].
- **The federal layer** weighs more here than in most states: federal land, ANCSA, ANILCA and federal law on Native nations (see the federal profile).

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading.** On left against right, Alaska's law sits **in the middle of the states**, having drifted there from the more liberal third: Caughey and Warshaw's overall policy liberalism ranks it 16th of 50 in 1960, 17th in 1980, 16th in 2000, 23rd in 2010 and 25th in 2014 [measured: CSPP v2.6, computed here]. On libertarian against authoritarian it is **libertarian toward its residents** (no income tax, a dividend instead of a tax bill, an express privacy right, home marijuana since 1975, permitless carry, no death penalty since 1957) **while the state itself is large and oil-funded**, and much of its most restrictive law is **chosen locally** (alcohol bans by community vote) or **set federally** (land, subsistence, federal law on Native nations) [synthesis: from the rows above]. By domain: economy, center with low taxes and high public spending; property and land, federally dominated; personal conduct, libertarian at state level and restrictive by local option; guns, strongly libertarian; speech and religion, the federal floor; punishment, mixed (no death penalty, high confinement, harsher sentencing since 2019); government reach, a large state with strong privacy and direct democracy.

**The author's reading:** none given for Alaska (brief, 2026-11-03). Recorded as none; no reading has been invented.

**Where the measures and the author's reading agree or differ.** No author's reading, so no comparison. **The measures disagree with each other,** and the disagreement is recorded, not resolved:
- **Cato ranks Alaska 15th freest overall** (2023 edition), up from 41st in 2007, crediting fiscal policy (falling state tax collections) and personal freedom (legal marijuana, low drug arrests, homeschooling, gun rights) [practitioner: Cato via Must Read Alaska, read; libertarian think tank]. **Fraser ranks Alaska 43rd of 51** on state-and-local economic freedom in 2023, because it scores government spending and government employment, where Alaska is near the bottom [practitioner: EFNA 2025, read; free-market think tank]. The two free-market measures part because Cato weighs what residents pay and may do, Fraser how large the public sector is, whoever pays for it [synthesis]. Caughey and Warshaw's academic measure sits between them: the middle of the states (25th of 50, 2014) [measured].
- **Incarceration:** Alaska's sentenced-prisoner rate is well below the national rate (202 against 360 per 100,000, 2023) [measured: BJS, search summary], but its all-confinement rate is above it (744 against 614) [practitioner: Prison Policy Initiative, read]. Alaska's single jail-and-prison system holds pretrial detainees and short sentences in state facilities, which the two counts treat differently [synthesis; the BJS note on unified systems from a search summary].

**Project canon and this research.** The project's `Alaska Republic - National Character Profile.md` says nothing about law, so nothing here conflicts with it. Two figures are recorded beside it: (1) canon's "Alaska minus the Panhandle is 668,504"; the Vintage 2024 estimate used here gives 669,058, a difference of vintage, not of substance [measured: Census Bureau, computed here]; (2) the DCI frameworks index reads Alaska as Indigenous-majority (Cascadia scope, method note 8), but Native people (the census category "American Indian and Alaska Native", alone or in combination) are 20.3% of the state, and a majority only in the rural areas outside the Panhandle and the Railbelt (55.4%) [measured: Census Bureau, Vintage 2024, computed here].

**Variation inside the jurisdiction.** Alaska has no counties, a single statewide prosecutor's office and no sheriffs, so local differences in enforcement run through municipal codes, borough powers, the alcohol local option, tribal courts and who polices rural places, not through elected county officials [synthesis: from Part A].
