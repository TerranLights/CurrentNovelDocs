## 1. Part A: legal system

- **Legal family.** Mexican civil law (see the federal profile), applied through the state's own constitution and codes (civil, penal, civil procedure) [synthesis; the texts were not read, so their dates and content are not given here].

- **How it got there.**
  - **As a federal territory (1824-1974), under the capital's law.** The peninsula was one Territory of Baja California from 1824; it was divided in 1930, and the South Territory took its present boundaries in 1931 [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia, "Baja California Sur", read]. The federal civil codes of 1870 and 1884 were enacted for the Federal District and the Territory of Baja California, and the 1928 code for the District and the Federal Territories [practitioner: Brostoff, read; official: Código Civil Federal reform register, read]. So for 150 years the south lived under federal-territory law made in Mexico City, longer than any other part of Cascadia's Mexican lobe [synthesis].
  - **Statehood.** Admitted as a free and sovereign state on **October 8, 1974**, with Quintana Roo, the 31st state [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia, read; English Wikipedia, "Governor of Baja California Sur", read]. The date of its first constitution was not verified (1975 is from general knowledge) [claim, not consulted].
  - **Founders and law.** The scope records the Jesuit mission province from 1697 (Loreto) and its ranching descendants as Sudcalifornia's founders (scope, section 2). No source read here ties the state's law to that founding; its codes descend, like Baja California's, from the federal-territory line [synthesis].
  - **Political layer.** The PRI governed from statehood until 1999; Leonel Cota Montaño (PRD, 1999-2005) was the first non-PRI governor, followed by Narciso Agúndez Montaño (PRD, 2005-2011), Marcos Covarrubias Villaseñor (PAN, 2011-2015), Carlos Mendoza Davis (PAN, 2015-2021) and Víctor Manuel Castro Cosío (Morena, 2021-) [practitioner: English Wikipedia, read].

- **Precedent.** Bound by federal *jurisprudencia* and by the SCJN's precedents (six votes), as every state [official: federal Constitution, Art. 94, read]. State-level precedent not checked [honest null].

- **Main sources of law.** The federal constitution and laws; the state constitution, codes and laws, including a **Ley de Derechos de las Personas, Pueblos, Comunidades Indígenas y Afromexicanas del Estado de Baja California Sur** [practitioner: Justia Mexico, via the scope's search summary; its date and content not read]; municipal regulations.

- **Trials.** Criminal: accusatory and oral under the national code (2014) [official: federal profile]. Civil and family: the national code (2023) must be in force by April 1, 2027; the state's schedule was not checked [honest null]. No juries [synthesis].

- **Judges.** The federal constitution requires state judges to be elected (Art. 116, 2024) [official: federal profile]. Whether Baja California Sur held a local judicial election in 2025 was not checked. Under the federal decree of June 2, 2026, every local judgeship not renewed in 2025 is to be filled in the coincident federal and local judicial election of the first Sunday of June 2028 [official: federal Constitution, decree of DOF 02-06-2026, transitional arts. 2-3, read], so the state's judges are elected no later than 2028 [synthesis].

- **Constitutional review.** By the federal courts (amparo; the SCJN's actions and controversies) [official: federal profile]. State-level procedures not checked [honest null].

- **Pluralism.** No native Indigenous people survives in the state (scope, section 5), so the Indigenous order here is that of **resident migrant communities**: 14,676 speakers of Indigenous languages in 2020 (1.7%), mostly from southern Mexico [measured: INEGI 2020, via the scope's search summary]. The state's rights law covers **Afro-Mexican** people as well as Indigenous ones [practitioner: law title, via the scope]; the federal constitution has recognized Afro-Mexican peoples and communities as subjects of public law since 2024 [official: federal Constitution, Art. 2, read]. *Ejidos* and *comunidades* are under federal agrarian law [official: federal profile]. Job L7 profiles the communities' own order.

- **Family and property law.** The state's civil code was not read [honest null on the marital property regime and inheritance rules]. What is known from other sources:
  - **Same-sex marriage:** approved by the state Congress on June 27, 2019 (14 to 5, one abstention), in force June 29, 2019 [practitioner: English Wikipedia, "Same-sex marriage in Mexico", read], two years before Baja California's legislation.
  - **Land:** federal Art. 27 governs title, *ejidos* and the restricted zone; the whole state is a peninsula, so most of it, and all of its coast, lies inside the 50 km coastal band closed to direct foreign ownership [official: federal Constitution, Art. 27, read; synthesis for the geography].

- **Levels.** As in the federal profile. The state sets its constitution, substantive civil, family and penal law, the state police and prosecutor, state taxes and its judiciary; the five municipalities set zoning and local regulations [synthesis, from the federal Constitution, Arts. 115, 116 and 124, read].

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

- **Overall reading** [synthesis; **low confidence**, because the state's own codes were not read]. Inside the federal frame (statist left on the economy; authoritarian on guns, policing and government reach), Baja California Sur's own law is **left-libertarian on marriage and reproduction** (same-sex marriage in 2019, two years before Baja California; abortion to 12 weeks in 2022) and **mid-table for Mexico on economic freedom**. It records **lower victimization than Baja California and the national average** and, in 2025, the lowest perception of insecurity in the country, though both measures moved the wrong way in the latest survey. If one label is needed: **socially liberal, economically middling for Mexico, comparatively safe by the measures, inside an authoritarian federal security frame**.
- **The author's reading:** none given for Baja California Sur (brief, 2026-11-03). Nothing is supplied in its place.
- **Where the measures and the author's reading agree or differ:** honest null. Between the measures: the ENVIPE's low victimization and the WJP's strong 2022-2023 ranking agree; the sources disagree on the WJP's 2023-2024 result (Part B table).
- **Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The whole state is one unit (**S5 Sudcalifornia**). Its five municipalities: Comondú, Mulegé and La Paz (all three 1972), Los Cabos (1981) and Loreto (1992) [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia, read]; populations are INEGI 2020 as recorded in the scope [measured]. Municipal law (zoning, alcohol, policing) was not researched, so the differences below are of setting, not of law [honest null on municipal law]:
  - **Los Cabos** (351,111), now larger than the capital: the tourist economy (scope), where the federal restricted-zone rules on foreign ownership bear hardest [synthesis].
  - **La Paz** (292,241): the capital and the seat of the state powers [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia, read].
  - **Mulegé** (64,022; seat Santa Rosalía, the French copper-works town named in the scope) and **Comondú** (73,021; seat Ciudad Constitución): the northern and central *municipios* [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia for the seats, read]; their economies and local law were not researched here [honest null].
  - **Loreto** (18,052): the 1697 Jesuit founding (scope), the oldest European settlement in Cascadia.
  - **Across the 28th parallel** (seam 4): compared with Baja California, a lower EFNA score (4.32 against 5.46), lower victimization (19,505 against 23,095 per 100,000), an earlier same-sex marriage law (2019 against 2021) and a later abortion reform (2022 against 2021) [practitioner and measured, read; search summary for the abortion vote].
