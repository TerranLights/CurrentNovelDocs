## 1. Part A: legal system

- **Legal family.** Mexican civil law (see the federal profile), applied through the state's own codes: the **Código Civil para el Estado de Baja California** (published January 31, 1974) [official, read], the **Código Penal** (August 20, 1989) [official, read], a code of civil procedure now being replaced by the national one, and the **Ley de la Familia** (December 30, 2011), a family-protection law rather than a family code [official, read].

- **How it got there.**
  - **As a federal territory (1824-1952), under the capital's law.** The Republic's first civil codes, of 1870 and 1884, were enacted "for the Federal District and the Territory of Baja California" [practitioner: Brostoff, Law Library of Congress, read; scholarly: search summary], and the 1928 code was written "for the District and the Federal Territories" [official: Código Civil Federal reform register, read]. So the peninsula's private law came from Mexico City, not from its own founders [synthesis].
  - **Statehood.** The North Territory of Baja California became the 29th state on January 16, 1952 [practitioner: English Wikipedia, "Baja California", read]; its constitution was published August 16, 1953 [official: Constitution of Baja California, read]. The state wrote its own civil code only in 1974, 22 years later [official, read]; which code applied in between was not checked [honest null].
  - **Founders and law.** The scope records a late, foreign-financed effective settlement (Ensenada 1882-1885, Tijuana 1889, Mexicali 1903 on the Colorado River Land Company's land) and the 1937 seizure that turned the company's land into *ejidos* (scope, section 2, disagreement 5). No source read here ties the state's law to those founders; the codes descend from the federal-district line [synthesis].
  - **Political layer.** In 1989 Ernesto Ruffo Appel (PAN) became the first opposition governor of any state since the Revolution; the PAN governed until 2019, and Morena since (Jaime Bonilla Valdez 2019-2021; Marina del Pilar Ávila Olmeda 2021-2027) [practitioner: Wikipedia, read; the 2021-2027 term from the codes' reform notes, official, read].

- **Precedent.** The state's courts are bound by federal *jurisprudencia* and by the SCJN's precedents (six votes) [official: federal Constitution, Art. 94, read]. Whether the state's own Superior Court issues binding state precedent was not checked [honest null].

- **Main sources of law.** The federal constitution and laws; the state constitution; the state codes and some 160 state laws (the Congress's index lists, for example, laws on amnesty (2022), therapeutic justice (2018), extortion (2025), citizen security (2020, reformed 2026), migrants (2025), the wine industry (2026) and journalists (2012)) [official: Congress index of laws in force, read for titles and dates; the amnesty, therapeutic-justice, journalists' and security laws were opened and read in their first articles]; municipal regulations; and, inside Indigenous communities, their internal normative systems (see Pluralism).

- **Trials.**
  - **Criminal:** accusatory and oral under the national code of criminal procedure (2014), like every state [official: federal profile].
  - **Civil and family:** moving to oral proceedings under the national code (2023): Playas de Rosarito first (operating by March 2026), Tijuana planned for March 2027, inside the national deadline of April 1, 2027 [practitioner: El Imparcial, March 30, 2026, read; Zeta Tijuana, search summary].
  - **Juries:** none [synthesis, as federal].

- **Judges: elected since 2025.** The state constitution's transitional articles set an **extraordinary local judicial election in 2025** for 17 numerary magistrates of the Superior Court of Justice (one for juvenile justice, sixteen mixed), three supernumerary magistrates, three numerary and one supernumerary magistrate of the new Tribunal de Disciplina Judicial, and **all** trial judges; sitting judges were placed on the ballot unless they declined; those elected took office September 1, 2025, for terms ending in 2033 [official: Constitution of Baja California, transitional arts. 4-5 of the judicial reform, read]. A new **Ley Orgánica del Poder Judicial** was published August 31, 2026 [official, read]. **Disagreement:** English Wikipedia's article on the 2025 judicial elections names only Durango and Veracruz as holding local judicial elections alongside the federal one [practitioner, read]. The constitution says what the law required, not whether the vote took place; recorded, not resolved.

- **Constitutional review.** By the federal courts: amparo, and the SCJN's actions of unconstitutionality and constitutional controversies. The state constitution's reform notes record several invalidations, among them action **112/2019** and its joined cases (published September 18, 2020), which voided Decree No. 351 of October 17, 2019 [official: Constitution of Baja California, reform notes, read]; that decree was the extension of the governor's term known as the *Ley Bonilla* [claim, not consulted: the content of Decree 351 is from general knowledge]. Others: 42/2015, 119/2020, 111/2021 [official, read]. A state-level constitutional court or procedure was not checked [honest null].

- **Pluralism.**
  - **Indigenous peoples.** The state constitution recognizes the state as "pluricultural, plurilingüe y pluriétnica", founded on its **native peoples** ("pueblos nativos": "Kiliwas, Kumiais, Pa Ipais, Cucapás, Cochimíes y Ku´ahles") and on **resident Indigenous communities** from other regions, and recognizes both as collective subjects of public law with legal personality and their own patrimony [official: Constitution of Baja California, Art. 7, read]. Native peoples are defined as those descended from populations settled there "before colonization and the establishment of the current borders" who keep their own institutions and "sistemas normativos propios" [official, read]. **Note for jobs L7 and I4:** the constitution names six native peoples, including the **Ku'ahl**, while the scope lists five [official, read; flagged].
  - **The Ley de Derechos y Cultura Indígena** (October 26, 2007; last reformed March 18, 2016) regulates Art. 2 of the federal constitution and recognizes "internal normative systems", defined as oral and written customary norms that communities use to govern themselves and settle conflicts, "siempre y cuando no se con[traponga]" to the law [official, read]. A 2015 report called it unapplied for lack of constitutional standing [practitioner: Zeta Tijuana, via the scope's search summary]; the constitution's present recognition of native peoples may postdate that report, but the date of the paragraph was not pinned (Art. 7 was added in 1992 and reformed 28 times since, most recently March 27, 2026) [official: reform notes, read; synthesis].
  - **Agrarian:** *ejidos* and *comunidades* under federal agrarian law and the federal agrarian tribunals (see the federal profile), including the Mexicali valley *ejidos* of 1937 (scope) [official and synthesis].
  - No Indigenous courts [synthesis; job L7 profiles the communities' own order].

- **Family and property law** (state) [official: Civil Code, read]:
  - **Marriage** is "la unión libre de dos personas" (Art. 143, as reformed by Decree No. 253, P.O. September 14, 2021), so it is open to same-sex couples; both parties must be adults, "No habrá excepción" (Art. 145). The state had stopped enforcing its ban in November 2017, after federal court rulings, and its Congress legalized same-sex marriage on June 16, 2021 by 18 to 4 [practitioner: English Wikipedia, read].
  - **Marital property:** there is no default; the couple must file an agreement stating "con toda claridad" whether they marry under **sociedad conyugal** (community property) or **separación de bienes** (separate property), even when they own nothing yet; spouses manage their own property without the other's authorization unless the agreement says otherwise (Arts. 95, 169).
  - **Inheritance:** testamentary freedom, limited by the duty to leave support (*alimentos*) to minor children, dependants unable to work and a surviving cohabiting partner in need; a will that fails this is *inoficioso* (Art. 1255 ff.). *Concubinato* is recognized for paternity and support (Art. 380 and the support rules).
  - **Other:** a new birth certificate for the recognition of gender identity (civil registry provisions); persons who fail to pay court-ordered support for more than thirty days may be barred from leaving the country.

- **Levels.** As in the federal profile: the state sets its constitution, substantive civil, family and penal law, the state police and the state prosecutor's office (its organic law in the version of December 26, 2025), state taxes, and its judiciary; the federation sets procedure, commerce, labor, guns, drugs (with state jurisdiction over small-scale dealing), religion and agrarian law; the municipalities (seven: Tijuana, Mexicali, Ensenada, Tecate, Playas de Rosarito, San Quintín and San Felipe) set zoning and local regulations [official: Congress index, read for the Fiscalía law and the municipal revenue laws of 2026 for all seven; practitioner: Wikipedia, read, for the list].

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

- **Overall reading** [synthesis]. Within Mexico's federal frame (see that profile: statist left on the economy, authoritarian on guns, policing and government reach), Baja California's own law leans **left-libertarian on personal conduct** (same-sex marriage, abortion to 12 weeks, an amnesty that reaches abortion, petty theft and poor or coerced drug dealers, and a therapeutic-justice track for addiction) and **comparatively market-friendly on the economy** (the EFNA ranks its state and local level second in Mexico, though falling). It pairs these with **weak delivery of order and justice** (the WJP's homicide and criminal-justice scores; victimization rising in 2025) and with **participatory checks** (recall of the governor and deputies; a term extension voided by the SCJN). The constitution's clause protecting life from conception, left in place beside the 2021 decriminalization, is the clearest sign that the law is not one voice. If one label is needed: **socially liberal, economically center-right for Mexico, inside an authoritarian federal security frame**.
- **The author's reading:** none given for Baja California (brief, 2026-11-03). Nothing is supplied in its place.
- **Where the measures and the author's reading agree or differ:** honest null. Between the measures: the EFNA's high ranking for economic freedom and the WJP's low score on constraints on government and corruption (0.38 and 0.35) measure different things and do not conflict; the EFNA is a free-market think tank's index [practitioner], and the state laws read here (a 4.25% payroll tax, gambling and ride-service taxes) are consistent with a modest state fiscal footprint [official; synthesis]. On the 2025 judicial election, the state constitution and English Wikipedia disagree (Part A); not resolved.
- **Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The whole state is one unit's lobe (**S4 El Norte**), with the **I4 Yuman peoples'** communities and the **San Quintín** migrant communities inside it. The 1953 constitution created four municipalities, Ensenada, Mexicali, Tecate and Tijuana [official: Constitution of Baja California, transitional articles, read]; Playas de Rosarito, San Quintín and San Felipe followed (dates not checked) [practitioner: Wikipedia, read]. Populations are INEGI 2020 as recorded in the scope (section 3) [measured].
  - **Tijuana** (1,922,523): the border metropolis paired with San Diego (seam 6). Its civil and family courts move to the national oral code only in March 2027 [practitioner: El Imparcial, read]. The Zona Norte's tolerated, municipally regulated sex trade [claim, not consulted] is the clearest municipal departure in personal-conduct law. City-level crime and policing figures were not consulted [honest null].
  - **Mexicali** (1,049,792): the state capital (Art. 96) [official, read], seat of the powers; the Colorado River delta's *ejido* agriculture of 1937 (scope), so agrarian law weighs more here than in the coastal cities [synthesis]; the Cucapá live in this municipality (scope) [practitioner, via the scope].
  - **Ensenada** (443,807): the wine valleys, now under a state law to protect and promote the wine industry (June 2026) [official: index, read for title]; most of the Kumiai, Pa ipai and Kiliwa communities named in the scope lie in this municipality [claim, not consulted, for the municipal locations].
  - **Playas de Rosarito** (126,890): first in the state under the national code of civil and family procedure [practitioner, read].
  - **San Quintín** (117,568): the farm valley of Mixtec, Triqui and Zapotec workers (scope); the amnesty law's clause for Indigenous people acting from extreme poverty or under coercion is most likely to apply in places like this [synthesis]; the communities' own order is profiled in job L7.
  - **Tecate** (108,440) and **San Felipe**: not researched separately [honest null].
  - **Across the seam to California** (seam 6): Mexican civil law with elected judges, mandatory pretrial detention and no juries, against California's common law; same-sex marriage and early abortion are legal on both sides, while gun law and policing differ sharply [synthesis; California's profile is job L2].
  - **Across the 28th parallel** (seam 4): see the Baja California Sur profile. The two states differ in the EFNA (5.46 against 4.32) and in victimization (23,095 against 19,505 per 100,000) [practitioner and measured, read]; on the WJP's index, the sources disagree about Baja California Sur's latest result (see its profile).
