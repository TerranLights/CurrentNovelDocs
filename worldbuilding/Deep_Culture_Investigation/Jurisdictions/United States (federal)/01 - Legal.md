Written for the whole federal layer, with Cascadia's parts named where they differ.

## 1. Part A: legal system

**Legal family.** Common law, in a federal union: one federal legal order over fifty state orders, each state in the common-law family except Louisiana's mixed civil-law private law, which touches no target in this library yet [claim: general knowledge, not consulted].

**How it got there (founders first, then later layers).**
- **The founding layer: English common law, received colony by colony.** The colonies practiced English law under their charters; at independence the states kept it by reception statutes, constitutional clauses or court decisions, each fixing a cutoff date (New York's 1777 constitution took English law as it stood on April 19, 1775) and leaving it subject to later legislation [practitioner: Wikipedia, "Reception statute", read]. The Northwest Ordinance (1787) promised the western territories "judicial proceedings according to the course of the common law", which is how the common law traveled west into territories that became states [practitioner: same, read]. Alaska's own reception statute (AS 01.10.010) makes "so much of the common law" as is consistent with the two constitutions and state statutes the rule of decision [official: Alaska Statutes, read]. California's and Oregon's receptions are for the L2 and L3 profiles.
- **The federal constitution** (1787, in force 1789) and the Bill of Rights (ratified December 15, 1791) [official: National Archives transcripts, read].
- **There is no general federal common law.** Since *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins* (1938), federal courts apply state common law in state-law matters; federal judge-made law survives only in narrow federal fields [official: ruling, not consulted].
- **Later layers:** the Reconstruction amendments (1865-1870), through which the Supreme Court in the 20th century applied most of the Bill of Rights to the states one right at a time [claim: general knowledge, not consulted]; the New Deal administrative state of the 1930s (federal agencies, regulation, Social Security) [claim, not consulted]; the civil-rights statutes of the 1960s [claim, not consulted]; and the present Court's turn to text and history (*New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen*, 2022, below) [official: ruling, read].

**Precedent.** Binding, vertically and horizontally. A U.S. Supreme Court ruling on federal law binds every court in the country; a regional circuit's published decisions bind the federal district courts in that circuit and later three-judge panels, until the circuit sitting en banc or the Supreme Court changes them. State courts are bound on federal questions only by the Supreme Court and treat circuit rulings as persuasive [claim: general doctrine, not checked].

**The Ninth Circuit: a regional legal layer of its own.** All of Cascadia's U.S. states and all of Alaska lie in one federal circuit.
- **What it is.** The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit covers nine states (Alaska, Arizona, California, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Washington) and two territories (Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands): 67.1 million people in 2023, 19.8% of the U.S. population, the most populous of the twelve regional circuits. It has 29 authorized judgeships, the most of any circuit (the Fifth Circuit is next with 17); its judgeships were last increased in 1984 [official: CRS report R48242, McMillion, October 24, 2024, read]. It rehears cases en banc with 11 judges (the chief judge and ten drawn at random), not the whole court, a procedure unique to this circuit [official: CRS R48242 and the court's "About" page, read]; the court's limited en banc was authorized by Congress in 1978 [practitioner: Wikipedia and the court's site, search summary]. A senator's floor statement quoted by CRS put its load at roughly 12,000 appeals a year and noted that the 11-judge panel means "only one-third of its judges" decide the law for the whole court [claim: as quoted in CRS, read].
- **Its bench.** On the date read, the 29 active judges were 16 appointed by Democratic presidents (Clinton 3, Obama 5, Biden 8) and 13 by Republican presidents (G. W. Bush 2, Trump 11, one of them in his second term) [practitioner: Wikipedia, current composition table, read]. Its reputation as the circuit most often reversed rests on figures such as 61% of its reviewed cases reversed in 1999-2008 and, in a 2018 study, the highest reversal rate per thousand cases [practitioner: Wikipedia, read; the underlying studies not consulted]. CRS deliberately sets ideology aside and records 59 bills between 1963 and 2024 to split the circuit, mostly on grounds of size; Alaska's Senator Dan Sullivan co-sponsored one in 2016 [official: CRS R48242, read].
- **What it means for this library.** (1) On every federal question, Washington, Oregon, California and Alaska live under one body of circuit precedent, set by a bench drawn from the whole West; the layer is wider than Cascadia and joins it to the Sonora (Arizona), Colorado (Idaho, Montana, Nevada) and Hawaii targets [synthesis: from the CRS coverage list]. (2) Because the en banc court is a random third of the judges, circuit law on a contested question can turn on the draw [claim: as argued in the CRS-quoted statement]. (3) The circuit has decided the federal questions that are most Western: Native treaty fishing rights in Washington (*United States v. Washington*, 1974, affirmed by the Ninth Circuit) [official: ruling, not consulted], federal subsistence priority on Alaska's navigable waters (the *Katie John* cases, 1995-2013) [official: rulings, not consulted], and the federalized National Guard in Los Angeles in 2025 (*Newsom v. Trump*: a panel stayed the district court's order in June 2025, the court denied en banc rehearing on October 22, 2025 over ten judges' dissent, and the deployment ended on December 31, 2025 after the district court's December order was upheld) [practitioner: Wikipedia, "Newsom v. Trump", read]. (4) **The federal district lines inside Cascadia follow Woodard's seams closely.** Washington's Western District is exactly the 19 Left Coast counties and its Eastern District exactly the 20 Far West counties; in California the Northern District is the Left Coast lobe with Lake instead of Solano and Yolo, the Eastern District is the Far West lobe with Solano and Yolo instead of Lake and San Bernardino, the Central District is El Norte's six northern counties plus San Bernardino, and the Southern District is San Diego and Imperial [official: 28 U.S.C. §§ 84 and 128, read; compared with `Data/Unit_Territories.md`]. Oregon and Alaska are one district each [claim: general knowledge, not checked].

**Main sources of law.** The federal Constitution, federal statutes (the U.S. Code), treaties, which with the Constitution are "the supreme Law of the Land" [official: Art. VI, cl. 2, read], federal regulations, and case law. Under the Tenth Amendment, powers not delegated to the federal government "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people" [official: read].

**Trials.** Adversarial. The Constitution guarantees a jury for "the Trial of all Crimes" (Art. III, § 2) and "an impartial jury of the State and district" (Sixth Amendment), and a civil jury in federal suits over twenty dollars (Seventh Amendment) [official: National Archives transcripts, read]. The criminal jury binds the states (*Duncan v. Louisiana*, 1968) and must be unanimous for a serious offense there too (*Ramos v. Louisiana*, 2020); the civil-jury guarantee does not bind state courts [official: rulings, not consulted]. In practice most federal criminal cases end in guilty pleas, not trials [claim: not checked].

**Judges.** No career judiciary. Federal (Article III) judges are nominated by the president, confirmed by the Senate, and hold office during good behavior, that is, for life [official: Art. III, § 1, read]; they come from practice, prosecution, academia and the state benches [claim, not checked]. State judges are chosen in each state's own way (election, appointment or merit selection; Alaska's is in its profile).

**Constitutional review.** By the ordinary courts, with no separate constitutional court. *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" [official: National Archives, Milestone Documents, read]. Any court, state or federal, can refuse to apply a law it finds unconstitutional, subject to appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court [claim: general doctrine, not checked].

**Pluralism: federal law on Native nations,** the frame for the tribal profiles (L6, L7), summarized here.
- **The tribes.** 575 tribal entities are federally recognized as of January 30, 2026, including the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina, added after Congress recognized it on December 18, 2025 [official: Federal Register, January 30, 2026, FR Doc. 2026-01899, read]. Alaska holds 229 of them [practitioner: news on Alaska's HB 123, search summary].
- **The Marshall trilogy:** *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823, tribes hold a right of occupancy, the United States the underlying title), *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831, tribes as "domestic dependent nations") and *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832, state law has no force inside tribal territory) [official: rulings, not consulted].
- **Plenary power.** Congress's power over Native affairs, traced to the Commerce Clause's tribal provision ("Commerce ... with the Indian Tribes", Art. I, § 8) [official: read], to treaties, to the Constitution's structure and to the trust relationship. *Haaland v. Brackeen* (June 15, 2023, 7-2, Barrett) reaffirmed it as "plenary and exclusive" yet "not unbounded": "plenary within its sphere, but even a sizeable sphere has borders" [official: slip opinion syllabus, read]. Its older foundations, *United States v. Kagama* (1886) and *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903) [official: rulings, not consulted].
- ***Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe*** (March 6, 1978; 6-2, Rehnquist; Marshall and Chief Justice Burger dissenting; Brennan not participating): tribal courts "do not have inherent criminal jurisdiction to try and to punish non-Indians" unless Congress authorizes it [official: ruling, read]. **The case came from Cascadia,** from the Port Madison Reservation on Puget Sound [official: same]. Congress's later repairs: *Duro v. Reina* (1990) and the 1991 "Duro fix" restoring tribal criminal jurisdiction over Native people who are not members of the tribe, upheld in *United States v. Lara* (2004) [official: rulings and statute, not consulted].
- **Public Law 280 (1953)** gave named states criminal jurisdiction on Native lands ("Indian country", the statute's term) "to the same extent" as elsewhere: all Native lands in California; all in Oregon except the Warm Springs Reservation; and all in Alaska, added for the territory in 1958 (Pub. L. 85-615), with the Metlakatla Indian Community on the Annette Islands keeping jurisdiction over offenses by Native people (the state-era text, 1970, Pub. L. 91-523) [official: 18 U.S.C. § 1162 and its amendment notes, read]. Washington took partial jurisdiction as an optional state [practitioner: BIA and encyclopedia, search summary, via the Cascadia scope]. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 lets a Public Law 280 tribe ask for concurrent federal jurisdiction, with the Attorney General's consent [official: § 1162(d), read].
- **The Indian Civil Rights Act (1968,** Pub. L. 90-284) applies most Bill of Rights guarantees to tribal governments and caps tribal sentences at one year and $5,000 per offense; since 2010 a tribe that provides licensed defense counsel and a law-trained judge may impose up to three years and $15,000, nine years in all [official: 25 U.S.C. § 1302, read].
- **The Indian Child Welfare Act (1978)** sets placement preferences and tribal rights in state custody cases involving Native children; *Brackeen* upheld it against Article I and anti-commandeering challenges and left the equal-protection and nondelegation claims unreached for lack of standing [official: syllabus, read].
- **The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988),** passed after *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians* (1987), a California case [official: statute and ruling, not consulted].
- **The Violence Against Women Act.** The 2013 act (Pub. L. 113-4) recognized participating tribes' "inherent power" to exercise special criminal jurisdiction over non-Native people for domestic and dating violence and protection-order violations; the 2022 reauthorization (Pub. L. 117-103) widened the "covered crimes" to nine (adding child violence, sexual violence, sex trafficking, stalking, obstruction of justice and assault of tribal justice personnel), with an impartial jury drawn from "a fair cross section of the community" among the defendant's required rights [official: 25 U.S.C. § 1304, read]. Two Cascadian tribes, Tulalip and Umatilla, were among the first three approved in February 2014 [official: Justice Department release, search summary, via the Cascadia scope]. The 2022 act's **Alaska Pilot Program** lets the Attorney General designate Alaska tribes to exercise this jurisdiction in their villages, which are not Native lands in the federal sense, "no more than five" a year and 30 in all; the program page listed no designations as of its update of August 9, 2024 [official: Justice Department, read].
- **Later layers** the tribal profiles should pick up: *Montana v. United States* (1981, limits on tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers), *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020) and *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022, state jurisdiction over crimes by non-Native people against Native people on Native lands) [official: rulings, not consulted]. ANCSA (1971) and *Venetie* (1998), which shape Alaska's tribes, are in the Alaska profile.

**Family and property law.** Almost entirely state law: marriage, divorce, marital property (community property in California and Washington; Oregon is a separate-property state; Alaska lets couples opt in), inheritance, contract, tort and most land law [claim: general knowledge; Alaska confirmed in its profile]. The federal overlays: the Constitution's floor on marriage (*Obergefell v. Hodges*, 2015) and the Respect for Marriage Act (2022); abortion returned to the states by *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) [official: rulings and statute, not consulted]; ICWA in Native child custody (above); federal tax and pension law over all of it [claim, not consulted]; and the Takings Clause (*Kelo v. City of New London*, 2005, allowed taking for economic development) [official, not consulted]. **The federal government is the West's largest landholder:** about 640 million acres, roughly 28% of U.S. land, at September 30, 2024, including 60.8% of Alaska, 52.3% of Oregon, 45.3% of California and 28.6% of Washington [official: CRS report R42346, updated September 14, 2026, read].

## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading.** The federal layer is a common-law, rights-centered constitutional order whose own law sits **right of the rich-democracy center on taxes, welfare, guns and punishment; libertarian on speech, religion and arms; authoritarian on incarceration, drug scheduling (on paper) and surveillance**; and it leaves most of personal conduct, family, property, land use and everyday policing to the states [synthesis: from the rows above]. So for Cascadia most of what differs between units is in the state profiles, not here. The federal layer's main contribution to difference inside Cascadia is structural: where federal land, Native lands and federal district lines fall (below).

**The national trend: the states have pulled apart under one federal frame.** In Caughey and Warshaw's policy-liberalism scores (a dynamic latent-variable measure built from 148 state policies), the spread between states grew steadily: the standard deviation across the 50 states was 0.82 in 1960, 0.90 in 1980, 1.15 in 2000 and 1.42 in 2014 [measured: Caughey and Warshaw 2016, the `pollib_median` series as carried in the Correlates of State Policy Project v2.6, computed here]. Their separate economic and social scores show the same widening (economic 0.83 to 1.21, social 0.82 to 1.28, 1960 to 2014) [measured: Caughey and Warshaw 2018, APSR replication summary file, computed here]. Their own summary of the long run is that state policy liberalism rose from the 1930s to the 1970s and then largely plateaued, with most states stable relative to one another [scholarly: Caughey and Warshaw 2016, abstract, search summary]. Federal devolution since 2018 (*Murphy*, *Dobbs*, the 2025 welfare cost shift) adds to the room in which the states diverge [synthesis]. The series in the Correlates dataset stop in 2014; the authors' later update in *Dynamic Democracy* (2022) was not consulted.

**The author's reading:** none given for the federal layer (brief, 2026-11-03). Recorded as none; no reading has been invented.

**Where the measures and the author's reading agree or differ:** no comparison is possible without an author's reading. Among the measures themselves, one disagreement is recorded, not resolved: on incarceration, the Bureau of Justice Statistics' sentenced-prisoner rate (360 per 100,000, 2023) and the Prison Policy Initiative's all-confinement rate (614 per 100,000) count different populations (people sentenced to more than a year in state and federal prisons, against everyone held in any facility, jails included) [measured and practitioner: as cited above].

**Variation inside the jurisdiction** (how federal law lands differently across the country and across Cascadia):
- **By circuit.** Federal law is the same text everywhere but not the same case law: the Ninth Circuit's precedent governs Washington, Oregon, California and Alaska, together with states whose own policies sit at the far ends of the national range. In Caughey and Warshaw's 2014 scores the circuit's states run from California (2nd of 50) and Hawaii (3rd) through Oregon (11th), Washington (12th) and Alaska (25th) to Arizona (33rd) and Idaho (43rd) [measured: CSPP v2.6, computed here]. One appellate bench thus sets federal case law for a region whose state law is anything but uniform [synthesis].
- **By federal district, tracking the unit seams.** In Washington the Western District is the Left Coast lobe and the Eastern District the Far West lobe, county for county; in California the four districts fit Left Coast, Far West and El Norte with four counties out of place (Lake, Solano, Yolo, San Bernardino) [official: 28 U.S.C. §§ 84, 128, read; compared with `Data/Unit_Territories.md`]. Each district has its own U.S. Attorney, whose charging priorities (for example on marijuana or immigration) differ by district [claim: general knowledge, not checked].
- **By federal land.** Federal land law weighs heaviest where the federal share is largest: Alaska (60.8%), Oregon (52.3%), California (45.3%), Washington (28.6%) [measured: CRS R42346, read]. Within the states the share is far higher in the interior and the Far West counties than on the urban Left Coast [claim: not checked at county level; Stage 2 can measure it].
- **By Native lands and Public Law 280.** Criminal jurisdiction on Native lands differs by state: state jurisdiction in California, in Oregon except Warm Springs, and in Alaska except Metlakatla; partial state jurisdiction in Washington; federal and tribal jurisdiction elsewhere [official: 18 U.S.C. § 1162, read; Washington from a search summary]. In Alaska little land counts as Native land in this sense at all after *Venetie* (Alaska profile). This splits the I1-I5 Indigenous units along state lines [synthesis].
- **By state cooperation with federal enforcement.** Where states decline to help enforce federal immigration law, or license marijuana that federal law still lists, federal law reaches residents differently from state to state [claim: the state laws are for the L2, L3 and Alaska profiles].
