## 1. Part A: legal system

| Field | Oregon |
|---|---|
| **Legal family** | **Common law** (Anglo-American), with a heavy layer of **direct legislation** by the voters (the "Oregon System"). No civil-law layer: unlike California and Washington, whose marital-property law came from Spanish civil law, Oregon is a separate-property state (see Family and property below). A short-lived community-property statute of the 1940s, adopted for federal income-tax reasons and then repealed, is from general knowledge [claim, not checked] |
| **How it got there** | **Founders first.** The settlers' Provisional Government adopted the **Organic Laws of July 5, 1843**, modeled on Iowa Territory's organic law of 1838 and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787; they took over the statute laws of Iowa Territory "in civil, military and criminal cases", with "the principles of common law and equity" filling the gaps. The 1845 revision dropped the reference to Iowa [practitioner: encyclopedia summaries of the Organic Laws, *(search summary)*]. So the common law arrived through the American Midwest, not directly from England, carried by the Yankee and Midwestern founders the Cascadia scope names for the Left Coast [synthesis: Woodard, via the scope]. **The 1857 constitution** copied "nearly all" its provisions from other states, "principally" the **Indiana Constitution of 1851** [practitioner: State Court Report, *(read)*; Wikipedia's convention article says over half derives at least in part from Indiana's, *(search summary)*]. **Its racial clauses:** a Bill of Rights clause barring Black people from coming to or living in the state, "the only one of its kind in a non-slavery state" [practitioner: State Court Report, *(read)*], put to the voters separately in 1857 and adopted [practitioner: Oregon Encyclopedia, *(search summary)*]; a suffrage clause excluding "negro, Chinaman or mulatto" voters [practitioner: search summary]. The exclusion clause was made moot by the Fourteenth Amendment but **repealed by the voters only in 1926** [practitioner: Oregon Encyclopedia; State Court Report, *(read)*]. Remaining racist language was removed later (2002, from general knowledge) [claim, not checked]. **Later layers:** the **Oregon System**: initiative and referendum ratified in **1902** "by a margin of 11 to 1", then the direct primary, the recall and women's suffrage within the next decade [practitioner: State Court Report, *(read)*]; 260 amendments in all [practitioner: State Court Report, *(read)*], most of them by the voters, which makes the constitution a working statute book as much as a framework |
| **Precedent** | **Binding** (stare decisis). Oregon Supreme Court decisions bind every state court; Court of Appeals decisions bind the trial courts [claim: standard common-law hierarchy, not checked against an Oregon source]. On federal questions the Ninth Circuit and the U.S. Supreme Court bind (see the federal profile) |
| **Main sources of law** | The Oregon Constitution (frequently amended by initiative); the **Oregon Revised Statutes**, many of them enacted or amended by ballot measure (Measure 11, Measure 91, Measure 110 and others below); administrative rules; case law; county and city charters and ordinances under home rule [claim: the home-rule dates (cities 1906, counties 1958) are from general knowledge, not checked] |
| **Trials** | **Adversarial.** Jury trial in criminal and civil cases. **Non-unanimous criminal juries, 1934-2020:** Ballot Measure 2, adopted May 18, 1934 with about 58% of the vote, amended Article I, section 11 to allow 10-2 and 11-1 felony verdicts (murder excepted) and to let defendants in non-capital cases waive a jury [official: *State v. Osbourne* (1936), and Ballotpedia, *(search summary)*]. Arguments for it cited the 1933 trial of a Jewish defendant, Jacob Silverman, whom a jury convicted only of manslaughter, and the rule is described as meant in part to dampen the influence of "racial, ethnic, and religious minorities on juries" [practitioner: Oregon Encyclopedia, *(search summary)*]. Oregon and Louisiana were the last two states with the rule [practitioner: search summary]. ***Ramos v. Louisiana*** **(2020)** held that the Sixth Amendment requires unanimous verdicts in state felony trials; the U.S. Supreme Court declined to make that retroactive (*Edwards v. Vannoy*, 2021), but the Oregon Supreme Court made it **retroactive under state law** in ***Watkins v. Ackley*** **(December 30, 2022)**: "anyone who had a non-unanimous jury conviction is entitled to a new trial" [official: Oregon Public Defense Commission, *(read)*], with a two-year window for post-conviction petitions [official: same, *(read)*]. **Civil juries:** a 1910 amendment (Article VII (Amended)) bars courts from re-examining facts a jury has found and allows three-fourths civil verdicts [claim: from general knowledge, not checked] |
| **Judges** | **Elected from practicing lawyers**, not a career judiciary. All judges (Supreme Court, Court of Appeals, Tax Court, circuit courts) are **elected in nonpartisan elections to six-year terms** [practitioner: encyclopedia summary of the Oregon Judicial Department, *(search summary)*]; the governor fills vacancies by appointment until the next election, so many judges first reach the bench by appointment [claim: from general knowledge, not checked]. **The courts** (Oregon Judicial Department, February 2026): a **Supreme Court of 7 justices**, which chooses its cases and hears direct appeals in death-penalty cases and challenges to **ballot-measure titles**; a **Court of Appeals of 13 judges**, "one of the busiest appellate courts in the nation"; a **Tax Court** (1 judge, 3 magistrates); and **circuit courts, 186 judges in 27 judicial districts**, one in every county, which "are not county courts — they are state courts" [official: OJD, *Oregon's State Courts: Overview*, February 2026, *(read)*]. Municipal and justice courts sit outside this list [claim, not checked] |
| **Constitutional review** | **The ordinary courts**, with the Oregon Supreme Court on top; no separate constitutional court. Oregon is the leading example of **state constitutional primacy**: Hans Linde, appointed to the court in 1977, argued that state courts should decide a case under the state constitution before turning to the federal one, and the court adopted the approach, often reading state guarantees as "significantly more protective of individual rights" than their federal counterparts [practitioner: State Court Report, *(read)*]. The best-known application is free expression (Part B, speech row). The ballot measure makes constitutional review politically central: the court reviews measures' titles before the vote and their validity after it (Measure 37, Measure 114 below) |
| **Pluralism** | **Tribal law and courts** of the nine federally recognized tribes. Oregon is a **mandatory Public Law 280 state, except the Warm Springs Reservation**, so the state holds criminal jurisdiction on most reservations; the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation were among the first three tribes approved (2014) to prosecute non-Native people for domestic violence under the Violence Against Women Act [practitioner and official: the Cascadia scope, section 1, from search summaries]. Details belong to the T2 profile (job L6). No religious or regional courts |
| **Family and property law** | **A separate-property (common-law) state with equitable distribution:** on divorce the court divides property in a way that is "just and proper in all the circumstances" (ORS 107.105(1)(f)), and may reach even separate property to do so [official: the statute as quoted in Oregon appellate decisions, *(search summary)*]. **Inheritance:** free testation, with a surviving spouse's **elective share** of an augmented estate rising with the length of the marriage (ORS 114.600; the summary gives 5% to 33%, reaching 33% after 15 years) [official: the statute, *(search summary)*]. **Founders' layer:** the federal Donation Land Claim Act (1850) granted land to settler couples with half in the wife's own name, an early break from coverture on the land itself [claim: from general knowledge, not checked; this belongs mainly to the units' land-and-inheritance profiles] |
| **Levels** | **Federal:** the U.S. Constitution as a floor (Oregon often exceeds it), federal crimes, the Controlled Substances Act (which still bans what Oregon legalized), federal law on Native nations, the U.S. District Court for the District of Oregon and the **Ninth Circuit** (see the federal profile). **State:** almost all criminal law and sentencing, family, property, contract and tort law, land-use planning (statewide since 1973), taxation, policing standards, elections. **Counties and cities:** home-rule charters and ordinances; elected **sheriffs** and **district attorneys** in every county, which is where enforcement diverges (section 3) [claim: elected sheriffs and DAs, from general knowledge, not checked]. **The voters** are a fourth level of their own: many of the laws in Part B were made by initiative, not by the legislature |

## 2. Part B: legal orientation, by domain

**How the placements were made.** Each domain's two placements are this profile's reading of the laws and measures in the same row [claim: this profile's reading], not a published score. **Left / right** follows the usual U.S. sense (redistribution, regulation, social liberalism against their opposites). **Libertarian / authoritarian** is how far the law reaches into the area and how hard it is enforced, whatever its direction. **The measures used:** Caughey and Warshaw's policy liberalism, computed this session from their replication files (Harvard Dataverse; overall 1936-2014, AJPS 2016; economic and social dimensions 1936-2014, APSR 2018; ranks among 48 or 50 states, 1 = most liberal) [measured; scholarly]; the Fraser Institute's *Economic Freedom of North America 2025* (data for 2023; higher = more economic freedom) [practitioner: free-market think tank, read]; Cato's *Freedom in the 50 States 2023* (law as of January 1, 2023) [practitioner: libertarian think tank, search summary only, because the report's pages refused automated access]; the Bureau of Justice Statistics [measured, read]; the Death Penalty Information Center [practitioner, read]. **Not consulted:** RAND's State Firearm Law Database (the gun row uses the laws themselves instead) and the Correlates of State Policy Project. **Caughey and Warshaw's data end in 2014**, before most of the 2015-2026 changes below; their 1936-2020 update (*Dynamic Democracy*, 2022) is served through an online app this session could not reach.

**The federal layer** sits on every row (see the federal profile): it sets some domains outright (the Controlled Substances Act, federal crimes and the federal death penalty, the Second and First Amendments as limits, federal income and payroll taxes, the federal minimum wage as a floor) and leaves others to the state (most criminal law, family and property law, land use, state taxes). ## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

**Overall reading:** **left, and a planning state, with a libertarian streak on personal conduct and expression** [claim: this profile's reading of the rows above]. Caughey and Warshaw place Oregon's policy **11th most liberal of 50 in 2014** (estimate 1.34, 95% interval 0.81 to 1.84; California 2nd) and in the top 17 in every year checked from 1936 [measured: computed this session], 10th on economic and 14th on social policy. By domain: left and high-reach on land, rent and labor; libertarian on drugs (until 2024), assisted dying and speech; tightening on guns; moving from punitive to lenient on punishment, with a turn back in 2024. **What sets Oregon apart is less any single position than who makes the law:** the voters, by initiative since 1902, have both built the system (the planning goals were the legislature's, but Measures 49, 91, 109, 110 and 114 were the voters') and braked it (no sales tax, the kicker, Measures 11, 37 and 57). The initiative pulls left on personal conduct and guns, and right on taxes and punishment [claim: this profile's reading].

**The author's reading:** none given for Oregon (brief, 2026-11-03).

**Where the measures and the author agree or differ:** no author's reading to compare. **Between the measures:** they agree once their definitions are lined up. Caughey and Warshaw's "liberal" economic policy is what EFNA and Cato count as low economic freedom (Oregon 46th of 51 on EFNA's subnational index; 46th overall on Cato's). On personal conduct the measures point the other way from the economy: Cato ranked Oregon high on personal freedom until recently, which matches the libertarian reading here. EFNA and Cato are think tanks with a declared free-market and libertarian orientation, tagged practitioner; their rankings are checked against the laws in each row, which they match in direction.

**Variation inside the jurisdiction.** The **Cascade crest** divides Oregon's 18 **Left Coast** counties (Benton, Clackamas, Clatsop, Columbia, Coos, Curry, Douglas, Jackson, Josephine, Lane, Lincoln, Linn, Marion, Multnomah, Polk, Tillamook, Washington, Yamhill) from its 18 **Far West** counties (Baker, Crook, Deschutes, Gilliam, Grant, Harney, Hood River, Jefferson, Klamath, Lake, Malheur, Morrow, Sherman, Umatilla, Union, Wallowa, Wasco, Wheeler) (`Data/Unit_Territories.md`). The legal seam follows the crest in most things but not all:
- **"Greater Idaho."** Between 2020 and 2024 voters in **13 counties** approved measures about leaving Oregon for Idaho: Baker, Crook, Grant, Harney, Jefferson, Klamath, Lake, Malheur, Morrow, Sherman, Union, Wallowa and Wheeler [practitioner: Ballotpedia and encyclopedia summaries, *(search summary)*]. **All 13 are Far West counties.** The Idaho House adopted a non-binding memorial inviting talks (February 2023, 41-28); Oregon's legislature did not act [practitioner: search summary]. One summary reports that Wallowa County voters rescinded their support in May 2026 [practitioner: search summary; check].
- **Gun-rights ordinances and sheriffs.** In November 2018 a "Second Amendment Preservation Ordinance", which lets the sheriff decide whether state and federal gun laws are constitutional and withhold county resources from enforcing them, passed in **eight counties: Baker, Columbia, Douglas, Klamath, Lake, Linn, Umatilla and Union** [practitioner: news summaries, *(search summary)*]. **Three of the eight (Columbia, Douglas, Linn) are Left Coast counties**, so on guns the seam runs inside the Left Coast's rural west as well as along the crest. After Measure 114 passed, several sheriffs, among them Linn County's, said they would not enforce at least part of it [practitioner: news summaries, *(search summary)*], and the state-court challenge was filed in **Harney County** [official: HB 4145 staff summary, read].
- **The state's own law encodes the seam:** the three-tier minimum wage pays most inside the Portland urban growth boundary and least in the "nonurban counties" [practitioner: search summary] (which counties count as nonurban, mostly but not only east of the crest, is from general knowledge [claim, not checked]); HB 4002 leaves drug "deflection" to each county's district attorney and police [practitioner: search summary].
- **Prosecutors.** Elected county district attorneys set charging policy, so enforcement of the same statute differs between, for example, Multnomah County (Portland) and rural counties; Multnomah's 2024 election replaced a reform-minded DA [claim: from general knowledge, not checked].
- **Woodard's reading of the seam:** the Left Coast "battles constantly against the libertarian-corporate agenda" of the Far West [synthesis: Woodard, as quoted in the Cascadia scope, section 4]. The county evidence above fits that on taxes, guns and secession votes; on land use the Far West's own resistance (Measure 37's claims) was statewide, not only eastern [practitioner: search summary; the county split of Measure 37 claims was not checked].
- **Tribal and federal land** cut across both units: reservations under Public Law 280 (state criminal jurisdiction), except Warm Springs; and the large federal estate east of the crest, where federal agencies, not the state, set most land rules [claim: see the property row]. Tribal law is the T2 profile's subject (job L6).
