## 1. Part A: legal system

- **Legal family.** Civil law of the Romano-Germanic (continental) family, in its Spanish and French line, with a U.S.-influenced federal constitution and an original remedy of its own, the *amparo* [practitioner: Avalos, GlobaLex 2019, read; it also notes pre-Columbian Indigenous law as a component]. Indigenous "normative systems" are recognized inside it (see Pluralism) [official: Constitution, Art. 2, read].

- **How it got there.**
  - **Spanish law (the founding layer).** Spain's law ruled for 300 years, and after independence Mexico went on living under uncodified Spanish law until the codification of the Reform era [practitioner: Avalos, read; Brostoff, Law Library of Congress blog, 2017, read]. Which Spanish bodies of law applied (the *Siete Partidas*, the *Recopilación de Leyes de Indias*) is from general knowledge [claim, not consulted].
  - **French codes, through Mexican codifiers.** A draft civil code by Justo Sierra O'Reilly (1860) drew on the Napoleonic Code and the Louisiana Civil Code; Maximilian's French-backed empire promulgated a civil code in 1866, abrogated in 1867; the Republic's civil code was promulgated December 8, 1870 and took effect March 1, 1871, and most states then copied it [practitioner: Brostoff, read]. A UNAM-hosted summary describes the 1870 code as modeled mainly on the French civil code [scholarly: search summary]. **The 1870 and 1884 codes were enacted "for the Federal District and the Territory of Baja California"** [practitioner: Brostoff, read; scholarly: search summary], so the peninsula lived under the capital's civil law from the first codification.
  - **The 1928 code.** Published in the DOF in four parts (May 26, July 14, August 3 and August 31, 1928) as the *Código Civil para el Distrito y Territorios Federales en materia común, y para toda la República en materia federal*; it is today's *Código Civil Federal* [official: Chamber of Deputies reform register, read]. It took effect in 1932 [claim, not consulted]. Both Bajas were federal territories under it until statehood (1952 and 1974; see their profiles) [synthesis].
  - **Commerce and crime.** The *Código de Comercio* dates from October 7, 1889 and the *Código Penal Federal* from August 14, 1931, both still in force, much reformed [official: Chamber of Deputies index of federal laws, read].
  - **Constitutions.** 1824, 1857 and 1917; the 1857 text drew on U.S. and French principles [practitioner: Avalos, read]. The current constitution was published February 5, 1917 [official: Constitution, read].

- **The amparo.** Born in Yucatán's 1841 constitution, drafted by Manuel Crescencio Rejón; raised to the federal level by the 1847 *Acta Constitutiva y de Reformas* (Art. 25), shaped by Mariano Otero, whose "Otero formula" limits a ruling's effect to the person who brought it; carried into the 1857 constitution (Arts. 101-102) and the 1917 one (Arts. 103 and 107); first amparo law November 30, 1861 [practitioner: Spanish-language Wikipedia, "Juicio de amparo", read]. It combines what common law splits among habeas corpus, injunction, mandamus and certiorari [practitioner: Avalos, read]. The current *Ley de Amparo* dates from April 2, 2013 [official: federal laws index, read]. **Its reach has narrowed since 2024:** rulings against a general norm may not have general effects, and suspension may not be granted with general effects [official: Constitution, Art. 107, as reformed DOF 15-09-2024 and 31-10-2024, read]; no amparo lies against constitutional amendments [official: same, read]. The October 16, 2025 reform of the Ley de Amparo (in force October 17) tightened *interés legítimo* to a "real and differentiated" injury and required courts to weigh suspension against the public interest [practitioner: law-firm and trade summaries (KPMG, Holland & Knight, IDC), search summary].

- **Precedent.** Formally none in the common-law sense, but a binding system of *jurisprudencia*:
  - **By reiteration** (the classic rule): five consecutive, consistent rulings on a point of law bind the issuing court and all lower federal and state courts [practitioner: Avalos 2019, read].
  - **By precedent, from the 2021 reform:** the SCJN's reasons bind all courts when approved by eight votes in the Pleno or four in a Sala, without reiteration; the Eleventh Epoch of the *Semanario Judicial* began May 1, 2021 [official: SCJN press releases, search summary; practitioner: commentaries, search summary].
  - **Now (2024 reform):** the Court has nine members sitting as one Pleno, and the reasons of its rulings bind every federal and state court when approved by **six votes** [official: Constitution, Art. 94, as reformed DOF 15-09-2024, read]. Since June 2, 2026 the Pleno may also sit in two sections [official: Art. 94, DOF 02-06-2026, read]. Jurisprudence of the collegiate circuit courts and regional *Plenos* is left to the law [official: Art. 94, read; the current Ley de Amparo rules were not read].

- **Main sources of law.** The constitution; human-rights treaties, whose rights the constitution puts beside its own (Art. 1: rights "recognized in this Constitution and in the international treaties") [official: Art. 1, read]; federal laws; **general laws** that bind all three levels (for example on kidnapping, disappearance, trafficking, and since 2025-2026 extortion and feminicide) [official: Art. 73, fr. XXI, and the decrees of DOF 09-10-2025 and 06-05-2026, read; the extortion general law, DOF 28-11-2025, per the federal laws index, read]; **national codes** that replace state procedure; state constitutions, codes and laws; municipal regulations; and *jurisprudencia*. Custom counts mainly inside Indigenous normative systems (Art. 2) and as a gap-filler in commercial law [claim, not consulted, for the commercial point].

- **Trials.**
  - **Criminal:** from a written, mixed-inquisitorial procedure to an **accusatory, oral** one: "El proceso penal será acusatorio y oral" (Art. 20) [official: Constitution, reform DOF 18-06-2008, read]. The reform's transitional article gave the federation and the states at most eight years, so the deadline fell in June 2016 [official: 2008 decree, transitional art. 2, read]. One **Código Nacional de Procedimientos Penales** (DOF 05-03-2014) now governs federal and state prosecutions alike [official: federal laws index, read; Art. 73, fr. XXI(c), read].
  - **Civil and family:** a single **Código Nacional de Procedimientos Civiles y Familiares** (DOF 07-06-2023) is being phased in state by state on each local congress's declaration, with a deadline of April 1, 2027 [official: federal laws index, read; practitioner: press summaries of the code's transitional articles, search summary]. Congress has had the power to issue it since 2017 (Art. 73, fr. XXX) [official, read].
  - **Juries:** none in ordinary trials; judges are professionals. The constitution still lists jury service among civic duties (Arts. 5 and 36) [official, read], and older guides mention federal popular juries [practitioner: Avalos, read], but no current jury trial was found [synthesis; honest null on any surviving use].

- **Judges.**
  - **Until 2024:** a career judiciary, with SCJN ministers appointed by the Senate from presidential shortlists and a Federal Judicature Council (1994) running careers and discipline [claim, not consulted, for the appointment method; official: the 2024 decree's transitional articles still refer to the Council, read].
  - **Since the 2024 reform, judges are elected by popular vote.** Ministers of the SCJN, Electoral Tribunal magistrates, the new **Tribunal de Disciplina Judicial**, circuit magistrates and district judges are elected "de manera libre, directa y secreta" from lists nominated by the three branches through evaluation committees and a public lottery [official: Constitution, Arts. 94 and 96, DOF 15-09-2024 and 02-06-2026, read]. Ministers serve twelve years, once; circuit and district judges nine years, re-electable [official: Arts. 94 and 97, read]. Candidates need a law degree with an average of 8 (9 in the relevant subjects) and five years of practice [official: Art. 95, read]. Campaigns may not be funded publicly or privately, and parties may not campaign [official: Art. 96, read]. Judicial pay may not exceed the president's [official: Art. 94, read]. An *órgano de administración judicial* replaces the Council for administration [official: Art. 94, read].
  - **The first election, June 1, 2025,** chose the whole SCJN, the Disciplinary Tribunal, the Electoral Tribunal's regional chambers and vacancies, and **half** of all circuit and district judges [official: 2024 decree, transitional art. 2, read]. Turnout was 12.57-13.32% by the INE's quick count [practitioner: press reports of the INE announcement, search summary]; more than 20% of ballots were blank or void [practitioner: English Wikipedia, "2025 Mexican judicial elections", read]. The nine new ministers took office before the Senate on **September 1, 2025**; Hugo Aguilar Ortiz presides for 2025-2027 as the most-voted candidate [practitioner: press, search summary].
  - **The second election was moved from 2027 to the first Sunday of June 2028,** for all remaining federal and local judgeships, coinciding with the federal and local elections; electees take office September 1, 2028 [official: decree of DOF 02-06-2026, transitional arts. 2-3, read].

- **Constitutional review.** No separate constitutional court: the SCJN acts as one, through amparo (individual cases), and through the *controversia constitucional* (disputes between governments) and the *acción de inconstitucionalidad* (abstract review, brought by legislative minorities, parties, the Attorney General and rights commissions) under Art. 105, rewritten December 31, 1994 [official: Art. 105 and its reform notes, read; who may bring an action, claim, not consulted]. A statute is struck down with general effect only by **six of nine votes** [official: Art. 105, as reformed 2024, read]. **Since October 31, 2024, constitutional amendments cannot be challenged** by controversy, action or amparo [official: Arts. 105 and 107, DOF 31-10-2024, read]. The SCJN upheld the 2024 judicial reform on November 5, 2024 when a proposal to invalidate parts of it fell short of the eight votes then required [practitioner: El Financiero and Expansión, search summary].

- **Pluralism.**
  - **Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples.** Article 2 took its modern form in the reform of August 14, 2001 (recognition of Indigenous peoples and their autonomy); Afro-Mexican peoples were added as Apartado C on August 9, 2019 [official: Art. 2 reform notes and the 2001 decree, read]. Since September 30, 2024 the constitution recognizes Indigenous peoples and communities, and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities, as *sujetos de derecho público* with legal personality and their own patrimony [official: Art. 2, DOF 30-09-2024, read]. Communities may decide their internal government "conforme a sus sistemas normativos" and apply those systems to internal conflicts, subject to the constitution's principles, human rights and "the dignity and integrity of women" [official: Art. 2, A, I-II, read]. Self-determination is exercised "in a constitutional framework of autonomy that ensures national unity" [official: Art. 2, read]. There are no separate Indigenous courts at the federal level (the runbook's terms; job L7 covers the Bajas' communities) [synthesis].
  - **Agrarian law.** *Ejidos* and *comunidades* hold land as legal persons; the general assembly is their "órgano supremo"; no *ejidatario* may hold more than 5% of an *ejido*'s land; federal **agrarian tribunals** with full jurisdiction were created by the 1992 reform [official: Art. 27, fr. VII and XIX, reform DOF 06-01-1992, read]. The **Ley Agraria** of February 26, 1992 is still the governing statute (last reformed November 14, 2025; no new agrarian law found) [official: Chamber of Deputies reform register, read]. The 1992 reform let assemblies grant *ejidatarios* full ownership (*dominio pleno*) of their parcels [official: Art. 27, fr. VII, read].
  - **Other jurisdictions:** military justice (*Código de Justicia Militar*, 1933) [official: index, read]; the Electoral Tribunal [official: Art. 94, read].

- **Family and property law.** Set **by each state** in its own civil (and sometimes family) code; the *Código Civil Federal* governs only federal matters [practitioner: Avalos, read; official: CCF title, read]. The common pattern, seen in Baja California's code: spouses choose *sociedad conyugal* (community) or *separación de bienes* (separate property) when they marry, and testamentary freedom is limited by a duty to leave support (*alimentos*) to dependants (see the Baja California profile) [official: BC Civil Code, read]. Child marriage was abolished in the federal code in 2019 [official: CCF reform DOF 03-06-2019, title read]. **Same-sex marriage** is available in all 32 entities since October 2022, after the SCJN's June 12, 2015 jurisprudence that state bans violate Arts. 1 and 4 [practitioner: English Wikipedia, "Same-sex marriage in Mexico", read]. **Land and its limits are federal:** the nation holds original ownership; expropriation only "por causa de utilidad pública y mediante indemnización"; *latifundios* are banned and small private holdings capped (100 hectares of irrigated land per person, with variants) [official: Art. 27, read]. Foreigners may not acquire direct ownership of land within **100 km of the borders or 50 km of the coasts** [official: Art. 27, fr. I, read]; the trust arrangement used instead is set by the Ley de Inversión Extranjera (1993) [official: index, read for the date; the trust mechanism itself, claim, not consulted].

- **Levels: what is federal and what each state sets.** Powers not expressly given to the federation are reserved to the states (Art. 124) [official, read]. **Federal:** the constitution; commerce, labor (Ley Federal del Trabajo, 1970), banking, guns and explosives, religious associations (Art. 130 gives Congress exclusive power), agrarian law, nationality, migration, federal crimes and organized crime, telecommunications, energy and the "strategic areas" [official: Arts. 27, 28, 73, 123, 130, read]. **National codes and general laws** (centralizing, and growing): criminal procedure, alternative dispute resolution in criminal matters, sentence enforcement and juvenile justice (single legislation since 2013-2017), civil and family procedure (2017 power, 2023 code), forfeiture (*extinción de dominio*), and general laws on kidnapping, disappearance, trafficking, extortion (2025) and feminicide (2026) [official: Art. 73, fr. XXI and XXX, read]. **State:** the state constitution; substantive civil, family and penal law (each state has its own codes); state taxes; the state police and prosecutor; the state judiciary (now elected too: Art. 116) [official: Art. 116, read; synthesis]. **Concurrent:** health (the Ley General de Salud, under which states prosecute small-scale drug dealing since 2009; see Part B), education, security, environment [official and practitioner: see Part B]. **Municipal:** zoning and urban land use, local police, regulations (Art. 115) [official: Art. 115, not read in full; claim, not consulted, for zoning].

## 2. Part B: legal orientation, by domain

**Reading the axes.** Left/right is read in the usual comparative sense (redistribution, state ownership and social liberalism to the left; markets, low taxes and moral traditionalism to the right); libertarian/authoritarian is how far the law reaches into the area and how hard it is enforced. In Mexico the two often split from party labels: the governing left has driven both the welfare expansion and the militarized, punitive security policy [synthesis]. ## 3. Part B: overall reading, the author's reading, and variation

- **Overall reading** [synthesis]. The federal layer is **left on the economy and statist** (constitutional welfare rights, state-led energy, a shrinking workweek, regulators inside the executive), **libertarian on marriage and abortion, where the change came mainly from the Supreme Court**, and **strongly authoritarian on guns, drugs, policing and punishment, and government reach**, where the change since 2019 has come from constitutional amendments: a militarized National Guard, a long and growing list of crimes with mandatory pretrial detention, a biometric national ID, and fewer checks on the executive (elected judges, unchallengeable amendments, a narrower amparo, dissolved autonomous bodies). Because the summary label hides the split, the row readings should be used, not one word. If one label is needed: **statist left with an authoritarian security and governance core**.
- **The author's reading:** none given for Mexico (brief, 2026-11-03). Nothing is supplied here in its place.
- **Where the measures and the author's reading agree or differ:** honest null, since there is no author's reading. Among the measures themselves: the WJP (rule of law; Mexico 121st of 143) and the EFNA (economic freedom; Mexican states at the bottom of the North American ranking) point the same way, toward weak constraints and a heavy state, but the EFNA is a free-market think tank's measure [practitioner] and should be checked against the laws, which the rows above do. The laws agree with both on direction; the EFNA's tax area does not capture the welfare-rights amendments, and the WJP's rule-of-law score does not separate the court-led liberalization of personal conduct from the statute-led hardening of criminal law [synthesis].
- **Variation inside the jurisdiction** (by state; the federal layer is uniform, but the states differ in what is left to them) [synthesis, from the sources cited]:
  - **Abortion:** decriminalized in 24 entities, still a crime in 8 (September 2026); in the targets that reuse this profile, **Baja California** (2021) and **Baja California Sur** (2022) decriminalized; in the Sonora target, **Sonora still penalizes** while **Chihuahua decriminalized** in 2024 [practitioner: Crónica, read; Chihuahua's year from a search summary]. The post-war México target holds most of the 8 that still penalize (Aguascalientes, Querétaro, Durango, Guanajuato, Morelos, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas) [synthesis, from the territory file's list of the 23].
  - **Judicial elections:** the states that renewed their judiciaries in 2025 against those whose judges are all elected in June 2028 (see the Baja California profile; the sources disagree on how many states held local judicial elections in 2025).
  - **Civil and family procedure:** each state moves to the national code on its own schedule, by April 1, 2027 at the latest (Baja California: Rosarito first, Tijuana in March 2027).
  - **The restricted zone:** foreigners may not hold direct title within 100 km of a border or 50 km of a coast [official, read]. On a peninsula no wider than about 250 km, most of both Bajas falls inside it, so foreign-owned homes there are held in trust [synthesis; the peninsula's width and the trust mechanism are from general knowledge, claim, not consulted]. In the interior states it bites much less [synthesis].
  - **The northern border:** a federal decree of tax incentives for the northern border region (VAT and income tax) and a separate border minimum-wage zone are from general knowledge, not checked here [claim, not consulted]. If they hold, Baja California sits inside a distinct federal fiscal regime and Baja California Sur outside it.
  - **Security:** victimization in 2025 ranged from 13,462 per 100,000 adults in Chiapas to 34,930 in Mexico City (the State of México 31,889); Baja California 23,095 (up from 20,647), Baja California Sur 19,505 (up from 18,827); in the Sonora target, Sonora 22,433 and Chihuahua 23,395 [measured: ENVIPE 2026, read].
  - **Rule of law:** WJP Mexico States 2023-2024: Querétaro highest (0.49); the State of México, Morelos, Quintana Roo, Mexico City and Guerrero lowest (0.35-0.37) [measured: Expansión, read]; Baja California 0.40 (25th) [measured: Zeta Tijuana, read].
