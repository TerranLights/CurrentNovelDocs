# Russia — Federal Fractures and the Far Eastern Question (survey)

**Stage 1 frameworks survey. Started 2026-09-27, first research pass only — not a finished analysis.**
Companion material-base survey not yet started (Stage 2). No map-data package exists yet (Stage 3).
`y-files/Map Files/Russia/` contains no usable prior work — everything found there on inspection is stray,
misplaced Latin America drafting (Yucatán/Belize/Guatemala/Mesoamerica content) left over from before that
region had its own folder; not a Russia deliverable of any kind. Russia is a genuine blank slate.

**This survey has a specific, load-bearing first job**: East Asia's own survey (`East Asia - Frontiers,
Civilizational Spheres and the Han Core (survey).md`, §3.5) deliberately deferred three border seams to
Russia's future work and marked the ground unclaimed on that region's map — **Outer Manchuria**, **Sakhalin
and the Kurils**, and **Buryatia and Tuva**. All three are resolved below (§2), with recommendations, not
assertions — the same "research proposes, author disposes" posture as every prior seam-check in this project.

## Headline findings

1. **Woodard's Doctrine of First Effective Settlement doesn't apply here, for the same reason it didn't apply
   to East Asia — and the fix is the same fix.** Russia is not a settler colony arriving in open territory; it
   is a centuries-old imperial state that annexed adjacent civilizations (Tatar, Bashkir, Caucasian, Siberian,
   Mongolic) outward from a Slavic core. Per `METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md`, this survey uses **tiered
   ethnic identity** (Tier 1: Russian/Slavic core, Turkic-Volga, North Caucasian, Turkic-Siberian, Mongolic,
   Tungusic/Paleo-Siberian; Tier 2: the ~190 individual recognized ethnicities inside those) rather than a
   founding-settlement doctrine.
2. **The real fragmentation logic in the real-world literature is economic and regional, not primarily
   ethnic — this is this survey's version of Oceania's "import lifeline" crux.** Real 2019 data: only **five**
   of Russia's ~85 federal subjects produced over 70% of federal budget revenue — the oil/gas okrugs
   Khanty-Mansi and Yamalo-Nenets, Moscow, St. Petersburg, and Tatarstan. **72 of 85 subjects are net
   recipients of federal subsidy.** Per-capita GRP disparity between the richest region (oil-producing Nenets
   AO) and the poorest (Ingushetia) is **62-to-1**. A collapse scenario is a story about which regions can
   feed themselves from their own resource base and which can't — not primarily a story about which regions
   have a different founding ethnicity, though the two maps partially overlap (see finding 4).
3. **The strongest, cleanest ethnic-secession cases are almost all in the North Caucasus, and Chechnya is the
   only one with a real, fought, modern war behind it.** Chechnya (96% Chechen, ~2% Russian) and Ingushetia
   (93% Ingush, ~1% Russian) are functionally mono-ethnic and both fought Russia for independence in living
   memory (First and Second Chechen Wars, 1994-2009). Dagestan is the opposite case in the same region — no
   single ethnic majority at all, ~30+ languages, which makes it internally incoherent as a single successor
   state on its own terms even though it is only 3.6% ethnic Russian.
4. **Tuva has the single strongest non-Caucasus ethnic claim in the country, and it already wrote the
   paperwork.** 88.7% ethnic Tuvan, and Tuva's own 1993 constitution asserted a right of secession from the
   Russian Federation outright — a documented, legally-articulated claim, not just sentiment. This is the
   direct answer to the northern-Mongolic-dimension question East Asia's survey flagged as undefined.
5. **Siberian and Far Eastern regionalism is a real, separate, non-ethnic phenomenon with 160 years of history
   and an actual historical precedent for exactly the territory East Asia is waiting on.** "Oblastnichestvo"
   (Siberian regionalism), running from the 1860s through the Russian Civil War, treated Siberia as an
   internal colony of Moscow and produced a real short-lived state in 1918 (the Provisional Siberian
   Government, Omsk). More directly useful: the **Far Eastern Republic (1920-22)** was a real buffer state
   built from almost exactly the territory in question here — Amur, Transbaikal, Kamchatka, **Sakhalin**, and
   **Primorye** — which means Outer Manchuria and Sakhalin/the Kurils both have a single real-world precedent
   for existing together as one polity, not two separate seams. See §2.1-2.2.
6. **A rump "core Russia" anchored on Moscow/St. Petersburg/Central Russia is at least as well-evidenced as
   any other region's anchor core.** Moscow (13.0M) and St. Petersburg (5.6M) are two of the five real donor
   regions above; the Central Economic Region is Rosstat's own densest, most industrially developed
   macro-region. This is the same structural role the Han core plays for the Sinian Federation and various US
   successor states play for North America — not yet fully mapped here (Stage 3 work), but the evidence
   supports it existing.

## Section 1 — Method: why tiered ethnic identity, not Woodard's doctrine

Per `METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md`, Woodard's Doctrine of First Effective Settlement requires an arriving
population founding a self-perpetuating society in territory that was, from the colonizing power's point of
view, open. Russia's expansion is the same structural shape as Qing China's, not as Anglo-America's: **a
continuous, centuries-old imperial core (Slavic/Orthodox, radiating from Moscow and Novgorod) annexed adjacent,
older, already-populated civilizations** — Volga Turkic khanates (Kazan fell 1552, Astrakhan 1556), the North
Caucasus (conquered over the 18th-19th centuries, resisted the longest), Siberia (Cossack conquest from the
1580s over existing Siberian Tatar, Buryat, Yakut, and other populations), and Tuva (only annexed outright in
1944, within living memory). There is no "first effective settlement" to point at for most of this territory —
there is a state that arrived, and the peoples it arrived onto.

**Tier 1 groupings this survey uses**, following the same table format East Asia's survey built:

| Tier 1 | Example Tier 2 identities | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Russian/Slavic core | Great Russians (regional sub-identities: Pomors, Cossacks, Siberians-as-settlers) | Central Russia, most of Siberia's settler population, the two capitals |
| Volga-Ural Turkic | Tatars, Bashkirs, Chuvash (Turkic but distinct, Orthodox-influenced), Mari, Udmurt, Mordvin (the last three Finno-Ugric, not Turkic — grouped geographically not linguistically) | Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Chuvashia, Mari El, Udmurtia, Mordovia |
| North Caucasian | Chechens, Ingush, ~30 Dagestani peoples (Avar, Dargin, Kumyk, Lezgin...), Circassian peoples, Ossetians (Indo-European, the exception in this row) | Chechnya, Ingushetia, Dagestan, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay-Cherkessia, North Ossetia |
| Turkic-Siberian | Tuvans, Khakas, Altaians, Yakuts/Sakha | Tuva, Khakassia, Altai Republic, Sakha |
| Mongolic | Buryats, Kalmyks | Buryatia, Kalmykia |
| Tungusic / Paleo-Siberian / Indigenous Far North | Evenki, Nenets, Chukchi, and dozens of small-population peoples across the Far North and Far East | Scattered okrugs and republics, Chukotka, Nenets AO, Yamalo-Nenets AO |

**Two cautions, ported directly from the East Asia method note**: Tier 2 has far more resolution than a
country-count map can carry (Dagestan alone has ~30 recognized peoples — it will need to be treated as one
unit regardless, the same way China's Min Chinese splits five ways under one label). And Tier 1 groupings are
themselves contestable — Ossetians are Indo-European, not Turkic or Caucasian by language family, but sit
geographically and politically inside "the North Caucasus" as a bloc; this survey groups them there on the
same geographic-coherence logic the method document uses elsewhere, not a linguistic claim.

## Section 2 — The three East Asia seams, resolved

### 2.1 Outer Manchuria (Primorsky Krai, southern Khabarovsk Krai, the Jewish Autonomous Oblast, Amur Oblast)

**The real numbers.** Overwhelmingly ethnic-Russian — over 90% in most constituent regions (95.17% in Amur
Oblast specifically, 2020 census). Primorsky Krai: 1.85M people. Khabarovsk Krai: 1.29M. Jewish Autonomous
Oblast: 150,453 (2021 census), declining to an estimated 145,802 by 2024 — despite the name, Jewish population
is now a small minority; it was a 1934 Soviet settlement project, not an organic community, and functions
today as an ordinary Russian-majority oblast with a legacy name.

**The historical hook, and it's a strong one.** This is almost exactly the territory of the real **Far Eastern
Republic (April 1920 - November 1922)** — a nominally independent buffer state the RSFSR itself created to
avoid direct conflict with Japan, built from Amur, Transbaikal, Kamchatka, Sakhalin, and Primorye, capital
first at Verkhneudinsk (today's Ulan-Ude, Buryatia's capital) then Chita. It voluntarily re-merged with Soviet
Russia once the Japanese withdrew from Vladivostok in 1922. **This is a real, sourced, twentieth-century
precedent for exactly this ground existing as its own polity** — not a settler-nation origin story, but a
regionalist/buffer-state one, the same category as Siberian oblastnichestvo (§4).

**East Asia's own framing, applied here.** East Asia's Manchuria entry (their #4) is explicitly "the strongest
case against" merging into a larger polity precisely because it's ethnically identical to its neighbor but has
a distinct institutional/economic history — the same logic applies here in reverse. Outer Manchuria's
population is ethnically identical to central Russia (not a Tier-1-distinct people), so by the tiered-identity
method it has **no ethnic case for independence at all** — its case, if any, is purely regionalist, resting on
distance from Moscow, a resource-extraction economy, and the historical buffer-state precedent.

**Recommendation**: treat this territory as the core of its own **Russian Far Eastern successor state** —
not reabsorbed into a "core Russia" (which per §6 below is plausibly anchored 6,000+ km away in
Moscow/St. Petersburg, a genuinely non-contiguous-feeling distance even before any collapse), and **not** any
kind of merge with East Asia's Manchuria/Chuang Guandong region despite the shared name and adjacency — the
populations are different Tier 1 groups (Han vs. Russian) on either side of a real historical treaty line
(Aigun 1858 / Peking 1860) that East Asia's own survey already treats as the hard border. This directly
resolves the seam: East Asia's entry 4 stops at the Heilongjiang/Jilin line as already drawn; north of it is
this new Russian Far Eastern polity, not blank ground.

### 2.2 Sakhalin and the Kurils

**The real numbers.** Sakhalin Oblast (which administers the Kurils): 466,609 (2021 census). Economy is
dominated by oil and gas (Sakhalin-I, Sakhalin-II projects, historically with Exxon/Shell involvement) plus
rich fishing grounds — a genuine resource-export economic base, not a subsidy-dependent one.

**The live real-world dispute**, worth carrying into the setting as texture even if not redrawn: the four
southernmost Kurils (Kunashir, Iturup, Shikotan, the Habomai group) were Japanese under the 1855 Treaty of
Shimoda, transferred to the USSR under the 1945 Yalta framework and 1951 Treaty of Peace, and Japan has never
recognized that transfer as legitimate — a live, unresolved territorial dispute in the real present day.

**Recommendation**: fold Sakhalin Oblast whole into the same Russian Far Eastern successor state as §2.1 —
this project's whole-first-order-subdivision rule means the Kurils can't be split off from Sakhalin Oblast
administratively, and the historical Far Eastern Republic precedent (§2.1) already included Sakhalin in the
same polity as Amur/Primorye, so this isn't a new merge, it's the same one. **Author's call, flagged, not
resolved here**: whether Japan's East Asia entry gets a "claim/tension" note pointing at the southern Kurils
(a sphere-style dispute marker, not a redrawn border) — this would mirror how this project has flagged other
live real-world territorial disputes without necessarily resolving them on the map (e.g. this survey doesn't
touch East Asia's own borders). This closes the seam for East Asia's entry 11 (Hokkaido): its sphere note "the
Ainu populations there are in any case effectively gone" stands; the Kurils remain part of the Russian Far
Eastern polity, not contested Japanese ground, unless the author wants to add the dispute as texture.

### 2.3 Buryatia and Tuva

**Tuva: the strong ethnic case.** 336,651 people (2021 census), **88.7% ethnic Tuvan, 10.1% Russian** — one of
the most ethnically homogeneous republics in Russia outside the North Caucasus. Real, documented independence
politics: the Tuvan Popular Front formed in 1989 explicitly demanding secession; the Peoples' Party of
Sovereign Tuva followed in 1992; **Tuva's 1993 constitution asserted an explicit right of secession from the
Russian Federation** — a genuine legal/political claim, not just sentiment, though tempered in practice by
poverty and dependence on Moscow transfers. Tuvans are linguistically Turkic (Turkified Mongol descent) but
sit inside the Mongolic cultural sphere geographically and historically — Oirat and Tümed Mongol tribes
historically inhabited what's now Tuvan territory.

**Buryatia: the weak ethnic case, structurally identical to East Asia's Inner Mongolia.** 295,273 Buryats
living in Buryatia itself (556,000 Buryats total across Russia) against a republic that was ~69-74%
ethnic-Russian through the late Soviet period — Buryats are a minority in their own titular homeland, the
same demographic shape as East Asia's Inner Mongolia entry (78.7% Han today). Buryatia did **not** pursue or
gain independence during the Soviet collapse, unlike Tuva's explicit 1993 move. Buryats are genuinely
Mongolic — the same Tier 1 grouping as Mongolia itself — and Buryat intellectuals (Tsyben Zhamtsarano, Erdene
Batkhaan, already cited in East Asia's own survey) were central to building the Mongolian People's Republic's
own institutions, so the cultural/historical tie to Mongolia is real and deep, even though the modern
population balance argues against a clean independence case the way Tuva's does.

**Recommendation, directly answering East Asia's flagged question**: **Tuva is the pan-Mongolic sphere's
northern dimension** East Asia's survey said was undefined — give it its own polity (strong ethnic claim,
documented secession politics, a real 1993 constitutional precedent) connected to Mongolia and Inner Mongolia
by a cultural/sphere overlay, the same non-hard-border treatment this project has used for Isan, Zomia, and
the Patani Malay World elsewhere. **Buryatia is the genuine judgment call** — structurally it's Inner
Mongolia's mirror case, and the same two directions apply: (a) its own polity or part of the Far Eastern
Russian successor state on straightforward geographic/administrative grounds (its Russian-majority population
argues for this), or (b) folded toward the Mongolic sphere alongside Tuva and Mongolia on cultural/historical
grounds (the Buryat-intellectuals-built-Mongolia history argues for this). **This survey does not choose**,
matching East Asia's own explicit non-choice on Inner Mongolia's analogous case.

## Section 4 — Chechnya, Ingushetia, Dagestan: the North Caucasus is not one case

Grouping these three under "the Caucasus" the way a first pass might is a mistake this section corrects.
**Chechnya and Ingushetia are the clean cases** — mono-ethnic (96% and 93% respectively), with Chechnya
carrying the strongest fought-and-recent independence history in the entire country (First Chechen War
1994-96, ending in a Russian withdrawal and de facto independence; Second Chechen War 1999-2009, ending in
reconquest and the current Kadyrov-led arrangement). Chechen resistance to Russian expansion has real roots
back to Sheikh Mansur's pan-Caucasian resistance movement starting 1785 — this is not a new or manufactured
grievance.

**Dagestan is structurally different and harder**, the same way East Asia's Xinjiang turned out to contain
two countries under one whole-subdivision rule. No single ethnic group is a majority; Avar is the largest at
roughly 800,000 speakers, with Dargwa, Kumyk, Lezgin and dozens more sharing the rest across ~30 recognized
languages. A Dagestani successor state would be internally as ethnically fragmented as the empire it seceded
from, just at a smaller scale — worth flagging now as a design problem for Stage 3, not resolved here.

**Not researched this pass, flagged as open**: North Ossetia, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay-Cherkessia — the
three-way Circassian/Balkar/Karachay/Ossetian layer of the Caucasus, real and historically significant (the
Circassian genocide/expulsion of the 1860s is a load-bearing fact for that region's demography) but not
researched in this pass given the survey's priority was closing East Asia's three seams first.

## Section 5 — Tatarstan and Bashkortostan: the Volga case

**Tatarstan**: the most economically credible non-Caucasus secession case in the country — one of only five
real donor regions to the federal budget (§6), industrially developed, and the site of the most serious
1990s sovereignty movement outside Chechnya. Declared sovereignty 30 August 1990; the Ittifaq party pushed for
full national independence through the 1990s, though the republic ultimately settled for a negotiated
autonomy arrangement with Moscow rather than Chechnya's path.

**Bashkortostan**: declared sovereignty 11 October 1990 (name changed from "Bashkiria" 1992), driven partly by
a defensive reaction — fear of assimilation into Tatar identity (up to a third of Bashkirs speak Tatar as a
native language) as much as a reaction against Moscow. Structurally awkward as a successor state on its own
terms: Bashkirs are only the **third**-largest ethnic group inside Bashkortostan itself, behind both Russians
and Tatars — a demographic problem worth flagging alongside Dagestan's as a place the whole-subdivision rule
produces an internally contested polity rather than a clean nation.

## Section 6 — Siberian/Far Eastern regionalism, and Sakha/Yakutia's resource case

**Oblastnichestvo is a genuinely distinct phenomenon from every case above — it's regionalist, not ethnic.**
Originating with historian Afanasy Shchapov and activist Nikolai Yadrintsev in the 1860s (Yadrintsev's group
was arrested and exiled to Arkhangelsk for it), the movement argued Siberia was an internal colony of Moscow
and deserved political autonomy on those grounds — not a different-people claim, a different-treatment claim.
It produced a real state: the **Provisional Siberian Government**, declared at Omsk 17 June 1918 during the
Russian Civil War, controlling most of Siberia by mid-1918 before being absorbed into the wider White
movement. This is the direct forerunner logic behind §2.1's Far Eastern Republic recommendation — Russia has
two separate real precedents (1918 Omsk, 1920-22 Chita) for its eastern territories organizing themselves
as their own polity on regionalist rather than ethnic grounds.

**Sakha/Yakutia is the resource-wealth version of the same regionalist grievance, sharpened by scale.**
Russia's largest federal subject by area, with a Sakha (Yakut, Turkic-Siberian) ethnic majority. **Produces
~98% of Russia's diamonds and roughly a quarter of the world's** (via ALROSA), plus gold, oil, gas, uranium,
coal — genuinely extraordinary resource wealth for its population. Declared sovereignty August 1990, signed
the 1992 Federation Treaty securing nominal control over its own minerals, but **the real revenue flow still
runs to Moscow and to federally-linked private interests** — local activists explicitly frame the Moscow-Sakha
relationship as colonial. Real 2020s-era protests have specifically targeted proposed increases in federal
control over ALROSA. Despite this, actual rebellion likelihood is assessed as low in the sourced material — no
recent history of armed action, "weak levels of protest." **This is arguably the single best-evidenced case in
this entire survey for "grievance is real and well-documented, but doesn't currently translate into active
secessionism"** — useful as a calibration point against Chechnya's opposite case (grievance that did escalate
to war) when Stage 2/3 work has to decide how much of this friction becomes an actual drawn border versus a
sphere-style internal tension.

## Section 7 — The economic case for fragmentation, and the core that survives it

Restating headline finding 2 with the full source detail: Rosstat's own statistical framework already
partitions Russia into **twelve non-overlapping economic regions** (a system separate from both the 89
federal subjects and the federal-district structure), built around geographic proximity, complementary
economic activity, transport connectivity and resource base — i.e., Russia's own government already thinks
about the country's economic geography in almost exactly the region-sized units this project's maps need.
**Not yet pulled into this survey**: the actual list and boundaries of those twelve Rosstat economic regions —
flagged as the single most useful concrete next step for Stage 2, the same way Rosstat's federal-subject GRP
data would let a material-base survey directly identify which of the twelve regions are donors and which are
recipients, the load-bearing fact this survey has only established in outline (five donor subjects, 72
recipient subjects) rather than mapped.

**The core.** Moscow and St. Petersburg are two of the five real donor regions, and the Central Economic
Region generally is Rosstat's most industrially developed macro-region — there is a straightforward,
evidence-backed case for a rump "core Russia," ethnic-Russian, anchored on the two capitals, playing the same
structural role the Han core plays for the Sinian Federation. **Not yet resolved**: how far this core's
territory actually extends beyond the two capitals and the Central Economic Region — this needs the Rosstat
twelve-region breakdown above before it can be drawn with any confidence, and is explicitly Stage 2/3 work,
not settled here.

## Open questions for the next research pass

- **The Rosstat twelve-economic-region list itself** — not yet pulled in, and probably the single highest-value
  next search, since it would upgrade §7 from "there are donors and recipients" to an actual drawable regional
  skeleton, the way Skinner's macroregions did for East Asia's Han core.
- **Kaliningrad** — a genuine exclave (489,359 people, 2020), physically separated from the rest of Russia by
  Lithuania and Poland, not discussed in this pass at all. Almost certainly needs its own seam-style treatment
  given its total geographic isolation from any Russian successor state's contiguous territory.
- **North Ossetia, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay-Cherkessia** — flagged in §4, not researched this pass.
- **Kalmykia** — flagged in §3's table, not researched beyond the one-line note that it's Europe's only
  Buddhist-majority republic; a Mongolic Tier-1 case geographically separated from Buryatia/Tuva by the entire
  width of Russia, worth its own look.
- **Whether Japan's East Asia map entry should carry a Kuril-dispute tension marker** — flagged as an author's
  call in §2.2, not resolved here since it touches East Asia's own finished map, not Russia's.
- **The Tuva ethnic-Russian-share discrepancy** (10.1% vs. 20% across sources cited in §3) — needs a primary
  census-data check before being treated as settled.
- **Whether Buryatia goes with the Far Eastern Russian polity or the Mongolic sphere** — explicitly left open
  in §2.3, mirroring East Asia's own non-resolution of the structurally identical Inner Mongolia question.
- **No material-base survey exists yet for any part of Russia** — Stage 2 is entirely unstarted. Given how
  load-bearing the donor/recipient economic finding is here (more load-bearing than in any prior region's
  Stage 1, arguably), Stage 2 may matter more for Russia than it did for Oceania or East Asia.
