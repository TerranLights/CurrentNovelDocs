# Central Asia — Steppe, Oasis and the Fergana Tangle (survey)

**Stage 1 frameworks survey. Started 2026-09-27, first research pass only — not a finished analysis.**
Companion material-base survey not yet started (Stage 2). No map-data package exists yet (Stage 3).
Covers **Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan** — the five post-Soviet "-stans."
This is one half of the newly-opened **West Asia** region (Central Asia + the Middle East + Turkey +
Afghanistan); the Middle East/Turkey/Afghanistan half is a separate, parallel research pass, not covered
here. West Asia is explicitly scoped to **exclude South Asia** (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, etc.) — a
separate future region.

## Headline findings

1. **First effective settlement fails here for a third, distinct reason** — not East Asia's "no founding
   moment," not Southeast Asia's "the population stayed put and sent for the institutions," but **the borders
   were drawn on purpose, by an outside power, specifically to prevent the nation the ethnography would
   otherwise have produced.** The Soviet "national delimitation" of 1924–1936 carved five republics out of a
   Russian Turkestan that had no such internal lines, using ethnographers and linguists working from
   incomplete data under explicit political instructions to **prevent any one group — above all a unified
   pan-Turkic identity — from becoming too powerful.** ⚠️ This is a documented policy motive, not this
   survey's inference. See §1.
2. **The Fergana Valley is this survey's single load-bearing geographic fact** — the region's equivalent of
   Mainland Southeast Asia's Isan or East Asia's Hu Line, except here the discontinuity isn't a line, it's a
   *tangle*. Shared by Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan, it holds **eight enclaves/exclaves** isolating
   roughly 100,000 people from their own central governments, produced **~20 armed border incidents between
   1989 and 2009**, and triggered a **2021–2022 flare-up that killed an unknown number in the hundreds and
   displaced up to 136,000 people** — a live, currently-dangerous border, not a historical curiosity. See §2.
3. **The Tier 1 civilizational split is Turkic-nomadic vs. Persianate-sedentary, and it is real, old, and
   still named as an ethnonym pair today.** "Tajik" itself emerged as an ethnic term specifically to
   distinguish sedentary Iranians from nomadic Turkic groups by the 11th century — this is not a modern
   retrofit, it is the oldest attested version of exactly the identity split this survey needs. Tajikistan is
   the one Persianate/Iranic country in a Turkic region (Kazakh, Uzbek, Turkmen, Kyrgyz); its closest living
   cultural relative is Afghanistan's Dari-speaking population, outside this survey's scope but in the
   Middle East/Turkey/Afghanistan half of West Asia. See §3.
4. **The upstream/downstream water divide is this region's Guri Dam** — a real, structural, resource-based
   dependency the same shape as the Guri Dam/Bolívar case in this project's own Latin America survey, except
   regional rather than bilateral. Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan hold the headwaters of the Syr Darya and Amu
   Darya (90% of the region's water); Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan are downstream and
   agriculturally dependent on water the upstream states also want to dam for hydropower. The **Rogun Dam**
   (Tajikistan, designed to be the world's tallest) was a near-military flashpoint with Uzbekistan for over a
   decade before a 2018+ thaw. See §4.
5. **Remittance dependence on Russia is this region's version of Oceania's import-lifeline crux, and it is
   severe.** Remittances — nearly all of it Central Asian labor migrants working in Russia — made up **~30% of
   Tajikistan's GDP and ~28% of Kyrgyzstan's GDP** in recent pre-war-shock years (2022 figures ran even
   higher: 49.9% and 26.6% respectively in one citation). **98% of Tajikistan's remittances and 79% of
   Kyrgyzstan's originate in Russia specifically** — a single-point-of-failure dependency on the same country
   this project has already mapped as Russia, not a hypothetical trading partner. See §5.
6. **Kazakhstan alone holds this region's real resource wealth, and it is genuinely extraordinary**: the
   world's largest uranium producer (~40% of global output, 2025) and a top-12 global oil reserve holder
   (~30 billion barrels). Turkmenistan holds the world's fifth-largest natural gas reserves but converts that
   wealth into extreme political isolation rather than regional influence — a real, documented "hermit state"
   character, the most closed of all former Soviet states. See §6.
7. **The Aral Sea is this region's Angkor** — a real, dated, already-occurred ecological collapse sitting in
   the setting's own past, not a hypothetical post-collapse scenario. Soviet cotton-monoculture irrigation
   diverted ~75% of the Amu Darya's and Syr Darya's flow starting in the 1950s; the once-fourth-largest lake
   in the world has shrunk from 26,300 sq mi to roughly 8,000 sq km split into two remnants, one in each of
   Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Unlike Angkor, this collapse is still actively running (annual depth loss
   80–110 cm) and has two litigants, not one abandoned city. See §7.
8. **Two real, live border seams to this project's own already-built neighbors, flagged and not resolved
   here.** Kazakhstan shares the region's longest and most consequential border with **Russia**, already
   mapped. Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan all border China's **Xinjiang/East Turkestan**, this
   project's own East Asia map — and Xinjiang's own large Uyghur population has a real diaspora presence
   inside all three Central Asian states (Kazakhstan alone holds ~300,000 Uyghurs). See §8.

## Section 1 — Method: why first effective settlement fails, and what replaces it

Per this project's established practice (`METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md`), Woodard's Doctrine of First
Effective Settlement requires an arriving population founding a self-perpetuating society in territory that
was, from the colonizing power's own point of view, open. Central Asia fails this test in a way distinct from
every prior region this project has surveyed:

- **East Asia** failed the doctrine because there was no founding-settler moment at all — a continuous
  imperial core absorbed adjacent civilizations.
- **Mainland Southeast Asia** failed it because the defining cultural layer (Indianization) was *chosen* —
  the population stayed put and adopted institutions voluntarily.
- **Russia** failed it for the same structural reason as East Asia — an old imperial core annexing
  already-populated neighbors.
- **Central Asia fails it for a fourth, distinct reason: the borders themselves are a deliberate artifact of
  outside political engineering, not a settlement pattern, an adopted culture, or an organic imperial
  frontier.** The Soviet Union's "national delimitation," carried out mainly in 1924 with corrections
  continuing to 1936, divided what had been Russian Turkestan — a region with no such internal lines — into
  the five Soviet Socialist Republics that became today's Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan
  and Tajikistan. Ethnographers, linguists and party officials worked from incomplete data and **explicit
  political priorities, not lived reality** — borders were drawn not only to reflect ethnicity but
  specifically **to prevent any one group from becoming too powerful**, cutting across historic trade routes,
  mixed settlements, and cultural zones on purpose. **Confidence: high** — this is the consistent framing
  across multiple independent sources, not a single contested claim.

**Tier 1 grouping this survey uses, following the same table format Russia and East Asia established:**

| Tier 1 | Example Tier 2 identities | Where | Historical mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turkic-nomadic/pastoralist | Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Turkmen | Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Turkmenistan | Steppe pastoralist confederations |
| Turkic-sedentary/oasis | Uzbek | Uzbekistan | Silk Road trade-city, agricultural-oasis tradition |
| Persianate/Iranic | Tajik | Tajikistan | Sedentary, urban, bureaucratic — the region's one non-Turkic titular nationality |

**⚠️ This three-way split is a real, historically-attested distinction, not this survey's own invention, but
it is also a simplification.** Kazakhs, Kyrgyz and Turkmen share a nomadic-pastoralist heritage, but Uzbek
identity itself carries real internal sedentary/nomadic layering (Uzbek tribal groups had their own nomadic
past before urbanizing), and Tajik populations have historically lived intermixed with Uzbek populations in
the same oasis cities (Samarkand and Bukhara, both inside modern Uzbekistan, are historically major centers
of Tajik/Persian culture and language, not exclusively Uzbek ones — a live, sometimes politically sensitive
point). Treat the table as the organizing first pass, not a claim that these lines are clean on the ground —
the whole point of §2 below is that they are not.

## Section 2 — The Fergana Valley: the region's one real tangle

The Fergana Valley — shared by Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan — is this survey's single most
load-bearing geographic fact, in the same structural role Isan plays for Mainland Southeast Asia or the Hu
Line plays for East Asia. Unlike those two cases, the Fergana Valley's discontinuity isn't a line that could
in principle be redrawn cleanly — it is an irreducible tangle of enclaves inherited directly from the Soviet
delimitation described in §1.

**The enclaves, by the end of the Soviet era:**

| Enclave | Ethnicity | Surrounded by | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sokh | Uzbek | Kyrgyzstan | One of four Uzbek exclaves inside Kyrgyz territory; among the largest |
| Shakhimardan | Uzbek | Kyrgyzstan | |
| Chon-Gara (Chon-Kara) | Uzbek | Kyrgyzstan | |
| Tash-Tepa (Tash-Dobe) | Uzbek | Kyrgyzstan | |
| Vorukh | Tajik | Kyrgyzstan | ~30,000 people, fully engulfed; reduced from 19,000 to 14,500 hectares in a 2025 border agreement |
| Kairagach (Western Kalacha) | Tajik | Kyrgyzstan | |

**Eight enclaves total isolate roughly 100,000 people from their own central governments.** This is not a
dormant historical artifact: the valley saw **approximately 20 armed conflicts between 1989 and 2009**, and
**37 border incidents in 2014 alone** (Kyrgyzstan's own count). Most seriously, a **2021–2022 flare-up —
reportedly starting over a water dispute specifically around the Vorukh enclave — left an unknown death toll
in the hundreds and displaced up to 136,000 people.** **Confidence: high**, multiple independent sources
converge on these figures. ⚠️ **A 2025 Kyrgyz-Tajik border agreement has resolved some of this** (the Vorukh
reduction above is one term of it) — treat the valley as a still-live, currently-de-escalating situation, not
a frozen or fully-resolved one; this pass did not verify how complete or durable the 2025 agreement is.

**Why this matters for the map**: this project's whole-first-order-subdivision rule cannot make this problem
disappear the way it did for Myanmar's ethnic states (which already align to first-order lines) — the
Fergana enclaves are sub-subdivision-level exclaves by nature, the same structural category as Kaliningrad
(Russia survey, flagged and unresolved there too) but multiplied eightfold and interlocking three countries
at once. **This needs its own design decision at Stage 3, not a default "whole subdivisions only" treatment**
— flagged here, not resolved.

### 2.1 Why Osh and Jalal-Abad are Kyrgyz territory at all — a real, sourced answer, added 2026-09-28

⚠️ **Added on a targeted research pass after the author asked directly why Kyrgyzstan's own share of the
valley floor (Osh and Jalal-Abad specifically) stayed Kyrgyz rather than Uzbek**, given that Osh itself was
**94% Uzbek by the 1923 census** — not a demographic accident, and not settled on ethnic grounds at all.

**The Soviet delimitation committees' own stated rule of thumb** was to give nomadic-pastoralist areas to the
Kyrgyz and settled agricultural/urban areas to the Uzbeks — which would have put Osh, an old, dense Silk
Road-era settled town, in Uzbekistan. **Osh and Jalal-Abad are the load-bearing exception to that rule, and
the reason is explicitly economic, not ethnic.** At the 3rd Plenary Session of the Central Committee of the
Communist Party of Turkestan (14 September 1924), the delimitation was described in its own words as made
**"with some infringement of national arithmetic taking into account the reasons of economic character
alone."** Kyrgyz representatives argued — and won the argument — that without these two urban/agricultural
centers, the new Kyrgyz Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic (built otherwise almost entirely from
high-mountain pastoral territory) would be **economically unviable as a republic**: no urban tax base, no
valley agricultural output, nothing to found a functioning administration on. The ethnic-demographic case for
Uzbekistan was acknowledged and overridden by this economic-viability argument.

**So the honest answer to "what keeps this territory Kyrgyz" is: nothing ethnic, and nothing geographic
either** — it's a 102-year-old (as of this survey's setting) deliberate Soviet administrative decision, made
explicitly *against* the demographic facts on the ground, to give an otherwise-unviable mountain republic a
real economic base. **Confidence: high** — the 1924 Plenary Session quote and the specific economic-viability
argument are corroborated by an academic source (Adeeb Khalid, "The Roots of Uzbekistan: Nation Making in the
Early Soviet Union," Central Asia Program); the 94% Osh figure and the general nomadic/settled rule-of-thumb
are corroborated separately (GeoHistory, Minority Rights Group, Wikipedia's own Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan border
article). **This is the same shape of finding as this project's own East Asia and Latin America work** —
a modern political fact resting on a specific, datable, documented decision rather than an organic settlement
pattern — and it directly informs whether "Kyrgyzstan keeps its south" is a defensible call in any future map
built from this survey: **the same economic-viability logic that justified the original 1924 assignment is
still available as a reason to keep it whole**, independent of (and arguably stronger than) any ethnic or
terrain-based argument.

**Sources**: [Osh & The Fergana Valley: Diversity and Division — GeoHistory](https://geohistory.today/osh-fergana-valley/); [Uzbeks in Kyrgyzstan — Minority Rights Group](https://minorityrights.org/communities/uzbeks-2/); [Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan border — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyzstan%E2%80%93Uzbekistan_border); [The Roots of Uzbekistan: Nation Making in the Early Soviet Union — Adeeb Khalid, Central Asia Program](https://www.centralasiaprogram.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/CAP-Papers-167-May-2016-Khalid.pdf).

## Section 3 — The five countries, as they stand

### 3.1 Kazakhstan — the resource-rich north, and a demographic frontier with Russia

**Population**: commonly cited around 19–20 million; not independently re-verified to a single source this
pass. ⚠️

**The Russian-population question is real and shifting fast.** Ethnic Russians made up **23.7% of
Kazakhstan's population at the 2009 census**, down to **14.4% as of 2026** — a genuine demographic retreat,
not a stable minority. But that national figure masks a sharp regional split: **northern and eastern
Kazakhstan remain predominantly Slavic, with some areas as high as 90%.** The ethnic-Kazakh share of northern
Kazakhstan specifically has risen from 44% at independence to 66% by 2024, and continuing Russian
out-migration (driven by economic opportunity, infrastructure and climate, not overt conflict) is projected
to shrink the northern population by a further 20–25%. **Confidence: high** on the figures; **medium** on
how this trend continues, since it is ongoing and not a settled endpoint.

**Real resource wealth, concentrated here and nowhere else in the region:**
- **World's largest uranium producer since 2009** — ~40% of global output in 2025 (25,839 tU), 14% of world
  uranium resources.
- **Top-12 global proven oil reserves** (~30 billion barrels) — major fields Tengiz (one of the world's
  deepest/largest, a $48.5 billion expansion underway) and Kashagan (~35 billion barrels total reserves, one
  of the largest global discoveries in 50 years).
- Oil and gas revenues alone account for **40–50% of government revenue and 60–70% of export value.**

**A real, recent, violent political crisis**: the **January 2022 unrest** ("Bloody January," Qandy Qantar) —
starting as fuel-price protests in Mangystau Region, escalating to the storming of Almaty's airport and
government buildings, at least **238 people killed** in the government crackdown, and a Russian-led CSTO
military intervention to help quell it. This is the region's most significant recent instability event and a
real data point for how fragile the current political settlement is. **Confidence: high**, extensively
documented by multiple international outlets.

### 3.2 Uzbekistan — the sedentary/oasis core, and the region's most populous state

**Population**: ~34.9 million (2026 census) — the most populous Central Asian state by a wide margin.

Holds the historic Silk Road oasis cities — **Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva** — that this survey's §1 already
flags as historically as much Tajik/Persian cultural centers as Uzbek ones. Uzbekistan sits at the center of
the Fergana Valley tangle (§2) both as the ethnicity with the most exclaves-in-others'-territory (four) and
as one of the three states whose own soil hosts other countries' exclaves. Debt to China stood at **~$3
billion (20% of total foreign debt) by 2020** — present but described as more manageable than Kyrgyzstan's or
Tajikistan's exposure (§6).

**⚠️ Added 2026-09-28, a real research pass on Samarkand/Bukhara's Tajik population specifically, prompted by
the author's own question about the two cities' historical and current ethnic makeup.** The §1 "sometimes
politically sensitive" flag understated the scale of this: it's a real, well-documented, currently live
dispute, not a soft cultural footnote.

**The historical paper trail is direct evidence of deliberate reclassification, not organic demographic
change.** When Tajikistan was carved out as its own republic (1924/1929), it did not receive Samarkand or
Bukhara, despite both being the traditional Tajik/Persian cultural and political capitals of the whole
region — Samarkand was even briefly the Uzbek SSR's own first capital before Tashkent. In Samarkand city
specifically, the **1920 census recorded 44,758 Tajiks vs. just 3,301 Uzbeks; the 1926 census, six years
later, recorded 43,364 Uzbeks vs. just 10,716 Tajiks** — an almost exact demographic flip within a single
generation. Sourced accounts describe real coercive pressure behind reclassifications like this: Tajiks in
Bukhara reportedly could not obtain an internal passport without registering as Uzbek, and registering as
Uzbek also meant avoiding relocation to the poorer, more mountainous Tajik SSR.

**The dispute is current and large, not merely historical.** Uzbekistan's official 2021 figures put the
national Tajik population at 4.8% (~1.7 million); independent and Tajik-advocate estimates put it at
25–30% (8–11 million) — an order-of-magnitude gap. Tajik sources specifically claim **Samarkand is ~70%
ethnically Tajik and Bukhara ~90%+**, against official statistics showing both as solidly Uzbek. Uzbekistan
has not run a real census since 1989 and reportedly plans its first in early 2026 — the true figure has not
been officially recounted in nearly 40 years.

**But the geographic footprint of that disputed population is narrower than the city-level figures suggest,
and matters for how a future map should treat this.** At the whole-region (viloyat) level — the level this
project's whole-subdivision rule requires — Samarkand and Bukhara regions are **not** majority-Tajik: official
Bukhara Region-wide Tajik population is ~60,898 out of a ~2 million total; Samarkand Region is 63% rural
(2022) with Tajik concentration specifically inside Samarkand city's historic eastern old-town core, not
spread across the rural region. **The extreme disputed figures are a city-core phenomenon, structurally the
same shape as Osh city's near-even population sitting inside a majority-Kyrgyz Osh Region** (§2.1). Two
further, smaller Tajik-minority regions: **Surxondaryo is 83% Uzbek / 12.5% Tajik** (above the national
average, attributed to mountainous terrain near the Tajikistan border) — still a clear regional Uzbek
majority; **Qashqadaryo's own Tajik share was not found this pass**, flagged as an open gap, though its
Pamir-Alay foothill terrain makes some Tajik presence plausible.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-28: Uzbekistan, including Samarkand/Bukhara/Surxondaryo/Qashqadaryo, stays as its current
whole territory for now** — the region-level demographics don't support a different call, the same logic
already applied to Osh. **⚠️ Flagged for future development, not built or dated**: the author's own scenario is
that once Moscow's influence is gone, roughly the first half of the First Interwar Period could plausibly see
this same underlying Tajik/Uzbek grievance escalate into severe violence, potentially producing partial
district-level breakaways from Uzbekistan to Tajikistan — most plausibly specific districts within Samarkand
and Bukhara regions (city and near-city districts), not the regions wholesale. This is a real, sourced-enough
premise for a later session to design out properly (who, when, how it resolves), the same way Thailand's Deep
South insurgency was eventually turned into a designed post-2083 independence outcome — not something to
default into without that design pass.

**Sources**: [Tajiks in Uzbekistan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tajiks_in_Uzbekistan); [The Tajik Tragedy of Uzbekistan — The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2016/09/the-tajik-tragedy-of-uzbekistan/); [Uzbekistan: Tajik Language Under Pressure in Ancient Samarkand — Eurasianet](https://eurasianet.org/uzbekistan-tajik-language-under-pressure-in-ancient-samarkand); [Tajiks in Uzbekistan — Minority Rights Group](https://minorityrights.org/communities/tajiks-2/); [In Early 2026, Uzbekistan Aims to Conduct First Census Since 1989 — The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2025/09/in-early-2026-uzbekistan-aims-to-conduct-first-census-since-1989/); [Demographics of Uzbekistan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Uzbekistan).

### 3.3 Turkmenistan — gas wealth converted into isolation, not influence

**Population**: ~5.6 million.

**The fifth-largest natural gas reserves in the world**, but export capacity is limited by infrastructure
deficiencies, and — more distinctively — **the country has converted its energy wealth into extreme
political closure rather than regional leverage.** Constitutionally committed to "permanent neutrality"
(UN-recognized 1995), which keeps it out of Russian-led structures (CSTO, the Eurasian Economic Union) but
also makes it, by multiple sourced accounts, **the most isolated of all former Soviet states** — restricted
foreign travel for citizens, closed to most foreigners, gas revenue channeled into an internal security
apparatus and a pervasive propaganda system alongside deep domestic subsidies. **This is a real, distinctive
national character** — worth treating as genuinely different in kind from its neighbors' relationship to the
outside world, not just "another -stan." **Confidence: high**, consistent across Carnegie, CRS, and regional
academic sources.

**A genuine littoral-legal-status note**: Turkmenistan is one of the five signatories to the **2018 Convention
on the Legal Status of the Caspian Sea** (with Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Iran and Russia) — the Caspian's legal
status (a body of water with a special regime, neither sea nor lake) is **now settled after 22 years of
negotiation**, though seabed/subsoil delimitation between adjacent/opposite states still requires separate
bilateral agreements. ⚠️ **This survey did not find a live, current Kazakhstan-Turkmenistan seabed dispute
specifically** — the well-documented Caspian oil-field dispute this pass surfaced (Kyapaz/Serdar) is between
Azerbaijan and Turkmenistan, both partly or wholly outside this survey's scope (Azerbaijan is Caucasus/Europe
side, not Central Asia). Treat "the Caspian dispute" as **largely resolved at the multilateral level**, not
as ongoing regional tension, unless a future pass finds a specific live Kazakhstan-Turkmenistan seabed
disagreement this one didn't surface.

### 3.4 Kyrgyzstan — mountainous, poor, and the site of the region's worst recent ethnic violence

**Population**: commonly cited around 7 million; not independently re-verified to a single source this pass. ⚠️

One of the poorest states in the region. Remittances from labor migrants in Russia made up **~28% of GDP in
recent years (26.6% in 2024, down from a 2022 high)** — one of the most remittance-dependent economies in the
world (§5). Debt to China is estimated at **~$4 billion, ~40% of total GDP** — the highest debt-to-GDP
exposure in the region, flagged by multiple sources as the country most vulnerable to "debt-trap" dynamics
alongside Tajikistan.

**The 2010 Osh riots** are this region's single worst documented ethnic-violence event: clashes between
ethnic Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in southern Kyrgyzstan (Osh and Jalal-Abad) following the April 2010 overthrow of
President Bakiyev, escalating from a nighttime brawl to **at least 420–500 people killed, several thousand
injured, and 400,000 displaced** (100,000 of whom fled into Uzbekistan as refugees), with ~2,000 properties
destroyed. This is the clearest real precedent for how badly the Fergana Valley's ethnic geography (§2) can
actually break down when a political trigger (here, a national government collapse) hits it. **Confidence:
high**, extensively documented by HRW, FIDH and multiple other bodies with consistent figures.

### 3.5 Tajikistan — the Persianate exception, and the most remittance-dependent economy in the world

**Population**: ~10.8 million (2025). Four provinces (viloyats): Khatlon (3.70M), Sughd (2.92M), Districts of
Republican Subordination (2.20M), the capital Dushanbe (1.56M), and mountainous Kuhistoni Badakhshon/
Gorno-Badakhshan (233,600 — sparsely populated but territorially large, bordering both Afghanistan and
Xinjiang).

**The one non-Turkic titular nationality in the region**, and the historical core of §1's Persianate/Iranic
tier. The **Samanid dynasty (875–999)** — the last Persian-speaking empire to rule Central Asia — is treated
by scholarship as the landmark event in Tajik ethnogenesis, reviving written Persian language and literature
after the Arab conquest; "Tajik" itself emerged as an ethnonym by the 11th century specifically to
**distinguish sedentary Iranians from nomadic Turkic groups** — the Turk/Tajik distinction became a
stereotyped shorthand for nomadic-military-executive versus urban-civil-bureaucracy roles, i.e. this survey's
Tier 1 split is not a modern analytical retrofit, it is a thousand-year-old, self-applied ethnonym. The
**Timurid dynasty** (Tamerlane, based at Samarkand) later incorporated the whole region into one empire,
keeping Persian as the language of administration and high culture even under a Turkic-dynastic state —
worth noting as a real historical precedent for a single polity spanning the whole Tier 1 divide, should a
"greater Turkestan"-style sphere overlay ever be considered here the way this project has used sphere
overlays elsewhere (Tai Continuum, Mongolic World).

**The most remittance-dependent economy in the region, and among the most in the world**: remittances (98%
of it originating in Russia specifically) made up **~30% of GDP in recent pre-shock years, spiking to a cited
49.9% in 2022.** Owes an estimated **$3.3 billion to foreign creditors, half to China — 27% of total GDP.**
Holds the headwaters that make the Rogun Dam (§4) possible and contentious.

## Section 4 — Water politics: the upstream/downstream divide

**The Syr Darya and Amu Darya supply 90% of Central Asia's water**, both originating in the mountains of
Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan before flowing northwest through the downstream states. This produces a clean,
structural two-group division:

- **Upstream, water-rich, energy-poor**: Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan — lack the oil/gas wealth of their neighbors
  and depend on hydropower, which means they want to **dam and release water on an electricity-generation
  schedule** (heavy release in winter for heating/power demand).
- **Downstream, water-dependent, resource-rich**: Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan — hold most of the
  region's oil and gas, and depend on **summer river flow for irrigation-season agriculture** — the opposite
  seasonal pattern from what upstream hydropower wants.

**The Rogun Dam (Tajikistan)** is the flashpoint case, designed to be the world's tallest dam. Uzbekistan
"strongly opposed" its construction from shortly after the USSR's collapse through most of the post-Soviet
period, on the documented grounds that a completed Rogun would cause **both summer water scarcity and winter
flooding** downstream. **Real, recent de-escalation**: relations evolved "from near-military confrontation to
emerging cooperation," and Uzbek President Mirziyoyev formally **dropped objections to Rogun's construction**
during a state visit that also produced a Tajikistan-Uzbekistan border-demarcation agreement. **Confidence:
high** on the history of the conflict; **medium** on how durable the recent thaw is, since it is very recent
relative to decades of prior tension.

**This is this region's structural analogue to the Guri Dam/Bolívar case in this project's own Latin America
survey** — a real, resource-based dependency that creates leverage in one direction and vulnerability in the
other, worth carrying forward as load-bearing material-base logic once Stage 2 begins, not just historical
color.

## Section 5 — Remittance dependence on Russia: the import-lifeline crux

Following the pattern this project used Oceania's food/fuel import dependency as a structural crux, Central
Asia has its own version, and it is at least as severe: **dependence not on imported goods but on exported
labor**, specifically to Russia.

| Country | Remittances as % of GDP (recent years) | Share originating in Russia |
|---|---|---|
| Tajikistan | ~30% (49.9% cited for 2022) | 98% |
| Kyrgyzstan | ~28% (26.6% cited for 2024, down from a 2022 peak) | 79% |

**Real labor-migration scale**: as of recent counts, **more than 4.5 million workers from Uzbekistan, ~2.4
million from Tajikistan, and ~900,000 from Kyrgyzstan** were working in Russia. **The 2022 Russian invasion of
Ukraine already produced a measurable, faster-than-expected economic hit to the region** via reduced
remittance flows (the ruble's depreciation and Russia's own labor-market disruption both feeding through
directly to Central Asian household income) — i.e., **this dependency is not hypothetical, it has already
been stress-tested by a real recent shock and shown to transmit fast.** **Confidence: high**, multiple
independent sources (RFE/RL, World Bank-adjacent reporting, IOM) converge on the scale and direction of this
effect, though exact percentage points vary somewhat by year and source.

**Why this matters structurally**: any post-collapse scenario for this region has to reckon with what happens
to Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan specifically if the Russian labor market and remittance channel disappears or is
severed — this is arguably a bigger single fragility than any of this region's internal ethnic-border
tensions, the same way Oceania's import lifeline mattered more than any single island's internal politics.

## Section 6 — Resource wealth and its absence: Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, and everyone else

Restating headline findings 6 with full detail: **the region's material wealth is extremely unevenly
distributed**, in a pattern that maps closely (though not perfectly) onto the upstream/downstream water
divide in §4:

- **Kazakhstan**: world's largest uranium producer (~40% of global output), top-12 global oil reserves,
  oil/gas revenue is 40–50% of government revenue and 60–70% of export value. The region's clear economic
  core.
- **Turkmenistan**: fifth-largest gas reserves in the world, but wealth converts to isolation rather than
  regional economic integration (§3.3) — a genuinely different pattern from Kazakhstan's, worth treating
  separately rather than lumping "the two gas/oil states" together.
- **Uzbekistan**: the largest population and a more diversified economy than Kyrgyzstan or Tajikistan, but
  without Kazakhstan's hydrocarbon scale; carries real but comparatively manageable Chinese debt exposure
  (§3.2).
- **Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan**: no comparable hydrocarbon wealth, the highest Chinese debt-to-GDP exposure
  in the region (§3.4, §3.5), and by far the highest remittance dependence (§5). Their one major natural
  resource is the hydropower potential §4 already covers, which is itself a source of regional friction
  rather than straightforward wealth.

**China's Belt and Road footprint across all five states** is real and substantial: **over $1.3 trillion
invested globally since 2013**, with all five Central Asian states named as major beneficiaries, and a new
**China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway** under construction as of 2025 — a real, current piece of
infrastructure that would, if completed, materially change regional connectivity in a way worth remembering
for any post-collapse trade-network logic. **Confidence: high** on the investment/debt figures; the
debt-to-GDP percentages above (Tajikistan 27%, Kyrgyzstan 40%, Uzbekistan 20%) come from slightly different
years/sources and should be treated as roughly-comparable orders of magnitude, not precisely simultaneous
snapshots.

## Section 7 — The Aral Sea: a collapse already in the past

**Restating headline finding 7 in full.** Soviet planners diverted roughly **75% of the Amu Darya's and Syr
Darya's combined flow starting in the 1950s** to grow cotton, alfalfa and rice in the arid land south and
east of the Aral Sea — then the world's **fourth-largest lake** (26,300 sq mi) and one of the most profitable
inland fisheries on Earth. **Today's surface area is roughly 8,000 sq km**, volume down to 75 cubic km,
maximum depth just 20 m, and the lake has **physically split into two remnants** straddling the
Kazakhstan-Uzbekistan border — still actively shrinking (**80–110 cm of depth loss annually**). **Some
stabilization has occurred**: the smaller "Little Aral," wholly within Kazakhstan, was stabilized via a
World-Bank-funded dyke completed in 2005, and Uzbekistan has restored just over half of ~3 million degraded
hectares in recent years. **Confidence: high**, consistent figures across UNCCD, National Geographic, and
multiple regional-policy sources.

**Why this belongs in the survey the way Angkor belongs in Mainland Southeast Asia's**: this is a real,
dated, already-occurred ecological collapse with two litigant successor states (Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan)
still living with its consequences and still working to partially reverse it — a genuine collapse-in-the-
setting's-own-past, distinct from every purely hypothetical post-2083-war scenario elsewhere in this project.
**Unlike Angkor, it is not finished** — the collapse is ongoing, which may make it a more useful piece of
in-world lore than a purely historical one (an active process characters could reference, monitor, or even
work to reverse further, rather than a closed chapter).

## Section 8 — Two real border seams to this project's own neighbors, flagged and not resolved

### 8.1 Russia (already mapped)

**Kazakhstan shares the region's longest and most consequential border with Russia** — the two countries
share the deepest post-Soviet integration history in the region (Kazakhstan was, alongside Belarus, one of
the core members of the Eurasian Economic Union), and northern Kazakhstan's own demographic Slavic-majority
pockets (§3.1) sit directly against that border. **No specific border-line recommendation is made here** —
this survey only flags that Kazakhstan's northern edge is the one place in this whole region that touches
this project's already-built Russia map directly, and any future Central Asia map-data work should stay
consistent with Russia's existing southern border as drawn.

### 8.2 China / Xinjiang / East Turkestan (already mapped, East Asia survey)

**Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan all border China's Xinjiang region** — this project's own East Asia
survey already settled that **Xinjiang/East Turkestan never joins the Sinian Federation** and remains its own
separate polity. Xinjiang's population is roughly **40% Han and 40% Uyghur** today (a reversal from
pre-1950s Uyghur majority of over 90%), and **the Uyghur diaspora inside Central Asia is real and
substantial**: an estimated **~300,000–306,000 Uyghurs in Kazakhstan alone**, with further populations
(~50,000–60,000 each, estimates vary and likely undercount due to long-standing cross-border intermarriage
and integration) in Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan. **This is a genuine, live cross-border ethnic-diaspora
question worth flagging for the author explicitly**, structurally similar in shape to how Zhuang/Tai
populations cross the East Asia/Mainland Southeast Asia scope line, or how Kelantan-Pattani Malay crosses the
Mainland/Maritime Southeast Asia line: **should a Uyghur/East Turkestan cultural sphere overlay extend into
Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan the way this project has used sphere overlays elsewhere?** ⚠️ Not
resolved here — this survey only establishes that the population and border-adjacency facts support
considering it, the same "research proposes" posture as every other seam-flag in this project.

## Open questions for the next research pass

- **The Fergana Valley's design treatment at Stage 3** (§2) — the whole-first-order-subdivision rule cannot
  resolve eight interlocking sub-national enclaves the way it resolved Myanmar's ethnic states; this needs a
  real design decision (sphere overlay? hatched pockets, Wa/Hakka-style? accept the enclaves as drawn
  sub-features despite the whole-subdivision rule?) before any Central Asia map-data package can be built.
- **How durable the 2025 Kyrgyz-Tajik border agreement and the Uzbek-Tajik Rogun thaw actually are** — both
  are very recent relative to decades of prior conflict; this pass did not find grounds to treat either as
  fully stable long-term fact rather than a current, possibly fragile state.
- **Whether a Uyghur/East Turkestan cultural sphere should extend into Central Asia** (§8.2) — flagged, not
  decided, directly parallel to this project's other cross-region sphere questions.
- **Exact, single-sourced current population figures for Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan** — this pass used commonly
  cited approximate figures (~19–20M and ~7M respectively) without pinning either to one specific recent
  census the way Uzbekistan (34.9M, 2026 census), Turkmenistan (~5.6M) and Tajikistan (10.8M, 2025) were.
- **No material-base survey exists yet for Central Asia** — Stage 2 is entirely unstarted. Given how
  load-bearing the water-politics (§4) and remittance-dependency (§5) findings already are, Stage 2 may
  matter as much here as it does for Russia (flagged as unusually load-bearing in that survey too).
- **Administrative subdivision counts for Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Turkmenistan** — this pass got Tajikistan's
  four viloyats and partial figures for a few Uzbek regions, but did not compile full first-order subdivision
  lists for any of the five countries. Needed before Stage 3 map data.
- **The Middle East/Turkey/Afghanistan half of West Asia** is a separate, parallel research effort — this
  survey does not touch it, but flags that Afghanistan specifically borders Tajikistan directly (Gorno-
  Badakhshan) and shares the Persianate/Dari-Tajik cultural connection noted in §3.5 — a natural seam-check
  once that survey exists, the same category of cross-file consistency check this project ran between the
  Mainland Southeast Asia and East Asia surveys over the Tai/Zhuang continuum.
