## Headline findings

1. **Woodard's First Effective Settlement fails here for a fifth, distinct reason: the Indus basin is a
   palimpsest, and the layer that fixed today's map is administrative and hydraulic, not a founding
   settlement.** Not East Asia's "no founding moment," not Southeast Asia's "institutions were chosen," not
   Central Asia's "borders drawn on purpose to prevent a nation" — but **twenty-five centuries of successive
   conquest from the northwest (Achaemenid, Macedonian, Kushan, Arab, Ghaznavid, Mughal, Durrani, Sikh), then
   a British layer of canal colonies, cantonments and recruitment that re-sorted the plain, then a 1947
   population exchange.** The doctrine survives only as a *local* tool, for two modern, datable settler
   events (the British canal colonies; the 1947 Muhajir settlement of Karachi and Hyderabad). See §1.
2. **The load-bearing structural fact is a three-part geography: an irrigated Indo-Aryan plain (Punjab and
   Sindh, 74% of the population), a Pashtun and Baloch "rim" of highland and desert to the west and
   southwest (22.5% of the population on 51% of the area), and a Karakoram-Himalaya "roof" (Gilgit-Baltistan,
   Azad Kashmir, Chitral).** The rim is Lattimore's "reservoir" in the sense the METHOD file already
   borrows: the frontier belt that has repeatedly supplied conquerors of the plain and, since the British,
   recruits for the plain's army. The plain holds the people and the water; the rim holds the conflict, the
   gas and the border. See §1.3 and §2.
3. **⚠️ The settled Balochistan scenario rests on a premise this survey's numbers only half support.** The
   Middle East survey (§9.2) calls Pakistan's Balochistan "independently Baloch-majority." The 2023 census
   gives **Balochi 39.91%, Pashto 34.03%, Brahui 17.22%**. It is Baloch-*plus-Brahui* majority (57.1%) only if
   the Brahui are grouped with the Baloch (Baloch nationalists do; the census does not), and even then
   roughly a third of the province, including **Quetta District (60.0% Pashto)** and ten northern districts
   at 86-99.9% Pashto, is Pashtun. The province is also 6.0% of Pakistan's population on 39.4% of its area at
   43 people per square kilometer. This is not a reason to reverse the author's call; it is a fact the
   scenario should carry explicitly. See §7.1.
4. **The Pashtun question is bigger inside Pakistan than outside it, and it is not primarily separatist.**
   Pashto is the mother tongue of about **44.9 million Pakistanis** (18.15% of 247.5 million, 2023; derived), a
   figure that plausibly exceeds the Pashtun population of Afghanistan itself (⚠️ Afghan population not
   sourced in this pass). Afghanistan has rejected the Durand Line every year since 1949; the Pakistani
   state completed about 98% of a border fence by April 2023; and in **February 2026 Pakistan struck Kabul
   and Kandahar and its defense minister called it "open war."** But the live armed actor in Khyber
   Pakhtunkhwa (the TTP) is Islamist rather than ethnonational, and the elected provincial government (PTI,
   Chief Minister Sohail Afridi since 13 October 2025) is federalist-oppositional, not secessionist. See §7.2.
5. **The army is the state's actual cohesion mechanism, and in November 2025 it was made
   constitutional.** The 27th Amendment (passed 13 November 2025, 234-4 in the National Assembly) created a
   Chief of Defence Forces (Field Marshal Asim Munir), abolished the post of Chairman Joint Chiefs, gave the
   CDF lifetime legal immunity, and set up a Federal Constitutional Court above the Supreme Court's
   constitutional jurisdiction. Historically **70-75% of rank-and-file is drawn from Punjab and 15-21% from
   the Pashtun north** (a commonly cited estimate; ⚠️ dated and not officially published). Any scenario in
   which Pakistan fractures has to say what happens to this institution first. See §9.
6. **Water is the plain's single point of failure, and it just became a legal and military variable.** About
   80% of Indus-system flow is allocated to Pakistan under the 1960 treaty; usable live storage is only
   **11.5-13.0 million acre-feet, roughly 30 days of flow** (approximate, search-derived). India placed the
   treaty "in abeyance" in April 2025; a five-member Court of Arbitration ruled on **31 August-1 September
   2026** that India has no such power, and India rejected the ruling. Meanwhile the **March-April 2025
   Cholistan canals crisis** showed that Sindh will mobilize mass protest over intra-Pakistan water shares
   too, and forced the federal government to shelve the plan on 24 April 2025. See §8.
7. **Karachi is the one place where the "whole first-order subdivision" rule and the human geography collide
   hardest, and it is city-state-scale.** Karachi Division holds **20.38 million people (2023), 36.6% of
   Sindh**, is **50.67% Urdu-speaking and only 11.12% Sindhi-speaking**, and by common (⚠️ unverified)
   Wikipedia-level figures accounts for roughly a quarter of Pakistan's GDP and most of its sea trade. Sindh
   without Karachi is not the same country as Sindh with it, and Karachi without Sindh has no water. See
   §7.5.
8. **The five fracture lines are not equal, and the ranking is the survey's most useful output.** By live
   violence and organized separatist intent: Balochistan (strongest), then Pashtun-belt militancy
   (violent but Islamist and cross-border). By mass political mobilization without arms: Sindh (Sindhudesh
   tradition, canal protests) and, newly, Azad Kashmir (Joint Awami Action Committee, 40+ dead by 28 July
   2026). By identity without armed movement: the Saraiki belt (a large but internally split demand).
   Gilgit-Baltistan is a constitutional limbo rather than a fracture. See §7 and the menu in §11.

## Section 1 — Method: why first effective settlement fails, and what replaces it

### 1.1 The doctrine, tested

Per `METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md`, Woodard's doctrine needs an arriving population founding a
self-perpetuating society in territory that was, from the founder's point of view, open. The Indus valley
never was open:

- **Mehrgarh (7000-5500 BCE) and the mature Indus cities (2600-1900 BCE, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro)** are
  older than any layer that survives in the language map; the civilization was abandoned around 1700 BCE
  (per the History of Pakistan chronology). Nobody today claims descent as a founding culture.
- **Every subsequent layer arrived from the northwest and sat on top of the last one:** Indo-Aryan
  settlement of the Punjab (c. 1500-500 BCE); the Achaemenid satrapies of Gandhara, Hindush and Arachosia
  (Cyrus on the Makran coast 542 BCE; Darius 518-516 BCE); Alexander through the Khyber Pass (326 BCE); the
  Mauryan, Indo-Greek (capital at Sialkot), Indo-Scythian, Indo-Parthian and Kushan states; the Alchon Huns;
  the Arab conquest of Makran (643 CE) and Sindh (Muhammad ibn al-Qasim, 712 CE); the Ghaznavids (c. 999),
  Ghurids, the Delhi Sultanate (1206-1526), the Mughals (1526-1707), the Durrani Empire (from 1747), and the
  Sikh Empire (1799-1849).
- **The British annexed Sindh in 1843 and the Punjab in 1849** and ruled directly after 1857 (dates per the
  History of Pakistan chronology).

**Result: no single "first effective settlement" can be named, and the doctrine's usual test (a small
founding population that sets lasting institutions regardless of later arrivals) returns the wrong answer
almost everywhere.** Punjabi and Sindhi are Indo-Aryan languages whose speakers descend from every layer
above; Pashto and Balochi are Iranic languages whose speakers arrived and spread at different times (the
Baloch's own origin tradition is a migration from the west; ⚠️ contested, not researched this pass).

### 1.2 Where the doctrine does work: two modern settler events

The doctrine fits two datable, settler-colonial-shaped events, and both matter for the map:

1. **The British canal colonies (c. 1885-1940; background knowledge, not re-verified this pass).** Irrigation
   turned the Bari, Rechna, Chaj and Sind Sagar doabs of the western Punjab and the Bahawalpur plain into
   settled farmland by moving peasant colonists in from central and eastern Punjab. The evidence is in the
   2023 language map: **Bahawalpur Division is only 49.1% Saraiki and 42.6% Punjabi**, despite being
   historically a Saraiki-speaking region, and the 1941 Bahawalpur State population growth is attributed to
   irrigation development (Bahawalpur princely-state article). A settler layer, laid down by an imperial
   power, that then stayed.
2. **The 1947 Muhajir settlement.** About 1.1 million Muslims from the United Provinces, Bombay Presidency,
   Delhi and Rajasthan settled in Sindh in the first wave (August-November 1947), about half in Karachi. By
   the 1951 census migrants were **57% of Karachi, 65% of Hyderabad and 55% of Sukkur**, and held 33 of 95
   senior civil-service posts and 36 of the 42 largest private companies (Muhajir people article). Urdu
   replaced Sindhi as Karachi's majority language (Sindhi fell from 51% in 1941 to 8.5% in 1951). This is a
   real, dated, Woodard-shaped founding event, and it is the reason Karachi is a separate question from
   Sindh.

### 1.3 The replacement framework proposed here: Plain, Rim and Roof (⚠️ proposal, not a decision)

The Central Asia survey proposed a Turkic-nomadic vs. Persianate-sedentary Tier 1. The Indus equivalent is
not primarily linguistic; it is **hydraulic-topographic**, with language as Tier 2:

| Tier 1 (position in the Indus system) | Tier 2 identities (examples) | Where | Historical mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The irrigated plain** (Indus and its five tributaries, delta, canal colonies) | Punjabi, Saraiki, Sindhi, Muhajir/Urdu, Hindu Sindhi, Christian | Punjab, Sindh, ICT | Settled agriculture, taxation, the canal-and-cantonment state |
| **The rim** (Sulaiman, Kirthar, Hindu Kush foothills; Balochistan plateau; Makran; the Khyber-Bolan-Gomal gateways) | Pashtun (many tribes), Baloch, Brahui, Hazara (Quetta) | KP, ex-FATA, Balochistan, northern Sindh and DG Khan fringes | Pastoralism and tribal confederation; the "reservoir" that supplied conquerors and later colonial-army recruits |
| **The roof** (Karakoram, Himalaya, Hindu Kush, Pamir-adjacent) | Shina, Balti, Burusho, Wakhi, Khowar, Kalash, Kohistani, Pahari-Pothwari, Kashmiri, Gojri | Gilgit-Baltistan, Chitral, Kohistan, Azad Kashmir | Small valley polities; the headwater "water tower" of the whole system |

**Language families under Tier 2 (standard classification; ⚠️ Dardic's status as a branch of Indo-Aryan is
debated):** Indo-Aryan (Punjabi, Saraiki, Sindhi, Urdu, Hindko, Pahari-Pothwari); Iranic (Pashto, Balochi,
Wakhi, Hazaragi); Dardic (Shina, Khowar, Kohistani, Kalasha); Tibetic (Balti); the Burushaski isolate; and
Dravidian (Brahui, an outlier surrounded by Iranic and Indo-Aryan speakers). That spread inside one country
is unusual and is one reason a single "ethnic identity" tier does not compress cleanly.

**Why this works better than ethnicity alone:** it explains the two things ethnicity does not. First, why
the country's demographic center (Punjab, 51.58% of the population) also holds its army recruitment and its
irrigation, while its territorial bulk (Balochistan, 39.4% of the area) holds almost nobody. Second, why
the rim's grievances travel along the water: the **two big storage dams that feed the plain sit outside
the plain** — Tarbela in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Mangla in Azad Kashmir (⚠️ locations from background
knowledge, not re-verified this pass; high confidence) — and Diamer-Bhasha is being built in the roof.

**Two cautions this framework creates.**
1. **⚠️ It cuts across the whole-first-order-subdivision rule in the same places ethnicity does**, because the
   rim runs *through* Punjab's western districts, Sindh's north and Balochistan's north, not along provincial
   lines (§12).
2. **⚠️ Tier 1 is a proposal.** A simpler alternative is to run Tier 1 on the four provinces plus the two
   Kashmir territories (the administrative reading). This file uses Plain/Rim/Roof to organize evidence and
   leaves the choice open.

## Section 2 — Geography and the Indus system

**Area and shape.** Summing the seven first-order units gives **881,888 sq km** (Punjab 205,344; Sindh 140,914;
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 101,741; Balochistan 347,190; ICT 906; Gilgit-Baltistan 72,496; Azad Kashmir 13,297; areas
per the provincial Wikipedia articles), close to the commonly cited national figure of about 881,913.

**The Indus system.** The Indus (about 3,180 km, per Britannica-derived summaries) rises on the Tibetan
plateau, crosses Gilgit-Baltistan, skirts the Nanga Parbat massif in a deep gorge, turns south through
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and runs the length of the country to the Arabian Sea. The **Punjab is named for five
rivers: Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej and Indus**. Of these, the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty gave the three
"eastern" rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej; about 33 million acre-feet, 16%) to India and the three "western"
rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab; about 135 million acre-feet, 84%) to Pakistan, i.e. Pakistan receives roughly
80% of flow (Indus Waters Treaty article). Pakistan operates what is described as the world's largest
contiguous irrigation system, about **181,000 sq km irrigated (1999-2000)**, and **96% of withdrawn water goes
to agriculture** (Water resources management in Pakistan article, 2000 figure; ⚠️ old data, Stage 2 should
refresh).

**Terrain, by piece:**
- **Roof:** Gilgit-Baltistan holds five "eight-thousanders" and 50+ peaks above 7,000 m including K2; over
  two-thirds is glaciers, mountains and lakes. Population density is **24 per sq km**.
- **Rim, north:** the Hindu Kush foothills, Swat, Dir, Chitral, and the seven ex-FATA agencies along the
  Afghan border (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Orakzai, Kurram, North and South Waziristan), linked to the plain
  through the **Khyber Pass** (Alexander's route in 326 BCE) and the Gomal and Bolan corridors.
- **Rim, south:** the Balochistan plateau (Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges to the east), the Makran coast, and the
  desert districts bordering Iran and Afghanistan. Density **43 per sq km**.
- **Plain:** Punjab (density 622 per sq km), Sindh (395), and the Indus delta at the coast. ICT is the
  densest unit at about 2,609 per sq km.

**Border lengths cited in this pass:** Pakistan-Afghanistan (Durand Line) **2,640 km**; Pakistan-Iran **909 km**;
Pakistan-China **596 km** (through Khunjerab Pass, the only modern crossing).

Ahmadiyya figure (0.07% nationally) is also an undercount (§5).

## Section 6 — History of state formation

### 6.1 1947 and the princely accessions

**Pakistan was created on 14 August 1947** after the Lahore Resolution (1940). Partition displaced an estimated
**10-20 million people** with 200,000 to 2 million deaths (History of Pakistan; the range is wide by nature).

- **Bahawalpur** acceded 5-9 October 1947 as "the first state to accede"; it was folded into West Pakistan
  on 14 October 1955. ⚠️ It was briefly a province-like unit before that (background knowledge; details not
  confirmed by the fetched page).
- **Kalat** declared independence on 11 August 1947 (per the Khanate of Kalat article; ⚠️ some accounts give 15
  August, background knowledge), remained sovereign about seven months, and **acceded on 27 March 1948** under Khan Ahmad Yar Khan.
  It was a **Brahui confederacy** ruled by the Ahmadzai dynasty since 1666, which used Persian for
  administration and Balochi as the court language; British "Baluchistan" had been carved from its north
  after the **1876 Treaty of Kalat (Sandeman)**. Lasbela, Makran and Kharan acceded between March and July
  1948. **The first Baloch insurgency was 1948-1950, a direct consequence of the accession.**
- **North-West Frontier:** the province was created in 1901 (background knowledge). In the 1947 referendum
  Pashtun nationalists boycotted, demanding an option for an independent Pashtunistan (Pashtun nationalism
  article). The 1893 Durand Line, **2,640 km**, has been declared void by Afghanistan since **1949**.
- **Gilgit and Kashmir:** the Pakistan-administered parts of the former princely state of Jammu and
  Kashmir became Azad Kashmir and (later administratively separate) the Northern Areas, now
  Gilgit-Baltistan. ⚠️ The 1947 Gilgit rebellion details were not researched this pass.

### 6.2 The One Unit scheme, 1955-1970

**Announced 22 November 1954, enacted 30 September 1955**, One Unit merged Punjab, Sindh, the North-West
Frontier, Baluchistan and the princely states into a single "West Pakistan" to give parity with East
Pakistan, which had **55% of the population** (44,251,826 in 1951). Scholars quoted in the One Unit article
say it was "mainly pushed by the Punjabi elite of West Pakistan since 1953" to keep East Pakistani
politicians from power at the centre. It was dissolved by **Yahya Khan on 1 July 1970**, restoring the four
provinces. **The second Baloch insurgency (1958-1960) was specifically against One Unit.** Karachi returned
as Sindh's capital in 1970-1972.

### 6.3 1971

The 1970 election gave the Awami League **160 of 162 provincial seats in East Pakistan and 167 of 313
National Assembly seats**, an outright majority. **East Pakistan ceased to exist on 16 December 1971.** This is
the direct historical precedent for every fracture-line question below: a Bengali-majority wing separated
after a language movement (1952), an election result the army would not honor, and a war. Pakistan is
now a single-wing state whose largest province (Punjab, 51.6%) holds a permanent majority; the difference from
1971 is that **no province today is a demographic majority of the country other than Punjab, and Punjab is not
an aggrieved party.**

### 6.4 Federal design since: the 18th Amendment and after

The **18th Amendment (passed 8 April, Senate 15 April, signed 19 April 2010)** removed the president's power
to dissolve parliament, devolved **17 federal ministries** (education, health, environment, culture among
them) to the provinces, renamed the North-West Frontier Province, and passed the National Assembly 292 of
342. Under the **7th National Finance Commission Award (2010)** provincial shares of the divisible pool are
**Punjab 51.74%, Sindh 24.55%, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 14.62%, Balochistan 9.09%**, with a 57.5% vertical split to
the provinces and a **82.98% population weight**; Article 160(3A) forbids a lower provincial share than the
previous award.

**The 27th Amendment (November 2025)** is the newest and most consequential change. It created the Chief of
Defence Forces role, abolished the Chairman Joint Chiefs post, established a Commander of National
Strategic Command (an army officer appointed in consultation with the CDF), gave the CDF lifetime immunity,
and set up a Federal Constitutional Court. Opposition and legal analysts also read it as a step back on
provincial autonomy, specifically proposed returns of education and population planning to the centre and
changes to the provincial share protection; **the government says there is no plan to undo the 18th
Amendment.** ⚠️ **The final text's effect on Article 160(3A), education and population welfare was not
verified this pass**, and the 10th NFC Award status was not found.

Each subsection gives the evidence and a menu of options with costs. **No option is chosen.**

### 7.1 Balochistan — the strongest and most violent line (settled scenario in the Middle East survey §9)

**Live facts.**
- **Phases:** 1948-50 (accession), 1958-60 (One Unit), 1963-69 (Sui gas revenue), 1973-77 (about 3,300
  Pakistani military casualties), **2003-present** (intensified after Akbar Bugti's death in 2006).
- **Groups:** Balochistan Liberation Army (BLA, from 1996; split in 2017 into BLA-Jeeyand and BLA-Azad),
  Balochistan Liberation Front (2003), the Baloch Raaji Aajohi Sangar (BRAS) alliance from November 2018
  (which also includes the Sindhudesh Revolutionary Army, linking §7.1 and §7.3). CTC West Point counts
  **302 BLA-J attacks in 2024** and BRAS **204 joint attacks in 2024**.
- **Tactics have shifted from guerrilla to temporary territorial seizure:** Operation Herof (August 2024, about
  800 fighters), the temporary occupation of Zehri (January 2025), and **Operation Dara-e-Bolan**, ending in
  the **11 March 2025 hijacking of the Jaffar Express (Quetta-Peshawar, over 300 passengers)**; the Majeed
  Brigade suicide unit; and female suicide bombers since April 2022.
- **2025-2026 escalation:** The **US designated the BLA and its Majeed Brigade a Foreign Terrorist
  Organization on 12 August 2025.** A **24 May 2026 vehicle bomb on a shuttle train in Quetta killed at
  least 47 (20 soldiers)**, claimed by the BLA. Baloch leader **Mir Yar Baloch** declared a "Republic of
  Balochistan" in May 2025 from exile; no state recognized it and ⚠️ **he is a diaspora figure, not a
  governing authority** (the ME survey correctly notes it is unrecognized).
- **Repression of the unarmed movement:** **Dr. Mahrang Baloch** of the Baloch Yakjehti Committee was arrested
  on 22 March 2025 and, with Sibghatullah Shahji, **sentenced to life imprisonment on 22 June 2026** by an
  Anti-Terrorism Court on charges of terrorism, sedition and murder (Front Line Defenders; both deny the
  charges). This removes the main nonviolent channel and is the strongest recent evidence that the movement's
  center of gravity is the armed groups.
- **National security data:** 2025 was Pakistan's deadliest militancy year in over a decade; **over 95% of
  attacks were in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan** (PICSS); Balochistan had the highest overall
  casualties (PICSS).

**Structural evidence for and against a separate Baloch entity:**

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Distinct language and tribal system; a historical Brahui khanate (1666-1948) | Baloch plus Brahui is 57.1% by census, and 34.03% is Pashto, including Quetta (60.0% Pashto) |

**Options menu (⚠️ open).**
- **A. Whole province as Baloch-led polity (the ME survey's settled treatment).** *Cost:* a one-third Pashtun
  minority including the capital; Baloch-Pashtun friction is the province's oldest internal cleavage

  demand, ⚠️ background knowledge). Carry the 34% figure into any national-character profile.
- **B. Province split along the ethnic line (Pashtun north to the Pashtun rim, Baloch-Brahui south).**
  *Cost:* **breaks the whole-first-order rule** (§12), and Quetta, the capital and communications hub, sits
  in the Pashtun plurality.
- **C. Whole province retained by a Pakistan-successor, with a Baloch insurgency continuing.** *Cost:* this

- **D. Baloch polity as a hydrocarbon-and-port client of an external patron (China, or an Iran-side

### 7.2 Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the Pashtun belt and the Durand Line

**Facts.**
- **Durand Line:** 2,640 km, established 12 November 1893, **declared void by Afghanistan in 1949, and
  unrecognized by any Afghan government since**. Pakistan completed about **98% of its fence by April 2023**;
  Tajik and Hazara politicians in Afghanistan have at times acknowledged the line, provoking Pashtun
  nationalist opposition. Torkham and Chaman are the main crossings.
- **Pashtunistan as an idea:** a Pashtun-inhabited zone "from Dir in the north to Quetta in the south," with
  the more radical version being "Greater Afghanistan" (Pashtun nationalism article). Organized carriers:
  the Khudai Khidmatgar movement of Abdul Ghaffar Khan (1929-1947), ANP, PKMAP (since 1989), and the
  Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (founded 2014; ⚠️ its 2024 ban is background knowledge, not verified).
- **TTP:** announced December 2007 under Baitullah Mehsud; **Noor Wali Mehsud leads since 2018**; about 60
  affiliated groups; Deobandi jihadist; publicly pledges allegiance to the Afghan Taliban, which has
  "publicly rejected" that affiliation (Wikipedia; Pakistan accuses Afghanistan and India of backing it).
- **Numbers (⚠️ the two Pakistani think tanks disagree because of different definitions):** PICSS counts
  **1,066 militant attacks in 2025**, the highest since 2014's 1,609, with **2,115 militants, 664 security
  personnel and 580 civilians killed**; KP had **413 attacks** and 581 deaths. PIPS counts **699 attacks and
  1,034 fatalities** (a 34% increase in attacks). Both agree 2025 was the worst year in over a decade.
- **State-province friction:** Sohail Afridi (PTI) has been Chief Minister since **13 October 2025** (90 of 145
  provincial votes). The army set a **25 January 2026 evacuation deadline for Tirah Valley**, displacing an
  estimated 70,000-80,000 of about 150,000 residents in winter; Afridi called the operation "imposed
  without consent." A preceding Bajaur operation was declared successful.
- **The Afghan war of 2025-2026:** an October 2025 clash week; Doha ceasefire; Istanbul talks collapsed;
  **Pakistan closed all border crossings after October 2025 (over 100 days by January 2026)**; on **27
  February 2026 Pakistan struck 22 targets in Kabul, Kandahar and elsewhere and its defense minister said
  "open war"**; a Turkey/Saudi/Qatar-brokered ceasefire on **18 March 2026** was partial (Torkham reopened only
  for deportee repatriation on 31 March); **Afghan strikes on Balochistan and KP followed in June 2026 and the
  ceasefire has collapsed**, with Chinese-led mediation so far unsuccessful. Bilateral trade fell about
  **40% in 2025 ($2.461 billion to $1.766 billion)**.
- **Afghan refugees:** over **2 million Afghans returned from Pakistan since September 2023, more than 1
  million of them in 2025**; the 7 March 2025 plan cancelled Afghan Citizen Cards; by October 2025 UNHCR
  said about 1.2 million PoR-card holders, 737,000 ACC holders and 115,000 asylum-seekers remained
  (⚠️ those figures conflict with the returns counts, so treat the remaining population as unclear).

**Assessment.** ⚠️ This is inference, not sourced: **the Pashtun rim is the most militarized and the least
secessionist line.** Three separate actors act in it (an Islamist insurgency across the border, an
opposition provincial government, and a dormant ethnonationalist tradition) whose goals do not align.

**Options menu (⚠️ open).**
- **A. KP (with ex-FATA) stays inside a Pakistan-successor.** *Cost:* Islamist insurgency and periodic
  Afghan conflict persist; **Tarbela Dam (background knowledge) and the Indus itself give KP leverage over
  Punjab**, so a KP that stays has bargaining power.
- **B. KP (with ex-FATA) as its own entity, without joining Afghanistan.** *Cost:* landlocked, about 40.9
  million people, needs Punjab's transit to the sea, and **splits from Hazara Division's 56% Hindko and DI
  Khan's Saraiki (§12)** or keeps them inside.
- **C. KP joins or federates with an Afghan state.** *Cost:* the ME survey has "confirmed" Afghanistan's
  extent as whole and warned that Afghanistan is a poor, two-neighbor-dependent, ethnically mixed state
  where Pashtuns are 40-50%; adding KP's ~41 million would make **Pashtuns an overwhelming majority of a
  union and dilute the Tajik, Hazara and Uzbek population the ME survey treats as load-bearing.** ⚠️ Needs an
  explicit author call because it is an *expansion* the ME survey did not contemplate.
- **D. Pashtun-majority north of Balochistan detaches toward KP** (linked to option B in §7.1). *Cost:*
  breaks the whole-first-order rule and reduces the settled Balochistan scenario.

### 7.2b Geology and hydrology of the Pashtun belt — added 2026-09-29 (research note, ⚠️ partly background knowledge)

**Question asked by the author:** on the actual makeup of the land, does the Pashtun belt of Pakistan sit more naturally with Afghanistan or with Pakistan?
**Short answer: the land does not pick a side — it draws a different line than the Durand Line.** The Durand Line cuts across single structures; the natural geological and hydrological seam runs between the *hills* and the *Indus plain*, not between Pakistan and Afghanistan.

- **The Sulaiman fold-thrust belt straddles the border** (sourced). It extends from Kandahar, Zabul and Paktia provinces in Afghanistan across Waziristan and Kurram in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, northern Balochistan and southwestern Punjab; it formed as the Indian plate collided with Eurasia beginning about 30 million years ago and is a sedimentary fold belt on the Indian plate. So the ex-FATA hills and Kurram are the same structure as Paktia, Khost and Zabul, not a distinct Pakistani unit. (Sulaiman Mountains, Wikipedia; Sulaiman Range papers, Geophysical Journal International.)
- **The real plate boundary is the Chaman fault system** (sourced): a transpressional (left-lateral strike-slip plus compression) boundary between the Indian and Eurasian plates, running northeast across Balochistan and then north-northeast through Afghanistan, passing just west of Kabul and bounding the Kabul Block. It is *not* the Durand Line. ⚠️ Its exact offset from the border in each stretch was not established in this pass. Wikipedia's Geology of Pakistan says "parts of Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa lie on the Eurasian plate"; this is a simplification that most plausibly refers to the Hindu Kush / Chitral / Kohistan northern belt, not the Waziristan-Kurram fold belt.
- **The northern belt is a third case** (⚠️ background knowledge, not fetched): Chitral, Kohistan and the Swat-Dir-Bajaur hills belong to the Hindu Kush and the accreted Kohistan arc on the Eurasian side of the collision, continuous with Afghanistan's Nuristan and Kunar.
- **The Peshawar Valley is an Indus-side basin** (⚠️ background knowledge): an alluvial basin on the Indian-plate margin, geologically closer to the Indus plain than to the Afghan plateau, in the same way Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan are Indus-side lowlands.
- **Hydrology ties the two together instead** (sourced): the Kabul River rises in the Hindu Kush, is joined by the Kunar and by rivers from Pakistan's Chitral, crosses into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, is augmented by the Swat, and joins the Indus at Attock, adding roughly 20-28 million acre-feet a year. The basin spans nine Afghan and two Pakistani provinces, holds about 25 million people, and provides about 85% of Charsadda's irrigation, 80% of Peshawar's and 47.5% of Nowshera's. Afghanistan is upstream, Pakistan downstream; there is no water treaty. The Peshawar Valley's farms therefore depend on Afghan headwaters even though its rock is Indus-side. (Kabul River, Wikipedia; Dialogue Earth; The Diplomat.)

**What this does to the options.** A "geological" cut would split the belt by elevation: the hill and fold-belt districts (ex-FATA, Kurram, and the districts of northern Balochistan already inside the settled Balochistan region) go with the Afghan side, and the Indus-side lowlands (Peshawar Valley, Mardan-Swabi, Bannu, Lakki Marwat, Dera Ismail Khan) go with the Indus plain — while the water argument pulls the Peshawar Valley toward the Afghan headwaters. The whole-first-order-subdivision rule cannot draw either cut cleanly; both would need district-level exceptions. ⚠️ Nothing here chooses; it adds a fourth axis (rock and water) to the language, sect and army axes the survey already sets out. Language and politics point the same way as the hills (Pashto on both sides; Afghanistan rejects the Durand Line), but the elected KP government is federalist rather than secessionist (§7.2).

**Open items for this note:** an authoritative geological map of the border districts; the Chaman fault's exact position against the Durand Line; the drainage of the Gomal, Tochi and Kurram rivers (⚠️ believed to flow east to the Indus, not verified here).

### 7.2c Pashtun public opinion and kinship toward Afghanistan — added 2026-09-29 (research note)

**Bottom line: kinship is strong and well documented; political support for leaving Pakistan is thin, and no poll measuring it was found.** ⚠️ No survey asking Pakistani Pashtuns whether they would join Afghanistan turned up in this pass; everything below is indirect evidence.

- **Kinship and grievance about the line (sourced, qualitative).** Pashtuns on both sides commonly call the Durand Line an artificial division of one people; the Pashtunistan idea dates to the 1947 Pashtunistan Resolution. The Pashtun Tahafuz Movement drew crowds reported in the hundreds of thousands, drew open support from Afghanistan's then-president, and rests on cross-border kinship. Its demands are rights inside Pakistan (ending disappearances and extra-judicial killings, landmine clearance), not secession. (Elliott School / GWU; AEI; Jamhoor.)
- **Identity: Pakistani first (dated poll).** Pew's 2009 Global Attitudes survey (1,254 adults, face to face) found 92% of those who identify as Pashtun see themselves first as Pakistani. ⚠️ 2009 data, taken before the 2014 and 2025-26 escalations, and read here from a search summary of the Pew page. (Pew Research Center 2009.)
- **Attitudes to the Afghan Taliban (mixed).** A Gallup Pakistan poll after the 2021 takeover found 55% of Pakistanis "happy" with the Taliban government and 65% in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (⚠️ media report of the poll, not the primary release). Yet Pew's 2014 poll found only 8% of Pakistanis rated the Taliban favorably, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa respondents rated extremist groups worst of any province. Sympathy for Afghan Pashtuns and a Taliban-led state are different things. (Geo/Gallup Pakistan; Pew 2014.)
- **Voting (sourced).** In the February 2024 provincial election PTI-backed independents took 93 seats on 38.44%, JUI-F 19 seats on 15.44%; the Awami National Party, the historic Pashtun-nationalist party, polled 696,338 votes and won one seat. Pashtun voters chose a federalist national opposition, not a nationalist or irredentist party. (Wikipedia; FAFEN.)
- **Pressure points that could raise sympathy for Afghanistan (sourced).** Roughly 1.3 million Afghans have been returned from Pakistan since November 2023 and more than 146,000 in 2026 so far, many after decades in Pakistan; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa took 71% of violent incidents in 2025, the deadliest year in over a decade; Pakistan struck Kabul and Kandahar in February 2026. Analysts warn this feeds a story that Islamabad is an enemy of Pashtuns, and Pashtun nationalist parties and religious leaders are pressing Islamabad for a softer line on refugees. (HRW; International Crisis Group; NatStrat.) ⚠️ The "enemy of Pashtuns" reading is an analyst's opinion, not a measurement.

**Reading it for the setting.** A collapse that removes the Pakistani state would remove most of the reasons Pashtuns gave for staying Pakistani (identity, army, order), and the kinship base is real. But the evidence supports "strong cross-border kin identity, weak organized secessionism" — the most likely fracture is not a clean union with today's Afghanistan, and a union with a Taliban government would be unattractive to the secular-nationalist and PTM constituencies.

### 7.2d If the Pashtun belt seceded to Afghanistan: what would retaking cost, and would it be worth it? — added 2026-09-29 (scenario analysis, ⚠️ mostly inference)

⚠️ **This is analysis built on sourced benchmarks, not a sourced forecast.** It also assumes a coherent Pakistani state with a working army exists to do the retaking; in this project's collapse setting that is itself an open question (§9).

**Benchmarks (sourced).**
- **Pakistan's forces:** about 660,000 active military personnel (other counts near 943,000 including paramilitary); the Afghan Taliban field about 150,000-172,000 with no air force, navy or radar network; Pakistan's 2025-26 defense budget was about US$9 billion and 2026-27 about US$10.8 billion. Conventionally Pakistan dominates. (IISS via press; Janes; Deccan Herald.)
- **What clearing one district cost:** Operation Rah-e-Nijat (South Waziristan, 2009) used about 28,000-45,000 troops against militants who numbered a fraction of that, and cost 80 soldiers killed in the ground phase; displacement estimates range from about 293,000 to 2 million. Operation Zarb-e-Azb (North Waziristan, 2014) used up to 25,000-30,000 soldiers and displaced 500,000 to about 1 million. Militants in Swat in 2009 had reached within about 70 miles of Islamabad. (Wikipedia; War on the Rocks; Al Jazeera; CTC at West Point.)
- **The prize:** Khyber Pakhtunkhwa had 40.8 million people in the 2023 census (about 16.5% of Pakistan) and produced about 10.5% of GDP (an older figure) and about 20% of mining output; Tarbela Dam, at 4,888 MW installed, is about 51.6% of WAPDA-linked capacity; the Kabul River adds 20-28 million acre-feet to the Indus at Attock; Frontier Corps rank and file are overwhelmingly Pashtun; and the army has historically drawn an estimated 15-21% of its rank and file from the Pashtun north (§5; ⚠️ dated). (Dawn; Wikipedia; WAPDA; Jamestown.)

**Scale of the effort (inference).**
1. *Conventional reconquest of the plains and roads* (Peshawar Valley to Attock, the N-5 corridor, Tarbela, Mardan, Bannu, Dera Ismail Khan) is within Pakistan's means: air and armor dominance, a short supply line from Punjab, and an Afghan force that cannot contest airspace. Order of magnitude: several corps, weeks to months, heavy urban and infrastructure damage, millions displaced.
2. *Holding a province of 40 million with a mountainous, armed, tribal hinterland* is a different problem. The 2009 and 2014 operations each needed a corps-scale force to clear one agency and took years to stabilize; a hostile population and a friendly cross-border sanctuary multiply that. A common counterinsurgency rule of thumb (about 20 security personnel per 1,000 residents; ⚠️ contested) would imply roughly 800,000 for 40 million, more than the whole active army. Pakistan could not garrison the hills without conscription, and it never fully controlled them before: the tribal areas were ruled indirectly under colonial-era frontier law until 2018.
3. *Two-front and third-party cost:* a war on the western border ties down forces against India; the Baloch insurgency and the Karachi Pashtun population (2.75 million Pashto speakers, §12) become internal fronts; refugees and militancy flow east.

**Would it be worth it? (inference).** Not evenly. The *Indus-side* prize (Tarbela, Attock, the Peshawar-Islamabad corridor 160 km or so from the capital, the Kabul River's lower reach, most of the population and revenue) is close to existential for a Punjab-centered Pakistan: losing it would put an Afghan-aligned state on the Indus above the country's water and power. The *hill belt* (ex-FATA, Kurram, Chitral-Kohistan-Swat-Dir) is worth little in revenue and has repeatedly cost more than it yielded; the historical pattern is buffer and indirect rule. The likely equilibrium is therefore a *partial* retake: Pakistan fights for the Indus-side line and treats the hills as a buffer, which closely tracks the geological and hydrological split in §7.2b (hills with the Afghan structure, plains with the Indus). ⚠️ A partial-retake outcome would produce a border that crosses the Kabul River's irrigation of the Peshawar Valley, i.e., a live water dispute.

✅ **Settled by the author, 2026-09-29, after reading this note: the Pashtun portion stays part of Pakistan.** No hills-versus-plains cut is drawn.

### 7.3 Sindh — Sindhudesh and the water line

**Facts.**
- **Tradition:** G.M. Syed, one of Pakistan's founding figures, turned to Sindhi nationalism after being
  influenced by Bengali nationalism; Jeay Sindh Mahaz declined after his death in 1995; the
  **Jeay Sindh Muttahida Mahaz (JSMM)** was founded in 2000. The **Sindhudesh Revolutionary Army** has been
  part of the BRAS alliance (CTC).
- **Language politics:** the 1972 Sindh Assembly declared Sindhi the only official language of Sindh and
  triggered Sindhi-Muhajir riots; quota policies then disadvantaged educated Urdu-speaking city dwellers,
  which fed MQM mobilization (§7.5).
- **The 2025 canal crisis:** the **Cholistan Canal Project** (announced March 2025 under the Green Pakistan
  Initiative by Field Marshal Munir and Punjab's Chief Minister; six canals from the Indus to irrigate about
  4.8 million acres of "barren" land under corporate farming) produced "Save Sindh" protests, a Sindh
  Assembly resolution on 13 March 2025, and the federal government **halting the project on 24 April 2025**.
  Sindh mobilized as a province, across party lines, on a water issue. The 2025-12 (9 December) Sindhi Culture
  Day clash in Karachi was reported by an Indian outlet with a strongly partisan angle, so ⚠️ **treat that
  item as low-reliability**; a 2017 Dawn piece reported "the romance of Sindhudesh fast fading away as
  workers desert nationalism."
- **Hindu minority:** 4.9 million, chiefly in Thar, is a structural stake in any Sindh polity.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Inference: Sindh has the cultural and territorial claim of a nation (a distinct language and a medieval state tradition: Soomra 1024-1351 and Samma 1351-1524 per the History of Pakistan chronology, after the 712 CE Arab conquest) but not the
armed capacity of Balochistan or the demographic coherence, since **Karachi (36.6% of the province) is 50.67% Urdu and
11.12% Sindhi.** The 2025 canals crisis showed its real leverage: it is the downstream end of the Indus, and
a province can be mobilized by threats to its water.

**Options menu (⚠️ open).**
- **A. Sindh whole stays in a Pakistan-successor.** *Cost:* Sindhi nationalist grievance stays live; Sindh is
  also a PPP stronghold (⚠️ background knowledge) tying it to the federal system.
- **B. Sindh whole as its own entity, Karachi included.** *Cost:* **Karachi's Urdu-speaking majority becomes a
  minority-in-the-capital problem of the MQM type**; Sindh depends on Punjab and the upper riparian for
  water, with only about 30 days of storage in the whole system (§8).
- **C. Sindh minus Karachi.** *Cost:* **breaks the whole-first-order rule**, strands Sindh's ports (Karachi
  Port and Port Qasim) and roughly a third of its population.
- **D. Karachi as a separate city-state or federal territory, Sindh otherwise whole** (see §7.5).

### 7.4 The Saraiki belt — identity without an armed movement

**Facts.**
- **The movement:** began in the late 1960s as a language campaign; **Riyaz Hashimi coined "Saraikistan" in
  1972**, after earlier Bahawalpur-province advocacy. Competing labels are competing projects:
  **Saraikistan** (ethnolinguistic), **Bahawalpur** (a historical political entity that was a state until
  1955), and **South Punjab** (a developmental, administrative framing).
- **Party positions:** PTI favors 13 districts; PPP supports 15 Punjab districts plus two from Khyber
  Pakhtunkhwa (Dera Ismail Khan and Tank); PML-N proposes **three** provinces instead. **A South Punjab civil
  secretariat began operating 15 October 2020**, but its powers were significantly reduced in 2021; a PPP
  senator reiterated the demand in the Senate in **June 2026**.
- **Numbers** are in §4.2: the area is 31.6% of Punjab's population, 49.8% of its area, with Bahawalpur only
  49.1% Saraiki and Multan District 63.9%.

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Inference: **the Saraiki belt has the weakest fracture evidence of the five.** There is no
armed wing; the movement is split between at least three territorial projects; the largest party
(PML-N) opposes the Saraiki form; and the settler layer (§1.2) means the Bahawalpur core is barely
half Saraiki. It is also the poorest, most agricultural part of the richest province, so an economic-grievance
case exists but a separatist case is thin.

**Options menu (⚠️ open).**
- **A. Punjab whole (the default).** *Cost:* the Saraiki question stays as internal politics.
- **B. Three southern divisions as a separate entity (Saraikistan/South Punjab).** *Cost:* **splits the
  demographic and army heartland** (about 31.6% of Punjab's population), cuts the Indus and Chenab
  irrigation networks in two, and the Bahawalpur and Multan sub-identities remain unresolved. ⚠️ Breaks the
  whole-first-order rule (§12).
- **C. Bahawalpur as a separate historical entity** (a single 13.4-million-person division). *Cost:* only
  49.1% Saraiki and 42.6% Punjabi, so it is not an ethnic unit.
- **D. Mianwali/Bhakkar/Layyah/DG Khan and DI Khan (KP) as a Saraiki extension.** *Cost:* **crosses the KP/
  Punjab provincial line** (40.1% Saraiki in DI Khan Division).

### 7.5 Muhajir and Karachi — a city-state-scale question

**Facts (all 2023 unless noted).** Karachi Division: **20,382,881 people (20.3 million division; 18.87 million
"city proper" in a different count), 36.6% of Sindh.** Language mix in §4.3. Wikipedia-level economic claims,
**⚠️ unverified and probably stated too high**: about 25% of Pakistan's GDP, 35% of tax revenue, about 30%
of industrial output and "approximately 95% of foreign trade." Port infrastructure is Karachi Port and Port
Qasim, and **the Karachi Nuclear Power Plant (KANUPP)** is a 1970s installation (§10).

**History.** Karachi was about 400,000 people and roughly 51% Hindu in 1947; the Muslim share rose from 42%
(1941) to 96% (1951); the Muhajir arrival was 470,000 by May 1948 and 100,000 annually until 1952. It was
the federal capital until 1958, then returned to Sindh's control in 1970-1972. **MQM-Pakistan** (a 2016
split from MQM-London, Altaf Hussain's original 1984 movement, ⚠️ founding date from background knowledge)
won **21 of 336 National Assembly seats and 36 Sindh Assembly seats in 2024**; **Operation Clean-up
(1992-1994) and the Rangers' 2013 operation** are the two security surges (the article claims crime fell
"from 6th to 128th worldwide by 2022," ⚠️ unverified).

**Assessment.** ⚠️ Inference: **Karachi is the only Pakistani unit where the plain's ethnic layers are visibly
interlocked at scale** (10.3 million Urdu, 2.75 million Pashto, 2.26 million Sindhi, 1.65 million Punjabi,
0.81 million Balochi, 0.75 million Saraiki, 0.65 million Hindko speakers). That is a city-state-scale
question: it has the economy of a nation and the population of a mid-sized state, and *no* ethnic
majority beyond Urdu-speaking plurality (about half).

**Options menu (⚠️ open).**
- **A. Inside Sindh as now.** *Cost:* Sindhi-Muhajir cleavage persists; Karachi is the province's revenue
  engine and ethnic anomaly.
- **B. Karachi as a separate province or federal territory (a long-standing "Karachi province" idea in Muhajir
  politics, ⚠️ background knowledge).** *Cost:* **breaks the whole-first-order rule**; Sindhi nationalists
  and the PPP treat any division of Sindh as a red line (⚠️ background knowledge).
- **C. Karachi as a city-state-scale entity.** *Cost:* no water source of its own (Karachi depends on the
  Indus and on reservoirs outside the division; ⚠️ not researched this pass); a landlocked Sindh rump.
- **D. Karachi stays with a Pakistan-successor that keeps the port.** *Cost:* only workable if a
  Pakistan-successor persists at all.

### 7.6 Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Kashmir — limbo and a live protest movement

**Gilgit-Baltistan.** 72,496 sq km, 1.71 million (2023), three divisions, ten districts. It has a
**"semi-provincial" status** under the **2009 Self-Governance Order and the 2018 reform order**, with a
33-seat assembly (24 directly elected) and chief minister, but no representation in Pakistan's National
Assembly. **The 4th Assembly election was held 7 June 2026** (turnout 57.69%, 555,066 of 960,496 registered
voters): PTI was denied its symbol and contested through independents; Amjad Hussain Azar (PPP) was elected
Chief Minister unopposed on 22 June 2026 under a PPP-PML-N power-sharing agreement with IPP support; two
constituencies were re-polled on 12 July. ⚠️ The party seat totals differ between sources and are omitted.
Assemblies have repeatedly passed resolutions seeking "provisional provincial status," phrased so as not to
prejudice the Kashmir dispute (2018 and 2021 coverage); **⚠️ whether any 2026 step was taken was not verified**
(a July 2026 Pamir Times commentary says the question remains open).
GB's geopolitical weight: the **Karakoram Highway** to China's Khunjerab Pass, CPEC and the **Diamer-Bhasha
Dam** (background knowledge; not re-verified). The 1963 China-Pakistan boundary agreement ceded the Shaksgam
Valley (Trans-Karakoram Tract) to China and includes a clause reopening the boundary if the Kashmir
dispute is resolved; India rejects it.

**Azad Kashmir.** 13,297 sq km, 4.33 million (2023), ten districts in three divisions (Muzaffarabad, Poonch,
Mirpur); governed under the **1974 Interim Constitution**. **The 2025-2026 unrest is significant and current:**
the Jammu Kashmir Joint Awami Action Committee (JAAC) presented a 38-point charter, with an **October 2025
Muzaffarabad Accord** left "largely unimplemented"; protests began in earnest on 7 June 2026, centered on
abolishing **12 seats reserved for refugees from Jammu and Kashmir living elsewhere in Pakistan**; Amnesty
reported **at least 40 dead (34 protesters, 6 security personnel) by 28 July 2026**, with local estimates
above 50. The **election ran in three phases (27 July, 2 August and 10 August 2026)** amid a JAAC boycott;
**PML-N won 25 seats and PPP 12; Iftikhar Gilani (PML-N) became Prime Minister** (46 of 53 seats contested
directly; figures from news reports).

**Assessment and options (⚠️ open).** The two territories are not fracture lines in the ethnic sense; they
are **legal-status questions with a fixed external constraint** (the Indian-administered side is out of
scope). The choices are structural, not ethnic:
- **A. Stay as Pakistan-administered territories (or provisional province).** *Cost:* the status limbo and, in
  AJK, the JAAC movement continue.
- **B. Gilgit-Baltistan alone as a separate entity.** *Cost:* 1.71 million people at 24 per sq km,
  landlocked, dependent on Karakoram Highway links to both China and the plain; strong sectarian
  fractures (§5), and it holds the headwaters and dam sites (§8).
- **C. Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Kashmir jointly.** *Cost:* no shared language (Pahari-Pothwari, Gojri
  vs. Shina, Balti, Burushaski) and no direct road link that avoids Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (⚠️ road geography
  not researched).
- **D. GB oriented toward the East Turkestan polity** the East Asia survey already settled. *Cost:* the
  only working land link there is Khunjerab; the KKH also ties GB to the plain.

**Seam (India):** the Line of Control separates Azad Kashmir and GB from the Indian-administered territory;
**the 22 April 2025 Pahalgam attack, India's 7 May 2025 "Operation Sindoor" strikes (including inside
Pakistani-administered Kashmir), Pakistan's 10 May "Operation Bunyan-um-Marsoos," and a ceasefire the same
day brokered with US involvement** are the current baseline. Beyond this one sentence, nothing about India
is researched here.

### 7.7 Sectarian and religious fracture lines (not territorial, but they shape it)

Kurram (about half Shia, §5), Quetta's Hazara, Gilgit-Baltistan's four-way sect map, Ahmadi exclusion, and
Christians (Punjab, 1.93%) are **minority-safety questions rather than partition questions**, but they set the

## Section 8 — Water

**The Indus is the plain's single point of failure, and the rim owns the taps.**

- **System size:** about 181,000 sq km irrigated (1999-2000); **96% of withdrawals go to agriculture (2000)**.
  Per-capita availability fell from 1,299 m3 (1996-97) to 1,101 m3 (2004-05) (older figures; ⚠️ refresh).
  About 36% of groundwater is highly saline. Hydropower supplies about 29% of generation (article,
  undated).
- **Storage:** Tarbela (Indus) and Mangla (Jhelum) are the two big reservoirs. Designed live storage of the
  three big dams is about 22.98 km3 (Tarbela 11.96, raised Mangla 10.15), but **usable live storage is about
  11.5-13.0 MAF, less than 10% of the annual allocation, roughly a 30-day buffer** (search-derived, source
  quality medium). ⚠️ The design-versus-usable gap is presumably sedimentation, not researched.
- **The treaty:** India declared the Indus Waters Treaty "in abeyance" in **April 2025** after the Pahalgam
  attack; Pakistan warned that stopping flow could be "an act of war." In **March 2026 Pakistan petitioned
  the Permanent Court of Arbitration; hearings were 26-28 April 2026 (India did not participate); a
  unanimous ruling dated 31 August 2026 (Al Jazeera reports 1 September) held India has no unilateral power to
  suspend the treaty and imposed interim limits on the Ratle project on the Chenab.** India rejected the
  tribunal's jurisdiction and says the abeyance remains. A Neutral Expert final award is expected around
  the end of 2026. Physical reality: India has not shared routine flow data since 2023 and has about 1
  million acre-feet of Chenab storage, small relative to Pakistan's needs, so **geography constrains India
  more than the treaty does right now** (a water expert quoted by Al Jazeera).
- **Intra-Pakistan shares:** the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord and the long-running **Kalabagh Dam**
  controversy are the standing provincial disputes (⚠️ background knowledge, not researched this pass); the
  **2025 Cholistan canals** episode is the current evidence that upstream-downstream provincial water
  politics can mobilize Sindh (§7.3).
- **Floods** in 2010 and 2022 (the 2022 floods destroyed about half of crops per one source) are a further
  water shock.

**Framework link:** any successor scenario in which Punjab and Sindh are separate polities makes the Indus
an international river with a downstream state, the same shape as the Central Asian Syr Darya/Amu Darya
case in `Central Asia - ... (survey).md` §4. If KP or Gilgit-Baltistan separate, **the dam sites are outside
the plain**.

| **Pashtun in Karachi** | 2.75 million Pashto speakers, about 93% of Sindh's | Not separable |
| **Pashtun edge in Punjab** | Mianwali 11.3% Pashto | Small |
| **Kohistan, Chitral, Kalash** | Kohistani 1.08% of Malakand Division; 7,466 Kalasha speakers | Too small for a map, relevant for character profiles |
| **Hazara (Quetta) and Shia Kurram** | ~500,000; ~390,000 (about half of 785,434) | Minority-in-place, not separable |
| **ICT** | 906 sq km, 2.36 million | Contiguity; Punjab-KP border enclave, ⚠️ assignment open |

**Frameworks conclusion.** The rule works cleanly for **Sindh vs. Punjab** (province lines follow the
Sindhi-Punjabi/Saraiki divide reasonably), **Gilgit-Baltistan** and **Azad Kashmir**. It fails at **four
places**: Balochistan's north, Karachi, the Saraiki belt, and Hazara Division. Where the rule holds, options
are legible; where it fails, each option costs a rule exception.
