# East Asia — Frontiers, Civilizational Spheres and the Han Core (Reconnaissance Survey)

**Date:** 2026-09-23
**Type:** reconnaissance survey — what exists to work from, plus a first-pass synthesis. Not a
country-by-country buildout, and not a set of final nations.

**Purpose:** find what fills, for East Asia, the slot that Colin Woodard's *American Nations* fills for North
America — a framework of durable regional cultures usable as the analytical basis for designing
post-collapse successor nations. Companion to `extractions/American_Nations_Extraction.md` (its Part Two —
nation vs. state, the Doctrine of First Effective Settlement, core/domain/sphere — and its Part Four
checklist are the rubric used throughout and are not restated) and to `extractions/Latin America - Regional
Frameworks and Colonial Origins (survey).md`, whose structure this file deliberately matches.

**Scope:** **China, Japan, Korea, Mongolia, Taiwan — and nothing else.** The following are named here so that
their exclusion is deliberate and on the record rather than accidental:

- **Southeast Asia** — Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Myanmar, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, the
  Philippines, Brunei, Timor-Leste. Vietnam is the fourth member of the Sinosphere and is discussed only in
  that capacity, at 1.6 and 3.4.
- **South Asia** — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, the Maldives. Named because the
  Tibetan Plateau's rivers run into it (2.4) and because Lattimore's own comparative case is the British
  Indian Northwest Frontier (1.3).
- **Central Asia west of Xinjiang** — Kazakhstan (which exists in canon), Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan,
  Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan.
- **West Asia and the Caucasus** — entirely outside.
- **Russia and Siberia — deferred entirely, by instruction.** Siberian and Russian Far Eastern territory is
  **unclaimed** wherever it abuts this survey's ground, exactly as the Latin America survey treated the
  Falklands corridor: the adjacency is noted, the traversal problem is flagged, and no polity is proposed.
  This matters at three specific seams and they are marked in place — **Outer Manchuria** north of the Amur
  and east of the Ussuri (2.1), whose 1858–60 loss is the direct cause of the Chuang Guandong policy
  reversal; **Sakhalin and the Kurils** (2.7), across which the Ainu range ran until 1875; and **Buryatia and
  Tuva** on Mongolia's northern flank (2.2). In each case the frontier zone on the East Asian side is
  described and the ground beyond it is left blank.

**Provenance:** all wording original; no source passage reproduced. Numeric claims are restated from named
sources. Every substantive claim carries a URL in the source list. Anything not verified in this session is
flagged ⚠️.

**A note on ethnic material, recorded once.** This survey names ethnic groups, counts them, and uses ethnic
identity as its central analytical variable, because that is what the method requires. Per the amendment of
2026-09-06 to `No_National_Stereotypes.md`, that document is a rule about **provenance** and governs in-world
Tepenian city description; it is not a prohibition on ethnicity and does not apply to real-world research. No
self-censorship has been applied here.

## Headline findings

1. **Woodard's method transfers to East Asia; his *doctrine* does not — and the substitution has already been
   made by the author.** The Doctrine of First Effective Settlement asks who arrived first, which is an
   unanswerable question about civilizations with no founding-settler moment. The author's own refinement —
   that the settling population, realistically, *is* **ethnic identity, grouped into major categories and
   then subdivided into personalized ones** — is the correct substitution, and it is what North America was
   secretly using all along. The second input to the New North America map was a county ancestry map. That is
   an ethnic-identity map at two tiers. Part 1 is built on this.
2. **The Hu Line is the strongest single piece of evidence in this survey, and the check came back
   positive.** Hu Huanyong drew a line from Heihe to Tengchong in **1935**. On the map of the day, 36% of the
   area held **96%** of the people and 64% held **4%**. On the modern map the same line gives **43% / 94%
   against 57% / 6%** — and the 2002 and 2015 readings are effectively identical to each other. Ninety years,
   a civil war, a revolution, the largest internal migration in human history and a state that spent decades
   actively trying to settle the west did not move it. This is East Asia's equivalent of Melissa Dell's
   mita-boundary result, and it is arguably a harder finding, because it is a whole-country demographic
   constant rather than a local discontinuity.
3. **Owen Lattimore and Hu Huanyong described the same object five years apart, independently, from opposite
   disciplines.** Lattimore's steppe/sown frontier (*Inner Asian Frontiers of China*, 1940) is an
   institutional and ecological argument; Hu's line is a demographic measurement. They land on the same
   ground, and the Great Wall — which tracks the **400 mm annual rainfall isohyet**, the boundary between
   land that will carry farming and land that will only carry herds — is the third witness. In the Latin
   America survey, the independent convergence of Ribeiro and Wagley was taken as evidence that a division
   tracked something real rather than one scholar's taste. This convergence is tighter and has a wall on it.
4. **G. William Skinner already drew the internal map of the Han core, and nobody in the brief named him.**
   His **nine physiographic macroregions**, defined by river drainage basins and travel-constraining terrain,
   each with its own core-periphery structure and its own unsynchronized development cycles, are the closest
   thing to a "Woodard for China" that exists. Two things make him decisive here. First, he is the right
   instrument for the Sinian Federation's internal sub-layer. Second, **he explicitly excludes Xinjiang,
   Tibet, Qinghai and most of Inner Mongolia from his scheme** — an independent scholarly vote for exactly
   the Han-core/frontier split the author has already settled on.
5. **The Han core's internal division is evidence-backed, contains eight-plus members rather than seven, and
   is severely lopsided — which is the best thing about it.** The author's own acquired language map, read in
   the companion extraction, supplies the Sinitic layer directly: **Jin, Ping and Huizhou are mapped
   separately** from Mandarin, Yue and Wu, and **Min is five languages, not one** (Min-Bei, Min-Dong,
   Min-Zhong, Min-Nan, Pu-Xian), with Min-Nan reaching Taiwan and Hainan. And **Mandarin covers roughly
   two-thirds to three-quarters of the Han area.** A federation whose largest member is bigger than all the
   others combined is not a balanced union and should not be written as one: **the asymmetry gives the Sinian
   Federation permanent internal politics, which is worth more than a tidy balance.** See 3.1.
6. **Manchuria is the one place in East Asia where the Doctrine of First Effective Settlement applies in
   Woodard's literal sense, and it has numbers.** The Qing banned Han settlement from a 1668 Kangxi edict and
   built the Willow Palisade to enforce it; then reversed course after Russia took Outer Manchuria in
   1858–60. Gottschang and Lary reconstruct **25.4 million arrivals from south of the Wall between 1891 and
   1942, 16.7 million returns, a net 8.7 million** in half a century. A Manchu homeland became a Han
   industrial region inside a hundred years, and the Manchu language is now down to **fewer than 100 fluent
   native speakers**.
7. **East Asia has three measured boundary discontinuities, not one.** Alongside the Hu Line sit two formal
   regression-discontinuity results on the Qinling–Huaihe line: **Chen, Ebenstein, Greenstone & Li (PNAS
   2013)**, who find life expectancy about **5.5 years lower** north of the Huai because a 1950s policy gave
   free coal heating to the north only; and **Talhelm et al. (*Science* 2014)**, who find measurably
   different psychology between rice-growing and wheat-growing Han Chinese, **with differences just as large
   between neighboring counties straddling the rice-wheat border** as between distant provinces. That last
   design is Dell's mita design applied to an agricultural boundary. The quantitative base for
   institutional persistence in East Asia is better than Woodard's own, and comparable to Latin America's.
8. **The hard problem is not the northern frontier; it is the southern border of the Han core — and the two
   bodies of evidence point in opposite directions.** "The Sinian Federation is the Eighteen Provinces" is
   clean at the Wall and messy in the south, because the Qing eighteen already contained Yunnan, Guizhou and
   Guangxi. The **ethnolinguistic evidence says exclude them**: the language map shows a shatter zone of some
   twenty-five non-Han peoples, with **Zhuang** drawn as a solid territorial block comparable in map area to
   Wu or Gan. The **material evidence says include them**: those provinces plus Gansu carry **over 90% of
   China's nickel, the largest aluminum, lead and tin reserves in the country, over 40% of national
   phosphate, about a third of its tin and manganese, and 160.9 million people.** Exclude them and the
   Federation is ethnically coherent and cannot make stainless steel, bronze or fertilizer from its own
   ground. **Section 3.3 sets out the options and their costs and marks the decision. It does not make it.**

## Part 1 — What replaces first effective settlement

### 1.1 The problem, stated plainly

Woodard's doctrine, credited to Wilbur Zelinsky in 1973, is that whichever group first establishes a viable,
self-perpetuating society in a territory sets that territory's lasting cultural character, however small the
group and however many later arrivals of other backgrounds follow. It is a **settler-colonial** mechanism. It
presupposes a moment at which a territory acquires its first effective society, and a documentary record of
who was doing the founding.

East Asia does not offer that moment. Chinese civilization developed in the North China Plain around the
lower Yellow River and expanded outward across several millennia, conquering and assimilating, and in some
centuries being conquered and assimilated in turn. There is no arrival. Korea and Japan have continuous
societies of comparable depth. Asking "who effectively settled Henan" is not a hard question; it is a
malformed one.

**Two things follow, and the second is more important than the first.**

First, the Doctrine fails as stated — with **one genuine exception**, Manchuria, where a state-enforced
settlement ban was lifted and a documented, dated, counted in-migration produced a new society inside a
century. Manchuria is treated at 2.1 as the Woodard case proper.

Second — and this is the useful part — **the Doctrine was never the real mechanism.** It was a proxy. What
Woodard is actually tracking is *which group's norms a territory runs on*, and "first effective settlement"
is simply the historical route by which North America acquired its answer to that question. Where a continent
has a different history, it will have a different route to the same answer. The question survives the
doctrine.

### 1.2 What transfers: tiered ethnic identity

**The author's refinement is the answer, and it also explains North America retroactively.**

Stated in the author's terms: what a settling population realistically *is*, is **ethnic identity — first
grouped into major categories, then subdivided into more personalized categories.** Two tiers. Major grouping
above, individual identity below.

This is the right substitution for three reasons.

1. **It is what the North American map already used.** `METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md` identifies three
   inputs to that map: Woodard's book, a blank base map, and *"Largest Ancestry by County, 2010."* An
   ancestry map is an ethnic-identity map, and it is already tiered — German, Norwegian, Irish, English,
   African American, Mexican, American Indian are tier-two identities resolvable into tier-one groupings
   (Germanic, Celtic, Nordic, African-descended, Hispanic, Indigenous). The METHOD file records that where
   Woodard and the ancestry map disagreed, **the author followed the ancestry map** — Midwestland is the
   German-and-Norwegian plains block, not Woodard's Quaker Ohio corridor. The method in practice was already
   ethnic identity. The doctrine was the justification, not the instrument.
2. **It survives the absence of a founding moment.** Ethnic identity is a present-tense, countable,
   map-drawable fact that does not require knowing who arrived first. It can be read off a census.
3. **It preserves what was actually valuable in Woodard.** Woodard's own position is that nationhood is
   *acquired, not inherited* — culture, not bloodline. Tiered ethnic identity in East Asia carries the same
   property, and carries it explicitly: the Chinese state's own **Hua–Yi distinction** (華夷之辨) is a
   civilizational rather than descent-based test, and the PRC's *minzu* system classifies the **Hui** — 11.4
   million Chinese-speaking Muslims with no non-Sinitic language — as a distinct ethnicity on religious and
   cultural grounds alone. East Asia has been running a culture-not-blood identity test for two thousand
   years.

**The two tiers, applied to East Asia.** Tier one is the major grouping, largely linguistic-family shaped
because that is how the region's identities actually cluster. Tier two is the personalized identity people
hold. The Han exception, where a third tier is required, is at 3.1.

| Tier 1 — major grouping | Tier 2 — the identities people actually hold |
|---|---|
| **Han** | *Three tiers here — see 3.1.* Mandarin, Jin, Wu, Huizhou, Gan, Xiang, **Min (five: Bei, Dong, Zhong, Nan, Pu-Xian)**, Hakka, Yue, Ping |
| **Tungusic** | Manchu, Xibe, Oroqen, Evenki, Hezhen (Nanai), Daur ⚠️ (Daur is Mongolic; listed here by association only) |
| **Mongolic** | Khalkha Mongol, Inner Mongol (Chahar, Horchin, Ordos, Buryat, Oirat), Daur, Monguor (Tu), Dongxiang, Bonan |
| **Turkic** | Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Salar, Uzbek, Tatar |
| **Tibetic and Tibeto-Burman** | Tibetan (Ü-Tsang, Amdo, Kham), Yi, Bai, Naxi, Qiang, Lisu, Hani, Tujia |
| **Koreanic** | Korean; Chosŏnjok (Korean-Chinese, Yanbian) |
| **Japonic** | Yamato; Ryukyuan (Okinawan, Amami, Miyako, Yaeyama) |
| **Ainu** | Hokkaido Ainu (language isolate; Sakhalin and Kuril varieties extinct) |
| **Tai-Kadai** | Zhuang, Bouyei, Dong (Kam), Dai, Li (Hlai) |
| **Hmong-Mien** | Miao (Hmong, Hmu, Xong, A-Hmao), Yao, She |
| **Austronesian** | 16 recognized Taiwanese Indigenous peoples; the unrecognized Pingpu plains peoples |
| **Hui — the anomaly** | Sinophone Muslims. Fits no linguistic family, because the grouping is religious |

The Hui row matters for method. It is the case where the tier-one grouping cannot be linguistic, and it is
the proof that the tiering is about **identity**, not language. A framework that derived identity from
language alone would have to either delete the Hui or misfile them as Han, and the Chinese state itself
declined to do either in 1954 — recognizing the Hui as separate despite their not meeting the Stalinist
criteria for nationality. **This is also the single most important caveat on the language map** (see the
companion file): the Hui speak Mandarin and are therefore invisible on it, and Manchu ethnicity vastly
outnumbers Manchu speakers. **The map is evidence for the tiering, not a substitute for it.**

**Two tier-one findings from the map that the census does not show, and that bear directly on Part 2.**

- **Ethnic Mongolia is much larger than Inner Mongolia.** The map shows a Mongolian arc running the entire
  northern frontier, from eastern Xinjiang to western Manchuria, **substantially exceeding the autonomous
  region's administrative borders.** A Mongol polity drawn on ethnic lines would be a long band, not a
  province.
- **Ethnic Tibet is much larger than the TAR**, extending far east into **Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan and
  northern Yunnan**, with **Amdo labeled separately** within it.

**Both collide head-on with the whole-first-order-subdivision rule**, because the extra ground is inside
provinces that cannot be split. The consequence is stated at 3.2 entries 6 and 8 and is worth stating once
here in general form: **wherever the ethnic map is larger than the administrative map, this project must take
the whole province or none of it, and there is no third option.**

### 1.3 Owen Lattimore — the closest thing East Asia has to Woodard

**Owen Lattimore (1900–1989), *Inner Asian Frontiers of China* (American Geographical Society, 1940).** This
is the deepest and most directly usable framework in the survey, and it deserves the treatment. Full text was
retrieved and read.

**The thesis.** Lattimore refuses to write the history of East Asia as the history of China plus its effect
on neighbors. He writes it instead as the **interaction between two kinds of society — settled farming and
pastoral nomadic — each of which continuously changed the other.** Neither is the protagonist. The frontier
between them is the protagonist.

Four components, in descending order of usefulness here.

**1. The frontier is a zone, not a line — and Lattimore said so in 1940.** His opening methodological move in
Chapter VIII is to insist on the distinction between a *boundary* (a line drawn on a map) and a *frontier*
(the zone whose edge that line represents). His evidence is the Wall itself: there is not one Great Wall but
many variant, alternative and supplementary lines of fortification, readable as the tidemarks of different
periods. What politics conceived as a sharp edge, history spread into a broad and vague margin.

He then names the structure explicitly — **the boundary proper, the inner frontier zone, and the outer
frontier zone**. That is Woodard's core / domain / sphere, arrived at independently, published seventy-one
years earlier, and applied to a frontier rather than a homeland. The Latin America survey used the
independent convergence of Ribeiro and Wagley as evidence that a division was real. This convergence is
between Lattimore and the entire cultural-geography tradition Woodard draws on, and it is exact.

**2. The frontier is the limit of diminishing returns.** Lattimore's second insight is economic and it is the
one most directly portable to post-collapse design. A frontier is conventionally described as a line for
keeping barbarians out. He argues it is equally, and more usefully, **the limit of growth of an imperial
system** — the point past which the cost of conquering, garrisoning, policing and administering more ground
exceeds anything that ground will return. He makes the argument comparatively, against the Roman *limites*
and the British Indian Northwest Frontier, where the Durand Line degraded in practice into zones of
"administered" and "unadministered" tribal territory that he reads as structurally identical to Inner and
Outer Mongolia.

**This is the East Asian equivalent of Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson's mechanism**, stated forty years earlier
and without regressions: *what a territory becomes is set by what the dominant power could profitably do
there.* Dense irrigated farmland could be taxed, so it was absorbed. Steppe could not be taxed, because the
taxable asset walks away, so it was not absorbed — it was managed, bribed, married into, and walled off.

**3. The "reservoir."** Lattimore's most distinctive idea, and a genuinely strange one. When a northern
people conquered part or all of China, **part of it entered China and part stayed behind** in the belt
immediately north of the Wall. That rear guard did two jobs: it defended the conquest against rival tribes
from further north, and it served as the **reservoir from which officials and garrison troops for the rule of
China were drawn**. Beyond it lay what he calls the lands of the unregenerate — tribes that had taken no part
in the conquest. Inner Mongolia is his type case, and his conclusion is that the belt's importance exceeded
both its population and its culture: **it was the key to sovereignty over North China, and sometimes over all
of it.**

For worldbuilding this is a ready-made political geometry. The frontier belt is not a buffer or a margin. It
is the recruiting ground of whoever rules the core, which means the core's security problem and its
legitimacy problem are the same problem and sit in the same place.

**4. The structure repeats, sector by sector — and this is Lattimore's own taxonomy of the frontier zones.**
He states it directly: the boundary/inner-zone/outer-zone structure is clearest between the Great Wall and
Inner and Outer Mongolia, but it also appears in **Manchuria** (Great Wall, Chinese Pale, Willow Palisade,
with Mongols to the west and Manchus to the northeast); in **Chinese Turkestan**, taken together with the
Muslim pale of Gansu and Ningxia; and in a vaguer form in **Tibet**, where the mountain escarpment does the
work the Wall does elsewhere.

That is the whole of Part 2's structure, supplied by Lattimore in 1940, and it independently validates the
author's decision to treat each frontier zone as a separate thing.

**The standard caution, recorded.** Lattimore absorbed and often discarded the influential theories of his
day — Ellsworth Huntington's ecological determinism, Karl August Wittfogel's Marxism, and most durably Arnold
Toynbee's treatment of civilizations as organisms that are born, mature, age and die. That last inheritance
is visible in the book's shape and is the thing to discount. ⚠️ Lattimore was also the target of Joseph
McCarthy's accusations in 1950 and his reputation suffered for a generation; his 1930s misjudgment of the
Soviet purge trials is real and is acknowledged even by sympathetic biographers. None of that touches the
frontier analysis, but a reader will encounter it.

### 1.4 The Hu Line — the measured discontinuity

**Hu Huanyong (1901–1998), 1935.** A line from **Heihe**, in Heilongjiang in the far northeast, to
**Tengchong**, in Yunnan in the far southwest, which he called a geo-demographic demarcation line.

| | Area east | Population east | Area west | Population west |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1935** (map including Mongolia, excluding Taiwan) | 36% | **96%** | 64% | **4%** |
| **2002** | 43% | **94%** | 57% | **6%** |
| **2015** | 43% | **94%** | 57% | **6%** |

**The check the brief asked for comes back positive, and then some.** The area change between 1935 and the
present is almost entirely an artifact of China's recognition of Mongolian independence after Yalta, not of
anything happening on the ground. The population change is two percentage points across ninety years. Between
2000 and 2015 the western population did in fact grow faster than the eastern — concentrated in Ürümqi,
Lanzhou, Ordos and Yinchuan — and it was **not enough to move the rounded figures**.

Set against what was thrown at it, that is extraordinary. In those ninety years China experienced the
Japanese invasion, a civil war, a revolution, collectivization, the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural
Revolution, the one-child policy applied to the Han majority but relaxed for the minority populations who
overwhelmingly live *west* of the line, the largest rural-to-urban migration in human history, and the
explicit state policy of China Western Development. **The line did not move.**

**Why it holds: it is a rainfall line.** The Hu Line approximates the **400 mm annual precipitation isohyet**
— the boundary between land that will support large-scale agriculture and land that will only consistently
support pastoralism. It is not a cultural or political artifact at all. It is where the rain stops, and
therefore where the farmers stop.

**Which is why Lattimore and Hu are the same object seen twice.** Lattimore's steppe/sown frontier is
institutional and historical; Hu's line is demographic and statistical; the 400 mm isohyet is climatic; and
the Great Wall — 21,196 km of fortification whose arc, in the standard description, roughly traces the edge
of the Mongolian steppe — is the built artifact. Four independent descriptions, four disciplines, one line.
**Nothing in the Latin America survey is supported this many ways.**

**Two cautions, because the survey should not oversell it.**

- **The Hu Line is not the Great Wall and is not the Eighteen Provinces.** It runs diagonally and cuts
  through Inner Mongolia, western Sichuan, western Yunnan and western Heilongjiang. It respects no
  provincial boundary. Under this project's absolute whole-subdivision rule it is **evidence, not a
  border** — exactly the status the METHOD file assigns to the county ancestry map.
- ⚠️ The Wikipedia article's "Present statistics" section is empty, so the most recent figure independently
  confirmed here is 2015. A 2020-census recomputation was not located in this session.

### 1.5 Two more measured discontinuities, both on the Qinling–Huaihe line

The **Qinling–Huaihe Line** runs from the Qin Mountains in the west to the Huai River in the east, roughly
along the 33rd parallel, and was formalized by the geographer **Zhang Xiangwen in 1908**. It coincides with
the **800 mm annual precipitation isohyet**, the **0 °C average January isotherm**, the divide between the
Yellow and Yangtze drainage basins, and the wheat/rice boundary. It was the border between the Northern and
Southern Dynasties in the third to sixth centuries and between the Southern Song and the Jurchen Jin in the
thirteenth. It is the internal seam of the Han core, and it has been a political border twice.

**Chen, Ebenstein, Greenstone & Li, "Evidence on the impact of sustained exposure to air pollution on life
expectancy from China's Huai River policy," *PNAS* (2013).** In the 1950s, China decided that subsidized
district heating — coal-fired — would be installed only north of the Huai. Nothing about the choice was
demographic or cultural; it was a line on a map and a budget. Using a **regression discontinuity design on
distance from the river**, the authors find ambient total suspended particulates about **184 µg/m³, or 55%,
higher on the north side**, and life expectancy about **5.5 years lower**, from cardiorespiratory mortality.
They estimate the policy costs northern China's 500 million residents more than **2.5 billion life-years**. A
2017 follow-up (Ebenstein, Fan, Greenstone, He & Zhou, *PNAS*) puts the general relationship at **0.64 years
of life expectancy per 10 µg/m³ of PM10**.

**Talhelm, Zhang, Oishi, Shimin, Duan, Lan & Kitayama, "Large-Scale Psychological Differences Within China
Explained by Rice Versus Wheat Agriculture," *Science* (2014).** The authors propose that a history of
farming **rice** — which requires coordinated irrigation and shared labor across household boundaries —
produces interdependent, holistic-thinking cultures, while farming **wheat**, which a family can do alone,
produces independent, analytic ones. They tested **1,162 Han Chinese participants at six sites** and found
rice-growing southern China significantly more interdependent and holistic than the wheat-growing north. The
design move that matters: to rule out climate, wealth and disease as confounds, **they tested people from
neighboring counties along the rice–wheat border, and found differences just as large there** as across the
whole country. Modernization and pathogen-prevalence explanations did not fit.

**Why these belong in this survey.** The Latin America file's central methodological claim was that Dell's
mita result is the cleanest existing demonstration of Woodard's premise — an institution abolished two
centuries ago, still legible in household data, across a line a viceroy drew. Talhelm's design is the same
design. A farming practice adopted millennia ago is still legible in how people reason, across a line that
the rain drew, **measured between adjacent counties on either side of it**. And the Huai River result shows
the same boundary producing a five-and-a-half-year life-expectancy gap from a 1950s heating budget.

**The general rule this yields, for reuse:** in East Asia, *the lines that persist are the ones the climate
drew, and the state's own arbitrary lines acquire permanence by being laid on top of them.* The Huai policy
line was arbitrary; it stuck because it was drawn along a boundary that was already there for other reasons.

### 1.6 The Sinosphere, and the Hua–Yi distinction

**The Sinosphere** (漢字文化圏 / 東亜文化圏, the Chinese-character cultural sphere) is the standard
civilizational-sphere framework. The term was coined by the Japanese historian **Nishijima Sadao
(1919–1998)** of the University of Tokyo, calqued on the German *Kulturkreis* of Fritz Graebner and Wilhelm
Schmidt via a 1941 Chinese translation, and subsequently re-borrowed into Chinese as a loanword.

**Membership:** China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam. Historically also the **Ryukyu Kingdom**. Vietnam is out of
scope here but is the standard fourth member and its inclusion is the point — the sphere is not coextensive
with East Asia and does not follow the region's physical geography.

**The shared inventory:** Confucianism, East Asian Buddhism, Taoist elements; Literary Chinese as the written
lingua franca of bureaucracy and scholarship; Chinese characters adapted locally as **kanji** in Japan,
**hanja** in Korea and **chữ Hán** in Vietnam; and the Confucian-based **imperial examination system**, which
shaped the bureaucracy and social structure of Korea and Vietnam directly. Each member eventually built its
own phonographic script — Japanese kana, Korean hangul, Vietnamese chữ Nôm then the Latin alphabet — but
Literary Chinese or modified forms of it (*kanbun*, the Idu script) stayed in official use into the modern
era.

**Four framings worth having, because they disagree usefully:**

- **Nishijima** — a cultural sphere defined by shared philosophy, religion and political structure.
- **Arnold Toynbee** — a "Sinic civilization" in the Yellow River basin growing into a "Far Eastern
  civilization" that extended to the Yangtze and into Korea and Japan, the commonalities being the residue of
  a "Sinic universal state."
- **Edwin Reischauer** — the "Sinic world," a group of centralized states sharing a Confucian ethical
  philosophy, with elites bound by a common written language, explicitly analogized to Latin in Europe.
- **Samuel Huntington** — Sinic civilization as one of the post-Cold War civilizations, and notably **the
  only one in his scheme based on cultural rather than religious identity**. He places **Japan outside it**,
  as a civilization of its own. For this project that dissent is load-bearing: canon already has Japan as a
  separate polity, and the most-cited civilizational taxonomy agrees.

**The Hua–Yi distinction (華夷之辨)** is the sphere's internal membership rule and the more useful object of
the two. It sorts the world into *Hua* (civilized, i.e. participating in the ritual, textual and political
order) and *Yi* (outside it). Its crucial property is that **it is a cultural test, not a descent test** — a
people could move across it in either direction, and dynasties founded by peoples from outside the Wall
repeatedly claimed the *Hua* side and were eventually granted it. This is structurally identical to Woodard's
"nationhood is acquired, not inherited," and it is two thousand years older.

**How the tributary system actually worked, in one paragraph, because it is often mis-stated.** It was a
ritual and diplomatic order in which peripheral rulers acknowledged the Chinese emperor's centrality,
received investiture, and exchanged gifts under a framework that was simultaneously a trade licensing
regime. It was not an empire and it did not generally involve administration of the tributary, taxation of
it, or garrisons in it. **For a post-collapse setting this is the single most usable institution in the
Sinosphere material**, because it is a model of hierarchy without occupation — a way for a large surviving
core to be the acknowledged center of a region it cannot afford to govern. That is precisely the condition a
post-catastrophe Han core would be in.

### 1.7 James C. Scott and Zomia

**Zomia** was coined in 2002 by the historian **Willem van Schendel** (University of Amsterdam), from *Zomi*,
a highlander self-term common to several Tibeto-Burman languages of the India–Bangladesh–Myanmar borderland.
It names the mass of upland mainland Asia historically beyond the control of lowland states. **James C.
Scott** (Yale) made it famous in *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast
Asia* (2009).

**Scott's argument.** The standard reading of hill peoples — as living ancestors, as what the lowlands were
before rice, Buddhism and civilization — inverts the actual history. Hill societies are better understood as
**runaway, fugitive, maroon communities**, populations that over two millennia fled the state-making projects
of the valleys: slavery, conscription, taxation, corvée, epidemic and war. Their dispersal, their swidden
agriculture, their oral rather than written traditions, their segmentary social structures and even their
mobility are read as *deliberate state-evading adaptations* rather than as backwardness. They produce a
surplus; they simply do not use it to support kings and monks.

**Relevance and limits for East Asia.** The massif's Chinese wing is **Yunnan, Guizhou and western Sichuan** —
the terrain of Skinner's Yungui macroregion. The anthropologist **Jean Michaud** proposed the parallel term
**Southeast Asian Massif** in 1997 for highlands above roughly 300 m across ten countries, with an Indigenous
population of about **100 million**, crossed by six language families (Austroasiatic, Hmong-Mien, Kra-Dai,
Sino-Tibetan, Indo-European, Austronesian) none of which is a majority. The region has **never been united
politically** — not as an empire, not as a set of feuding kingdoms, not even as a zone of harmonized
political systems.

**Two boundaries on the concept matter here.** First, Michaud explicitly **excludes Tibet** from the massif,
on the grounds that Tibet had a centralized, religiously unified core and a long distinct political
existence — a state, in other words, which is exactly what the massif's societies did not develop. Tibet
therefore cannot be filed under Zomia and needs its own treatment (2.4). Second, ⚠️ Zomia is contested:
critics hold that it is fragmented and marginal enough that it may lack the coherence to function as an area
of study at all, and Scott's flight-from-the-state reading is not a settled consensus.

**What Zomia is for, in this framework.** It is the direct analogue of Woodard's **Far West** — a region
whose character is set by terrain's hostility to normal state-building rather than by any settler group's
values — and per the Part Four checklist (item 6) a continental framework should contain at least one such
case. East Asia's is Yungui, and Yungui sits **inside** the Eighteen Provinces, which is why it lands in
section 3.3 rather than being a clean separate region.

### 1.8 The Eighteen Provinces, and why they do not map onto modern subdivisions

**The unit.** The Ming governed fifteen administrative entities — thirteen provinces plus two directly
governed areas. The Qing, having conquered from outside, **kept the Ming system for the former Ming lands and
did not apply it to their other domains** — Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet were governed by separate
apparatus. Minor reform turned the Ming fifteen into the **Eighteen Provinces (內地十八省)**, and it is these
eighteen that early Western sources meant by "China proper."

**Two things the Qing arrangement tells us that matter more than the list itself.** First, the Qing
themselves drew exactly the line the author has drawn: a core administered one way, frontiers administered
another. Second, the Qing were explicit in their own ideology that this was *not* an ethnic boundary — they
identified their whole state as China (**Dulimbai Gurun** in Manchu), defined it as multi-ethnic, and used
the formula 內外一家, "interior and exterior as one family." Some Han scholar-officials objected and insisted
on reserving *Zhongguo* for the culturally Han territory; Wei Yuan's early nineteenth-century *Shengwuji*
calls the Inner Asian polities *guo* while reserving *Zhongguo* for the provinces and the three eastern
provinces of Manchuria. **The argument the author is adjudicating is two centuries old and was had in
Chinese.**

**The late-Qing extension attempt, which is where several of Part 2's regions come from.** Toward the end,
the Qing tried to extend the provincial system outward. **Xinjiang became a province in 1884. Taiwan became a
separate province in 1885** (it had been administered under Fujian since 1683) **and was ceded to Japan in
1895. Manchuria was split into Fengtian, Jilin and Heilongjiang in 1907.** Equivalent proposals for Tibet,
Qinghai, Inner Mongolia and Outer Mongolia were discussed and **not implemented**, so those four were outside
the provincial system when the dynasty fell in 1912. That list of four is close to the list of frontier
regions still contested a century later, which is not a coincidence.

**⚠️ The problem for this project, stated because the whole-subdivision rule makes it unavoidable.** The Qing
eighteen do not map onto modern first-order subdivisions. The documented changes:

| Modern unit | Carved from | Consequence for a "Sinian Federation = the eighteen" draw |
|---|---|---|
| **Beijing**, **Tianjin** | Zhili (renamed Hebei) | Municipalities; clearly inside |
| **Shanghai** | Jiangsu | Municipality; clearly inside |
| **Chongqing** | Sichuan | Municipality; clearly inside |
| **Hainan** | Guangdong | Island province; inside on inheritance, arguable on geography |
| **Ningxia** | Gansu | **Now a Hui autonomous region.** Inside by inheritance, a minority homeland by designation |
| **Guangxi** | was itself one of the eighteen | **Now a Zhuang autonomous region.** Same collision, at 19.6 million people |

So the honest statement is: **the Eighteen Provinces are a defensible historical basis for the Sinian
Federation and they are not a drawable modern boundary without four explicit decisions.** Those decisions are
set out and costed at 3.3.

### 1.9 G. William Skinner's macroregions — the internal map

**G. William Skinner (1925–2008)**, anthropologist, is the author of the framework this project most needs
and the brief did not name. Two contributions.

**"Marketing and Social Structure in Rural China" (1964–65)**, a three-part article that remains a
cornerstone of the field. Skinner showed that periodic rural marketplaces knitted villages into cohesive
networks and linked them upward to urban centers, and — the part that matters here — that **these marketing
systems, not administrative units, set the real social and cultural boundaries**, including the boundaries of
kinship, dialect, class and religion. That is a Woodard-shaped claim: the units that matter are not the ones
on the government's map.

**The nine physiographic macroregions**, set out in *The City in Late Imperial China*. Skinner divided China
proper according to the **drainage basins of the major rivers and other travel-constraining landforms**,
arguing that before mechanized transport, terrain governed movement and therefore governed economy, and that
the resulting regions each had their own resources, their own culture and their own **unsynchronized
development cycles** — they boomed and declined on separate clocks.

| # | Macroregion | Rough modern coverage |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | **Northeast** 东北区 | Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang |
| 20 | **North China** 华北区 | Hebei, Shandong, Henan, Shanxi, Beijing, Tianjin |
| 30 | **Northwest** 西北区 | Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia — sub-divided into the Wei-Fen basins, the upper Yellow River, and the **Gansu Corridor** |
| 40 | **Upper Yangtze** 长江上游区 | Sichuan, Chongqing |
| 50 | **Middle Yangtze** 长江中游区 | Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi — sub-divided into Middle Yangtze proper, the Gan basin, the Yuan basin, the upper Han |
| 60 | **Lower Yangtze** 长江下游区 | Jiangsu, Anhui, Shanghai, northern Zhejiang |
| 70 | **Southeast Coast** 东南沿海区 | Fujian, eastern Guangdong, southern Zhejiang, **and Taiwan** — sub-divided into the Ou-Ling basins, the Min basin, Zhang-Quan, the Han basin, and Taiwan |
| 80 | **Lingnan** 岭南区 | Guangdong and Guangxi — the two Guang, *Liangguang* |
| 90 | **Yungui** 云贵区 | most of Yunnan, most of Guizhou — the Yungui Plateau |

**Three findings fall out of this table that shape Part 3.**

- **Skinner excludes Xinjiang, Tibet, Qinghai and most of Inner Mongolia outright.** He is not making a
  political claim; he is saying his instrument does not apply there, because those places are not organized
  by a Chinese river basin and a Chinese marketing hierarchy. That is an independent, non-political,
  methodologically motivated vote for the author's Han-core/frontier split.
- **Skinner puts Taiwan inside the Southeast Coast macroregion**, as one of its five subdivisions alongside
  the Min basin and Zhang-Quan. That is the Hokkien connection stated economically: Taiwan's Han settlement
  came from exactly that coast and spoke exactly its language. It is the strongest available argument
  *against* treating Taiwan as fully separate, and it should be recorded on the case-against side at 3.2.
- **Skinner includes the Northeast** as macroregion 10, despite it being outside the Wall and outside the
  eighteen. He is describing the twentieth-century economy, and by then Chuang Guandong had made the
  Northeast a Chinese macroregion. That tension is the Manchuria problem in a single line.

### 1.10 New Qing History — why the frontier zones are separate things

A historiographical school that emerged in the United States in the mid-1990s, and the scholarly basis for
treating Part 2's zones as distinct rather than as Chinese provinces with unusual demographics.

**The origin.** American scholars began learning **Manchu** in the 1980s and working the Manchu-language
archives in Beijing and Taipei, which had been largely ignored on the assumption that they were translations
of Chinese documents. **Joseph Fletcher** (Harvard) discredited that assumption; **Beatrice Bartlett** (Yale)
showed that the Yongzhong emperor's Grand Council operated **only in Manchu until the 1730s**, that many
important edicts and memorials have no Chinese version, and that the Manchu archives are likelier to be
complete because they were less often raided or weeded. ⚠️ The transliteration "Yongzhong" here should read
**Yongzheng**; corrected from general knowledge, the underlying claim is as retrieved.

**The argument.** **Evelyn Rawski's** 1996 presidential address to the Association for Asian Studies,
"Re-envisioning the Qing," attacked the sinicization thesis that **Ping-ti Ho** had set out in 1967. Rawski's
position: the Qing ruled China successfully for nearly three centuries **not** by becoming Chinese but by
**retaining Manchu characteristics** and using them to hold together a multi-ethnic empire of Manchus, Han,
Mongols, Tibetans and Uyghurs. The emperor performed different roles for different subject populations —
Confucian Son of Heaven for the Han, **Bogda Khan** for the Mongols, **wheel-turning king** for Tibetan
Buddhists — and often did so out of sight of his Han ministers. The Kangxi emperor's summer capital at
**Chengde, north of the Wall**, later enlarged by Qianlong to include a replica of the Potala, is the
physical argument.

**The associated claims that matter here.** The school dates the empire from **1636**, when the dynasty was
proclaimed, rather than 1644, when it took Beijing. **Mark Elliott** argues acculturation and differentiation
were both essential, that the cultural gap narrowed but the ethnic boundary did not close, and that adopting
Chinese institutions was not the same as becoming Chinese. **Peter C. Perdue** describes the Qing as a
colonial empire comparable to the Ottoman, Mughal and Romanov empires — and argues it was not only a victim
of imperialism but a practitioner of it. The military expansion of the frontiers was expensive and drained
resources from the rest of China, which is Lattimore's diminishing-returns point arriving from the archives.

**⚠️ It is contested, and the dissent is not only nationalist.** Ping-ti Ho's rebuttal, "In Defense of
Sinicization," holds that the pattern of Chinese history is for conquest dynasties to adopt Chinese rule and
culture. **Zhao Gang** argues from the evidence that the Qing self-identified as China. Some Chinese scholars
read the school as importing American anxieties about race and identity. **Mark Elliott himself** insists
the school does not separate the Qing from China but asks how the changing concept of "China" was used at the
time. Take the school as a well-founded reason to treat Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet as things the
Qing governed *differently* — which is a documentary fact — rather than as an argument about sovereignty.

### 1.11 What is genuinely missing

To state it once, plainly, as the Latin America survey did:

**There is no published map of East Asian culture-nations with bounded territories, cores, domains and
spheres.** Lattimore maps a frontier and its zones, brilliantly, but only the frontier — the Han interior is
background in his book. Hu draws one line. Skinner maps the Han core's macroregions but explicitly stops at
the frontier and is describing economies rather than cultures. The Sinosphere literature names a sphere and
does not draw edges. Zomia names a terrain and is deliberately, programmatically anti-cartographic. New Qing
History is archival historiography and produces no map at all.

And there is a second gap, specific to this project: **nobody has drawn the Han core as a federation of its
linguistic nations.** Skinner's macroregions and the dialect groups exist as separate literatures and, so far
as this pass found, have not been systematically overlaid. Section 3.1 is a first attempt at that overlay,
and it is the most original thing in this file.

## Part 2 — The frontier zones

Lattimore's own sector taxonomy (1.3, point 4) supplies the order and the structure: each zone gets a
boundary, an inner frontier and an outer frontier.

### 2.1 Manchuria — the one true Doctrine of First Effective Settlement case

**This is where Woodard's doctrine applies literally, and it is the only place in East Asia where it does.**
It deserves the space.

**The ban.** Manchuria — *Guandong*, "east of the pass," after the **Shanhai Pass** at the Wall's eastern
end — was thinly peopled, mainly by Tungusic peoples. There had been a substantial Han population under the
Ming, reaching **over three million** by the dynasty's end, but the Qing conquest evicted and assimilated it.
The **Shunzhi** emperor built the **Willow Palisade** to mark and police the boundary, and in **1668 the
Kangxi emperor decreed a further prohibition** on non-Eight-Banner people relocating there. Only Manchu,
Mongol and Han bannermen were admitted. The reason is New Qing History's reason: it was the dynasty's own
homeland, held in reserve as a Manchu reservation.

**The ban failed continuously for two centuries.** Manchu landlords wanted Han tenants to work their land and
pay rent, so most Han migrants who crossed the Wall and the Palisade were not evicted. By the eighteenth
century Han were farming **500,000 hectares** of privately held land in Manchuria plus **203,583 hectares**
of courier-station, noble-estate and banner land, and made up **80% of the population of garrisons and
towns**. The **Qianlong emperor issued edicts banning settlement from 1740 to 1776 and simultaneously
admitted Han peasants fleeing drought.** By **1800 Han were the majority in Manchuria's urban areas**; under
the **Daoguang** emperor the treasury sold formerly Manchu land along the Songhua to Han buyers; by the
**1840s** Han filled most of Manchuria's towns.

**The reversal, and its cause — which is Russia.** The emptiness of the northeastern borderlands is precisely
what let the Russian Empire annex **Outer Manchuria**, north of the Amur and east of the Ussuri, by the
**Treaty of Aigun (1858)** and the **Convention of Peking (1860)**. In **1860 Tepuqin**, military governor of
Heilongjiang, proposed opening Guandong to Han civilian farmers explicitly **to forestall further Russian
annexation**. The Qing changed policy and began encouraging poor farmers from Zhili (now Hebei) and Shandong
to move north, opening district after district.

**The migration, counted.** Exact numbers are impossible — many walked, and the statistical apparatus was
rudimentary — but from Chinese Maritime Customs and South Manchuria Railway records, the historians **Thomas
Gottschang and Diana Lary** estimate that between **1891 and 1942** some **25.4 million** people arrived in
Manchuria from south of the Wall and **16.7 million** returned, a **net 8.7 million** over half a century.
Migrants were overwhelmingly poor farmers from Shandong, traveling overland through the Shanhai Pass or by
the Yantai–Lüshun ferry across the Bohai. The local Han population passed **20 million before the Second
Sino-Japanese War**, and organized post-1949 settlement to "develop the Great Northern Wilderness" (开发北大
荒) eventually pushed the region past **100 million**.

**Why this is the Woodard case.** Every element of the doctrine is present and dated: a territory with a
sparse prior population; a state-enforced closure; an opening; a specific founding population with a known
origin province and a known route; and a resulting society whose character was set by those founders rather
than by the prior inhabitants. **This is the only place in the region where you can name the year the answer
changed.**

**Manchu today.** The **2020 census counts 10,423,303 Manchus** — the fifth-largest minority — which is a
large number attached to a nearly erased culture. Two figures tell the story: the population was **10,387,958
in 2010 and 10,708,464 in 2000**, so it is flat or slightly declining while every other large minority grows;
and **fewer than 100 skilled native speakers of Manchu remain**, most of them in **Sanjiazi**, Heilongjiang.
Revival efforts exist — Manchu classes in Liaoning, Jilin and Heilongjiang schools, volunteer teaching, and
thousands of non-Manchu learners — but the language is functionally gone. ⚠️ Manchu survives in a related
form as **Xibe**, spoken by 191,911 Sibe (2020 census), most of them in **Xinjiang**, descended from a Qing
garrison posted there in the eighteenth century and thereby preserved by distance from the homeland.

**The industrial history.** The Northeast was **one of the first parts of China to industrialize**, and the
foundation was laid under **Japanese** control — the sphere of influence from 1905 and then **Manchukuo**,
under which many enterprises that later became Chinese state-owned firms were built. After 1949 it was the
planned economy's flagship, nicknamed **"the Republic's eldest son"** (共和国长子). **Anshan Iron and Steel**
was its most important enterprise until the **Daqing oil field** was found in **1959**. Its industries were
and are steel, automobiles, shipbuilding, aircraft and petroleum refining; it also holds the country's
largest expanse of arable flatland and its fertile black soil, producing **over 20% of China's staple food**
in 2020.

**And its collapse.** Reform and opening favored the coastal south. State-enterprise mass layoffs in the late
1990s — *xiagang* (下岗) — gutted the workforce; skilled people have been leaving since 2000; the region is
routinely called China's Rust Belt. The **Northeast Area Revitalization Plan** launched in 2003 has not
reversed it. **Heilongjiang had the second-lowest GDP per head of any Chinese province in 2023 and the
fastest-declining population in the country.** Total regional population is about **107.4 million, roughly 8%
of China's**. There is even a cultural formation around the decline — the "Dongbei renaissance," nostalgia
for the 1970s boom mixed with self-deprecation and speculation about the region's future.

**For post-collapse design this is the most useful fact in Part 2:** the Northeast is a region with heavy
industry, food surplus, a coherent regional identity, a live sense of grievance against the coastal core, and
a hundred-year-old habit of thinking of itself as having been the country's most important place. That is a
successor state's raw material, and it is already assembled.

**Where it abuts:** Russia to the north and east (out of scope), Korea across the Yalu and Tumen, Inner
Mongolia to the west — and note that five eastern Inner Mongolian prefectures (Hulunbuir, Hinggan, Tongliao,
Chifeng, Xilin Gol) are formally defined as part of the Northeast under the 2003 revitalization plan.

### 2.2 Inner Mongolia and Outer Mongolia — why one became a province and one a state

**The Qing arrangement, which set up everything after it.** From **1691** the Mongols were ruled by the Qing,
and it is under Qing administration that northern and southern Mongolia became **Outer** and **Inner**. The
dynasty promoted Tibetan Buddhism and built monasteries that grew rich and powerful, and it **forbade
ordinary Mongols from crossing their own banner borders** — not only into the eighteen provinces, but into
other Mongol banners — under serious penalty, explicitly to keep the Mongols divided. Pilgrims needed
passports. **The Qing kept the Mongols fragmented as policy, and Inner Mongolia is where that policy was
combined with Han settlement.**

**The divergence is a story about distance and Russia.** Han settlers were moving illegally into the Inner
Mongolian steppe through the eighteenth century — by **1791** there were enough in the Front Gorlos Banner
that the local *jasak* petitioned to legalize them. In the nineteenth century, facing the same Russian
pressure that reopened Manchuria, the Qing **encouraged** Han farming settlement in both Mongolia and
Manchuria; railways accelerated it; Mongol princes sold or leased land, or it was simply taken from
pastoralists and given to farmers. Inner Mongolia was close, reachable, and partly arable. Outer Mongolia was
far, dry and beyond the practical reach of the same process.

**Outer Mongolia's route to statehood, dated.** **1911**, the Qing collapse — Outer Mongolia declares
independence under the 8th Jebtsundamba Khutuktu as the **Bogd Khan**, and calls on Inner Mongolia to join
it. **1912**, treaty with Russia. **1915**, the Treaty of Kyakhta — autonomy under Republic of China
suzerainty. **1919**, ROC troops enter the capital and depose the Bogd Khan. **1921**, the Mongolian People's
Revolution: Damdin Sükhbaatar's cavalry, backed by Soviet troops, takes the capital. **1924**, the Mongolian
People's Republic, the world's second communist state. **February 1945**, Yalta — the Allies agree to
recognize the status quo in Mongolia as a condition of Soviet entry against Japan. **1945**, the Sino-Soviet
Treaty: Chiang Kai-shek is persuaded to recognize Mongolian independence in exchange for Stalin's promise not
to support the CCP. **October 1945**, an independence referendum passes. **1946**, formal ROC recognition.
**1961**, UN membership, after the Soviet Union threatened to veto the admission of newly decolonized African
states if the ROC (by then on Taiwan, having withdrawn recognition and renewed its claim) used its veto
again.

**Two footnotes with design value.** The **1950 Sino-Soviet Treaty** guaranteed Outer Mongolia's independence
and thereby **ended Choibalsan's hopes of reuniting it with Inner Mongolia**. And Mao privately hoped for
reintegration, raising it in 1949 and again in 1954; the Soviet answer was that the Mongols were free to
decide. **Pan-Mongolism is a real historical project that was defeated twice by great-power arithmetic, not a
fictional invention** — which makes it the single most plausible irredentist program available in the
setting.

**Inner Mongolia's demographic reality, which is the case against a Mongol polity there.**

| | Han | Mongol |
|---|---|---|
| **1947**, at the autonomous region's founding | **83.6%** | **14.8%** |
| **2020 census** | **78.7%** | **17.7%** |

**Mongols have been a minority in Inner Mongolia since before the region existed.** The Han share has fallen
by five points in seventy-three years. But the absolute numbers cut the other way: **over 4 million Mongols
in Inner Mongolia, which is the largest Mongol population in the world — larger than the country of
Mongolia's.** And they are concentrated: **over 70% of Inner Mongolia's Mongols live in under 18% of its
territory**, in the **Hinggan League and the prefectures of Tongliao and Chifeng** — all in the east, all
inside the zone the 2003 plan assigns to the Northeast.

**The assimilation evidence is the strongest in the survey, and it points away from separatism.** Mongolian
is spoken by roughly half of China's Mongols; about **2.1 million speakers in Inner Mongolia** against 4.1
million ethnic Mongols, and use has declined since the 1980s in a documented cycle of decline and
revival
(late Qing decline, 1947–65 revival, 1966–76 decline, 1977–92 revival, 1995–2012 decline). Mandarin has been
the region's sole official language since 2021. **Intermarriage is very common** — by the 1980s nearly 40% of
marriages involving a Mongol in the former Jirim League were Mongol–Han — and the sources are explicit that
this **stands in very sharp contrast to Tibetans and Uyghurs in their regions**. Highly educated Mongols tend
to migrate to cities and become socially indistinct from Han. Children of mixed marriages are registered as
Mongol and identify as Mongol.

**Read that carefully, because it is a genuinely interesting finding rather than a simple one.** The
*language* is going and the *identity* is not. The urban Chinese-speaking Mongol identity looks likely to
survive on the strength of urban ethnic community alone, and people who have wholly lost the language — the
Tumets are named as an example — remain registered and self-identified as Mongols. **That is Woodard's
"nationhood is acquired, not inherited" running in the other direction: identity outliving its own cultural
content.** For a post-collapse setting it means an Inner Mongolian Mongol population that is large, dispersed,
bilingual, intermarried, urban, and still a people.

**Political scaffolding worth noting:** the chairman of the regional government must legally be a Mongol,
though the party secretary who outranks him is usually Han and from elsewhere; and the Mongol share of the
regional People's Congress has been **fixed at 37–39% since 1954**, roughly double the population share. ⚠️
During the Cultural Revolution, Ulanhu's administration was purged and repression followed: **75% of the
victims in Inner Mongolia were Mongols, who were about 10% of the population**. In **1969** the region was
dismembered among neighboring provinces, and reassembled only in **1979**.

### 2.3 Xinjiang / East Turkestan

**Founding character: Turco-Persianate, not Sinic.** This is the clearest civilizational break in the survey.

**The terrain does the sorting.** High ranges split the region into the **Dzungarian Basin** in the north and
the **Tarim Basin** in the south, and **only about 9.7% of Xinjiang's land area is habitable** — oases at the
feet of the Tian Shan, Kunlun and Altai. Xinjiang is **more than a sixth of China's total territory** and a
quarter of its boundary length.

**The deep population.** Before the Xiongnu or the Han, the Tarim was inhabited by peoples who were probably
part of the Indo-European migrations — **Tocharians** at Turfan and Kucha, **Saka** centered on Shule and
Khotan, along with Tibeto-Burman groups related to the Qiang. Turkic and then Islamic layers arrived later.
The oasis city-states of the Tarim rim — Kashgar, Khotan, Yarkand, Aksu, Turfan — are the founding political
form: not a steppe empire and not a Chinese province, but a chain of irrigated market towns on the caravan
routes, each one a small polity, each one dependent on meltwater.

**The Dzungar destruction, which is the region's founding trauma and its demographic reset.** The Qing fought
the Dzungar Khanate through the seventeenth century and into the eighteenth. In **1755**, with help from the
Oirat noble **Amursana**, they took Ghulja and captured the Dzungar khan; when Amursana's own claim was
ignored he revolted, and over the next two years Qing armies destroyed what remained. Combined campaign and a
simultaneous smallpox epidemic killed an estimated **80% of the 600,000 or more Dzungars**; Wei Yuan
described thousands of *li* of empty plain. **Recovery took generations, and the Qing resettled the emptied
north with Han, Hui and Uyghur settlers.**

**The demographic record, which is the whole argument in one column.**

| Year | Uyghur | Han |
|---|---|---|
| Early 19th c. (Qing census, ethnic shares) | ~60% Turkic | ~30% |
| **1953** (first modern census; total pop. 4,873,608) | **74.7%** (3,640,125) | 25.3% Han *and all others combined* |
| **1978** | 46% | 40% |
| **2000** | 45.21% | 40.57% |
| **2010** | 45.84% | 40.48% |
| **2020** | **44.96%** | **42.24%** |

Two readings, and the survey should give both. The nationalist reading is a collapse from three-quarters to
under half in seventy years. **Stanley W. Toops's** reading is that the present balance closely resembles the
**early Qing** balance, and that the 1953 figure is the anomaly — the product of political killings and
expulsions of non-Uyghurs during the uprisings of the 1860s and 1930s. Both are true. **The Han share was
restored, not created.**

**Where people actually are, which matters more than the totals.** Han and Hui live overwhelmingly in
**Dzungaria** and the east — Ürümqi, Karamay, Shihezi, and the Changji, Bortala, Bayingolin, Ili and Kumul
prefectures; **55% of the Han population, mainly urban, is in the north.** Uyghurs are in the southwest:
**about 80% of Xinjiang's Uyghurs live in four prefectures — Kashgar, Khotan, Kizilsu and Aksu — where they
are roughly 90% of the population**, plus Turpan in the east. **Southern Xinjiang is about 95% non-Han and
has an average income roughly half the regional average.** Kazakhs concentrate in Ili and are the majority in
the far north.

**This is the survey's sharpest collision with the whole-subdivision rule.** Dzungaria and the Tarim are two
different countries by founding population, by present demography, by economy and by income. The rule forbids
separating them. Noted at 3.2 as the case against.

**Institutions.** The **Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (XPCC, 兵团)**, created **1954** to settle
demobilized soldiers on the land, hold the Soviet border and develop the economy, is the region's
characteristic institution and has no parallel elsewhere in China: **3.6 million people, about 14% of the
population, producing roughly 19.7% of the economy.** Xinjiang became a province in **1884** and an
autonomous region in **1955**; more than 50% of its area is designated autonomous areas **for thirteen
non-Uyghur groups**, which is its own kind of fragmentation. Modern Uyghur identity in its current form dates
to **1955**, when the PRC recognized previously separately self-identifying oasis peoples as one nationality
— the identity is old, its unified form is recent, and that is worth knowing.

**Political history of the separate state.** A Soviet-backed **Second East Turkestan Republic** existed
**1944–49** in what is now Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture; a coalition government with the ROC was agreed
in 1946 and collapsed in 1947; the PLA entered in 1949, and five ETR leaders traveling to negotiate died in
an air crash near Kabansk that year. **The name East Turkestan, the territory and the precedent all exist.**

**The resource base**, which is what makes it strategically non-optional for any Chinese successor state:
over a fifth of China's hydrocarbons and the highest concentration of fossil fuel reserves of any region;
**about 40% of national coal reserves**, roughly 2.2 trillion tonnes; the West-East Gas Pipeline to Shanghai;
**84% of Chinese cotton**, with China supplying 26% of global cotton exports; and **45% of global
solar-grade polysilicon production in 2020**.

**Where it abuts:** Kazakhstan (exists in canon, out of scope), Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan,
Pakistan, Mongolia, Tibet.

### 2.4 Tibet — a separate civilization, and the water

**Founding character.** A distinct civilization with its own script, its own branch of Buddhism, its own state
tradition and its own aristocracy. Michaud excludes it from the Southeast Asian Massif precisely because,
unlike the massif's societies, **Tibet built a centralized, religiously unified state with a long
independent political existence.** That is the case for separateness stated by a scholar who had no stake in
it.

**The state tradition, dated.** Central Tibet (**Ü-Tsang**) was ruled from Nêdong, Shigatse and Lhasa from
1354 to 1642. In **1642** the **Ganden Phodrang** government of the 5th Dalai Lama was established by **Güshi
Khan** of the Khoshut Khanate. The Khoshuts ruled until **1717**, when the Dzungars overthrew them; the
Dzungars were expelled by the Qing expedition of **1720**, beginning Qing overlordship. Some historians hold
that Tibet under the Ganden Phodrang (**1642–1951**) was an independent state under varying foreign
suzerainties. In practice Tibet was **outside the Qing provincial system when the dynasty fell in 1912** and
functioned independently until 1951.

**The modern sequence.** **1950**, the PLA defeats the Tibetan army near Chamdo. **1951**, the Seventeen
Point Agreement; ratified by the 14th Dalai Lama in October. **10 March 1959**, the Lhasa uprising fails; the
Dalai Lama flees to India and renounces the agreement as signed under duress. **1965**, the **Tibet
Autonomous Region** is established, replacing the ROC-era Tibet Area.

**The TAR is not Tibet, and this is the most important practical fact in the section.** The TAR's borders
were generally set in the eighteenth century and **include about half of cultural Tibet**. Amdo is largely
**Qinghai**; Kham is split between the TAR and **Sichuan** — eastern Kham was added to Sichuan under the
Qing. So:

| | Figure |
|---|---|
| TAR area | over **1,200,000 km²** — second-largest province-level division |
| TAR population | about **3.6 million**, roughly **3 per km²** |
| TAR ethnic Tibetan share (2020) | **86.0%** |
| TAR Han share | **12.2%**, mostly recent migrants; as of 2010 only 8% of Han in the TAR held household registration there |
| **Tibetans in China, 2020 census** | **7,060,731** — so **roughly half of all Tibetans live outside the TAR** |
| TAR urbanization, 2024 | **39.7%** — among the lowest in China, up from 22.6% in 2011 |

**Under the whole-subdivision rule, "Tibet" therefore means TAR alone, or TAR plus Qinghai, and nothing
else.** Kham's Sichuan portion cannot be recovered without splitting Sichuan, which is forbidden. This is a
hard constraint and should be stated to the author rather than finessed.

**The water, which is the strategic fact.** The **Tibetan Plateau** is about **2,500,000 km²**, roughly
1,000 km north to south and 2,500 km east to west, with an average elevation above **4,500 m**. It contains
the headwaters of most of the rivers of surrounding Asia, including the three longest — the **Yellow**, the
**Yangtze** and the **Mekong** — and the **Salween**, the **Brahmaputra** and the **Indus**, the last rising
near Lake Manasarovar in the west. Its tens of thousands of glaciers make it a natural water tower, and it is
called the **Third Pole** because its ice fields hold **the largest reserve of fresh water outside the polar
regions**.

**Whoever holds Tibet holds the water supply of China, Southeast Asia and South Asia.** In a post-collapse
setting that is not a metaphor; it is the region's single most valuable asset, and it is held by 3.6 million
people at three per square kilometer.

**And it is degrading.** Temperatures on the plateau are rising about **four times faster than elsewhere in
China**; over the past five decades **80% of the glaciers have retreated**, losing about 4.5% of their
combined area; permafrost thaw is a further hazard. Near-term this means expanding lakes, floods and
mudflows. Long-term it means the rivers lose their regulator. **A setting that wants a resource war in Asia
does not need to invent one.**

**⚠️ Two flags.** The relocation programs — roughly **280,000 Tibetans** moved from villages and nomadic
herding into towns from 2006, following about **300,000 nomads resettled in Qinghai** first, under the
"Comfortable Housing" program — are reported with the state's framing and the exile critique side by side in
the source; this survey has not independently assessed either. And the caterpillar fungus *Yartsa Gunbu*
(*Cordyceps sinensis*) is reported as contributing about **40% of rural cash income** and **8.5% of TAR GDP**,
which is a startling figure for a wild-harvested fungus and should be re-checked before use.

### 2.5 Taiwan

**Four founding layers, in order, each of which left something.**

**1. Austronesian.** Humans have been on Taiwan for at least 30,000 years; a farming culture appears around
3000 BC, believed to mark the arrival of the ancestors of today's Indigenous peoples. They maintained regular
trade with Southeast Asia long before any Han settlement. Taiwan is, in the mainstream linguistic account,
the **Austronesian homeland** — the point of origin for a language family that reaches Madagascar and Easter
Island. ⚠️ That last claim is stated here from general knowledge and was not independently verified in this
session.

**2. Dutch and Spanish, 1624–1662.** The VOC built **Fort Zeelandia** on Tayowan off the southwest coast in
1624 after being driven from Penghu; Spain established Santísima Trinidad at modern Keelung in 1626 and was
expelled by the Dutch in 1642. The Dutch found about 1,500 Chinese visitors and residents on arrival. **The
decisive Dutch act was recruiting Han labor**: they brought Chinese to hunt deer for the skin trade, then to
plant sugarcane at Sakam from 1632, then rice. After 1648 nearly all company revenue came from the Chinese
population. **Han settlement of Taiwan began as a Dutch colonial labor policy.** By 1650 the Dutch estimated
about 50,000 Indigenous people in the western plains and administered 315 villages totaling about 68,600
people, which they reckoned at 40–50% of the island's Indigenous population.

**3. Koxinga and the Qing, 1662–1895.** **Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga)** defeated the Dutch in 1662; his
descendants were defeated by the Qing in **1683**. Over two centuries of Qing rule Taiwan's population **rose
by over two million and became majority Han**, through illegal cross-strait migration and encroachment on
Indigenous land, with Qing-administered territory eventually covering the whole western plain and the
northeast. Settler colonization was **actively encouraged** in the later period. Taiwan was governed under
**Fujian** until it became a separate province in **1885** — which is why Skinner files it inside the
Southeast Coast macroregion and why the island's Han language is **Hokkien**, i.e. coastal Southern Min.

**4. Japan, 1895–1945, and the 1949 influx.** Ceded to Japan after the First Sino-Japanese War, Taiwan became
an industrialized, rice- and sugar-exporting colony, and later a staging base for Japanese operations in
China, Southeast Asia and the Pacific. In **1945** the ROC took control; in **1949**, having lost the
mainland, the KMT withdrew to Taiwan and Chiang Kai-shek declared martial law, which ran for forty years
until the reforms of the 1980s; the first direct presidential election was **1996**.

**The population term that matters.** **Waishengren** (外省人) are those who arrived from the mainland
between the 1945 Japanese surrender and the 1949 KMT retreat, formally defined by an ancestral home outside
Taiwan passed through the father's line; **benshengren** are the Hoklo and Hakka who had lived under Japanese
rule. ⚠️ The commonly cited figure of roughly one to two million arrivals in 1949 was **not confirmed in the
sources retrieved** and should be checked.

**Indigenous numbers today.**

| | Figure |
|---|---|
| Nationally recognized Indigenous people | **600,303**, about **3%** of the population, in **16 recognized peoples** |
| Including unrecognized **Pingpu** (plains) peoples | **over 800,000** |
| Including people of mixed ancestry | possibly **over one million** |

Only **Kavalan, Sakizaya and Siraya** among the plains groups have won recognition. The obstacles are
documentary: petitions require genealogies, group histories and evidence of continued linguistic and cultural
identity, and colonial language policy destroyed much of the evidence. Indigenous people could not use
traditional names on identity cards until **1995**, when a 1946 ban was lifted, and the form still allows
only 15 characters.

**The assimilation history is unusually well studied and the modern consensus is the interesting one.** Three
narratives compete: that Han migration from Fujian and Guangdong pushed the plains peoples into the mountains
where they became the highland peoples; that intermarriage between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries
completely sinicized the plains peoples; and — the current ethnographic position — that there was a **mutual
cultural shift experienced by both Han and plains Indigenous, producing a hybrid**. The evidence cited for
the third: taking a Han surname was the entry ticket to Confucian personhood and its economic benefits, so
many did; immigrant Han formed sworn brotherhoods under a common surname with manufactured genealogies, and
plains Indigenous joined them for protection and acquired a Han lineage in the process. **The upshot is that
Taiwanese family books are largely unreliable as ancestry evidence, and that people who count as Han on
Taiwan demonstrate major cultural differences from Han elsewhere.**

**That is the strongest single argument for Taiwan as its own nation rather than a Min province**, and it is
an argument about founding population rather than about politics.

### 2.6 Korea

Unified in this setting, so the research question is what the unity rests on and how far the two halves have
diverged.

**The historical unity is the deepest in the region.** Korea has been a single polity for most of the last
thirteen centuries. ⚠️ The Silla unification of 668 and the Goryeo and Joseon successions are stated here
from general knowledge; the retrieved source begins its account at the Korean Empire and the Japanese
annexation of **1910**, and the earlier chronology was not independently verified in this session. What *is*
verified is that the two Koreas were one nation immediately before the Japanese annexation, and that the
division is a 1945 event with no prior basis.

**The division, which is the most arbitrary border in this survey and possibly anywhere.** At Cairo in
November 1943 Roosevelt, Churchill and Chiang declared that Korea should in due course become free and
independent; Roosevelt floated a trusteeship at Tehran and Yalta. The USSR declared war on Japan on **8
August 1945**. Soviet troops advanced fast, and on **10 August 1945** two young American officers — **Dean
Rusk and Charles Bonesteel** — were told to define an American occupation zone. **Working at extremely short
notice and completely unprepared, they used a National Geographic map and chose the 38th parallel**, because
it split the country roughly in half while leaving **Seoul** on the American side. **No expert on Korea was
consulted.** Neither man knew that Japan and pre-revolutionary Russia had discussed partitioning Korea along
the same parallel forty years earlier. Rusk later said that had he known Korean history he would almost
certainly have chosen a different line.

**The division placed 16 million Koreans in the American zone and 9 million in the Soviet zone.** To the
Americans' surprise the USSR accepted immediately, and the line went into **General Order No. 1**, approved
17 August 1945. It was understood as temporary, pending the five-power trusteeship agreed at the Moscow
Conference in December 1945. Negotiations failed; the question went to the UN; UN-supervised elections were
held **in the south only** in 1948; the **DPRK was established 10 July 1948** and the **ROK 15 August 1948**,
each claiming the whole peninsula. The war of **1950–53** ended in stalemate and the DMZ.

**Two design-relevant details.** The original DPRK constitution was **primarily written by Stalin and
Shtykov**, and in December 1946 Soviet generals designed the Provisional Committee's election results —
seat distribution by party, number of women, precise social composition — **without Korean input**. And ⚠️
the Yanbian Korean population in Manchuria (2.1, 4) is the third Korean community, which a unified Korea
would have an obvious interest in and no obvious claim to.

**Why unity is plausible in a post-collapse setting, stated as the research supports it.** The division has
no founding-population basis, no institutional basis before 1945, no linguistic basis and no ecological
basis. It is a line two officers drew on a magazine map in a few hours. Per the Latin America survey's test —
*an institution that shaped where people physically live survives the state; an institution that only
allocated authority does not* — the 38th parallel is pure authority allocation, and the seventy-five-year
divergence in political economy sits entirely on top of states that would be the first thing a collapse
removes. **Korea is the cleanest reunification case in the setting and the research supports the author's
existing decision without qualification.**

### 2.7 Japan, the Ainu and the Ryukyus

The archipelago is canon. What the research adds is that **Japan absorbed two distinct peoples at both ends
of itself within a single generation of the 1870s**, and that both retain identity.

**The Ainu, in Hokkaido.** A people indigenous to northern Japan and the surrounding Sea of Okhotsk region —
Hokkaido, historically northern Honshu, Sakhalin, the Kurils, Kamchatka — who call the territory **Ainu
Mosir**, the land of the Ainu. Their culture combines a Jōmon-period substrate, a layer acquired through long
interaction with Japanese speakers, and a strong northern element absorbed from the **Okhotsk** population.
The **Ainu language is a genuine isolate**, unrelated to Japanese.

**The absorption, dated.** The Matsumae domain held exclusive trading rights from the 1630s and later leased
them to merchants. From **1799 to 1806** the Tokugawa shogunate took direct control of southern Hokkaido;
Japan proclaimed sovereignty over Sakhalin in 1807. In this period Ainu women were separated from their
husbands and forcibly married or raped, and Ainu men were deported to merchant subcontractors on five- and
ten-year service terms. Combined with smallpox, this collapsed the population: **about 80,000 Ainu in the
eighteenth century; by 1868 about 15,000 in Hokkaido, 2,000 in Sakhalin and about 100 in the Kurils.** The
**1875 Treaty of St. Petersburg** traded Sakhalin to Russia for the Kurils and their Ainu inhabitants. The
**1899 Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act** labeled the Ainu "former aborigines," took their land,
granted automatic Japanese citizenship and thereby **denied them status as an Indigenous group**, banned
animal sacrifice and tattooing, and redistributed the land to Yamato settler farmers under a policy called
*colonization* (拓殖) at the time and later euphemized as *opening up undeveloped land* (開拓).

**Today.** Official Hokkaido surveys received **11,450 responses in 2023**; about **300** Ainu in Russia as
of 2021. **Unofficial 2002 estimates put the total in Japan at 200,000 or higher**, because assimilation was
near-total and many people of Ainu descent do not know their ancestry. The language: about **300 native
speakers in 1966**, fewer than 100 in the 1980s with only 15 using it daily, and **Hokkaido Ainu is likely
extinct today** with no known native speakers; Sakhalin and Kuril Ainu were declared extinct in the twentieth
century. Revitalization efforts are active. ⚠️ The 1997 Ainu Cultural Promotion Act and the 2019 law
recognizing the Ainu as an Indigenous people are named from general knowledge and were **not confirmed** in
the retrieved text.

**The Ryukyuans, in Okinawa.** A **Japonic-speaking** people — the Ryukyuan languages form a branch of the
Japonic family alongside Japanese, not a dialect of it — indigenous to the island chain running from Kyushu
toward Taiwan. Distinct culture with matriarchal elements, an indigenous religion, and a cuisine in which
rice arrived late, in the twelfth century. Northern (Amami, Okinawa) and Southern (Miyako, Yaeyama) Ryukyuans
are culturally and genetically differentiated from each other, the divergence being especially sharp between
Okinawa and Miyako; the two halves were culturally unified only in the tenth century.

**The state tradition.** Three Okinawan polities were unified by **Shō Hashi between 1416 and 1429** into the
**Ryukyu Kingdom (1429–1879)**, with its capital at **Shuri**, prospering on maritime trade and on tributary
relations with Ming China begun in **1372**. In **1609** the **Satsuma** domain invaded; the kingdom kept a
fictive independence in **dual subordination to both China and Japan**, which suited everyone because
Tokugawa Japan was barred from trading directly with China. **1871**, the kingdom is placed under Kagoshima
Prefecture. **1872–79**, the Ryukyu Domain. **1875**, the Ryukyuans are forced to end tributary relations
with China against their own preference for dual allegiance. **1879**, the domain is abolished, **Okinawa
Prefecture** is created, and the last king **Shō Tai** is exiled to Tokyo as a viscount. Ulysses S. Grant
proposed a sovereign Okinawa with the other islands divided between China and Japan; China declined to ratify
at the last minute and the proposal died. China renounced its claims in **1895**. Meiji policy then suppressed
Ryukyuan identity, tradition, culture and language.

**Today.** **Over 1.4 million Ryukyuans in Okinawa Prefecture alone**, plus the Amami Islands in Kagoshima
Prefecture and a substantial diaspora. **They are not a recognized minority in Japan** — the authorities
treat them as a subgroup of the Japanese people — which makes them, on the retrieved source's assessment,
**the largest unrecognized ethnolinguistic minority group in Japan.** The endonyms are **Uchinaanchu** and
**Shimanchu**, not "Ryukyuan," which is an exonym from the Chinese *Liuqiu*; and people on the other island
groups keep their own separate self-designations and do not generally call themselves Uchinaanchu. The
islands were under United States occupation **1945–1972**, and resentment of the continuing US military
presence and of Tokyo's handling of it is strong and current.

**Why this matters for the map.** Both Hokkaido and Okinawa are **prefectures** — first-order subdivisions.
Under this project's rule they can be drawn out of Japan **without splitting anything**. That makes them
available in a way that, for example, Kham is not. Whether to use that availability is the author's call and
is set out at 3.2.

**This is not a proposal for a set of nations and it is not a map.** It is a first-pass division offered so
the author has something concrete to argue with. Every entry ends with the case against it, so that rejecting

### 3.1 First: the Sinian Federation's internal sub-layer

**Author's decision, being executed here, not proposed:** the Han core is drawn as a federation of linguistic
nations, sitting inside the Federation the way states sit inside a union. **One Sinian government; Mandarin,
Wu, Yue, Min, Hakka, Xiang, Gan and Jin as divisions under it. These are not separate nations and are not
candidates for Part 3's region count.** They are what the post-2083 fragmentation fragmented *into* before
reunification, which is why they need to exist on the map at all. **This is the one place three tiers of
identity apply.**

**The three tiers, with the middle tier supplied by a named scholar rather than invented.**

- **Tier 1 — Han.** The major category.
- **Tier 2 — three zones**, following **Jerry Norman**, who used a carefully chosen set of phonological,
  lexical and grammatical features to sort the seven traditional dialect groups into **Northern (Mandarin)**,
  **Central (Wu, Gan, Xiang)** and **Southern (Hakka, Yue, Min)**. Independent support: a large-scale mutual
  intelligibility study found a **primary division between the northern groups (Mandarin and Jin) and all
  others, with Min as an identifiable branch** — i.e. the data agree with Norman on the first cut.
- **Tier 3 — the nations themselves.** **The eight federal members** — Mandarin, Wu, Yue, Min, Hakka, Xiang,
  Gan and Jin — plus **Huizhou** and **Ping**, which the map and the *Language Atlas of China* (1987) both
  distinguish on Li Rong's classification but which are too small to carry federal weight.

**⚠️ Correction to the brief, from the map.** The brief's original list named **seven** Sinitic groups. **The
map shows eight-plus, and the difference is load-bearing.** Four corrections, all carried from the
ethnolinguistic extraction:

- **Jin** is mapped as a distinct block over **Shanxi**, separate from Mandarin. Many classifications fold it
  in; this map does not, and neither does the *Language Atlas of China* (1987), which follows Li Rong.
- **Ping (Pinghua)** is mapped separately in **central Guangxi**, inside Zhuang territory — it descends from
  the speech of the earliest Chinese migrants to Guangxi, predating both the Yue and the Southwestern
  Mandarin arrivals.
- **Huizhou** is mapped as its own small unit in southern **Anhui**, adjoining Wu, and is genuinely hard to
  classify: it shares features with Wu, Gan and Mandarin, and earlier scholars assigned it to each in turn.
- **And Min is five languages, not one** — **Min-Bei, Min-Dong, Min-Zhong, Min-Nan and Pu-Xian** are mapped
  separately, with **Min-Nan reaching Taiwan and Hainan**. Min is the only Sinitic branch that cannot be
  derived directly from Middle Chinese and is the most internally divided group on the map; in the western
  Fujian mountains, adjacent villages can be mutually unintelligible.

**Recommendation on Min, carried from the map extraction and endorsed here: keep Min as one federal member.**
Splitting it five ways would give Fujian more representation inside the Federation than the entire north, and
the map's granularity exceeds what a nations layer can carry. This is precisely the tension Ribeiro's five
*Brasis* created for the Latin America file, and it resolves the same way — **the finer resolution is
recorded so it is available, and the coarser one is used for drawing.**

**The Federation is severely lopsided by construction, and that is the most useful thing about it.**
**Mandarin covers roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the Han area on the map** and has about 990 million
first-language speakers against the other seven combined at perhaps 400 million. A federation in which one
member is larger than every other member put together is not a balanced union and cannot be written as one.

**Say so rather than smoothing it.** A tidy eight-way balance would be a design that has no politics in it.
This one has permanent internal politics: a standing majority that does not need the others' consent, seven
members who can only matter in coalition, and a written standard and examination tradition (1.6) that were
built by and for the majority's language. Every federal argument the Sinian Federation can have — revenue
sharing, language of instruction, appointments, where the capital sits, whether the south's 83% economic lead
over the north (1.5) buys it anything — follows from the asymmetry rather than having to be invented. **The
lopsidedness is the Federation's story engine.**

**Three observations that make this a nations layer rather than a dialect map.**

1. **Wikipedia's own infoboxes assign an *ethnicity* to most of these groups** — Wu people, Cantonese people,
   Hunanese people, Gan people, Hakka people, Min. That is not this survey's editorializing; the
   identity-level reading is already the standard one.
2. **They are not mutually intelligible and the field knows it.** Chinese is usually treated as a **language
   family** within Sino-Tibetan, not a language. Estimates of the number of languages implied by mutual
   intelligibility run from dozens to hundreds. Intelligibility testing found significant unintelligibility
   *even within Mandarin*, with listeners in a few cases understanding fewer than 70% of words from speakers
   in their own province. In Fujian, neighboring counties — in the western mountains, neighboring villages —
   can be mutually unintelligible. **John DeFrancis** proposed *regionalect* and **Victor Mair** *topolect*
   as translations of 方言 precisely because *dialect* misleads.
3. **The thing holding them together is a writing system and a state, which is exactly the category Woodard
   predicts will not survive the state.** The varieties are popularly and officially treated as one language
   because speakers share a standard written form and a long history of political unity. Strip the state and
   the standard, and what remains is the family.

| **Upper Yangtze** (Sichuan, Chongqing) | Southwestern Mandarin | **Agree** |
| **Lower Yangtze** (Jiangsu, Anhui, Shanghai, N. Zhejiang) | **Wu + Jianghuai Mandarin + Huizhou** | **Disagree.** Three nations in one macroregion, and the Wu/Mandarin seam runs through the middle of Jiangsu and Anhui |
| **Middle Yangtze** (Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi) | **Xiang + Gan + Southwestern Mandarin** | **Disagree.** Three nations in one macroregion — though they correspond decently to Hunan, Jiangxi and Hubei respectively |
| **Southeast Coast** (Fujian, E. Guangdong, S. Zhejiang, **Taiwan**) | **Min**, in its five mapped varieties | **Agree, and this is the strongest agreement in the table.** Skinner's own subdivisions — the Ou-Ling basins, the Min basin, Zhang-Quan, the Han basin and Taiwan — track the Min sub-varieties closely enough that the two schemes are describing one object |
| **Lingnan** (Guangdong, Guangxi) | **Yue + Hakka + Ping + Zhuang** | **Partial, and the most crowded cell in the table.** Yue is the prestige nation; the macroregion also holds dispersed Hakka, Ping in central Guangxi, and the 19.6-million Zhuang block, which is not Sinitic at all. This single cell is where 3.3's whole problem lives |
| **Yungui** (Yunnan, Guizhou) | Southwestern Mandarin, over a non-Han substrate | **Disagree in the way that matters most.** Mandarin-speaking, not Han-founded. See 3.3 |

**And the finding that falls out of it, which is the best structural observation in this survey.**

**Hakka has no macroregion. It cannot be drawn under the whole-subdivision rule at all.** Forty-four million
people, a distinct self-identified ethnicity with its own prestige variety (Meixian), occupying the **hills
where Guangdong, Fujian and Jiangxi meet** — the interstices of three provinces and three macroregions, in
terrain nobody's lowland core wanted. The name means "guest families." **The map confirms it directly:
Hakka appears as scattered interior pockets, not as a contiguous territory.**

That is **Woodard's Greater Appalachia, exactly**: a mobile, hill-dwelling, historically marginal population
occupying the seams between other people's territories, defined by not being any of them, and unmappable
onto administrative units because administrative units were drawn around the lowlands it sits between. The
METHOD file's rule 6 — *seams get marked, not resolved* — was written for precisely this situation, and the
DMZ on the North American map is its precedent.

**Two ways to handle it, and the author should pick knowingly rather than by default.** Either **Hakka is a
non-territorial member of the Federation** — a nation with representation and no province, which is genuinely
unusual and, so far as this survey found, unused in comparable fiction — or **it is absorbed into its
neighbors (Yue, Min, Gan) and the loss is recorded.** The survey's own preference is the first, on the
grounds that a federation containing one member defined by having no ground is a better object than one that
quietly deletes forty-four million people; but it is a real choice. Either way the Hakka hills are also, not
incidentally, the obvious location for a separatist movement, a bandit country, or a partisan war.

**Two further notes for the sub-layer.** The **Qinling–Huaihe line** (1.5) is the Federation's real internal
fault, with a 5.5-year life-expectancy gap and measurable psychological difference across it, and it has been
a political border twice — Northern versus Southern Dynasties, Southern Song versus Jin. And the economics
have inverted: the gross regional product of provinces below the line **equaled** those above it in **1960**
and was **83% larger by 2019**. **The Federation's north/south seam is live, measurable, historically
precedented as a border, and currently running in the south's favor for the first time since the Ming.**

### 3.2 The regions

**Nine firm entries plus three contingent ones. Labeled FOR CONSIDERATION ONLY.**

---

#### 1. The Sinian Federation *(canon — not proposed)*

**Founding character.** Han, in the tiered sense of 1.2 and 3.1: one major category resolving through three
Norman zones into ten linguistic nations. Continuous civilization from the North China Plain outward, with no
founding-settler moment, which is why the internal structure is linguistic rather than chronological.

**Characteristic institution.** The examination-recruited bureaucracy and the shared written standard — the
two things that made a territory of mutually unintelligible languages administrable as one polity. Beneath
them, Skinner's periodic market hierarchy, which set the real social boundaries.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the North China Plain and the Lower Yangtze. Domain: the eighteen
provinces and their modern successors. Sphere: the Sinosphere as described at 1.6 — a tributary-shaped zone
of acknowledged centrality without administration, reaching Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

**Durably distinct because** it is the oldest continuously administered political tradition on earth and the
only entity in this survey whose existence requires no argument.

**The case against — internal, not external.** That "the Han core" is one thing at all. Ten mutually
unintelligible languages, a measured north/south discontinuity worth 5.5 years of life expectancy, an
83% economic gap across it, and two historical precedents for that exact line being an international border.
The Federation is a real answer to this, but it is an answer, not a given: the word "Federation" is carrying
the weight, and 3.1 is what it is carrying.

---

#### 2. Korea *(canon — not proposed)*

**Founding character.** Koreanic; the deepest single-people/single-territory correspondence in the region.

**Characteristic institution.** A Sinosphere member that took the examination system and Confucian
bureaucracy directly and built **hangul** anyway — adoption without absorption.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the peninsula. Domain: the peninsula. Sphere: the Yanbian Korean population
in Manchuria, and the diaspora.

**Durably distinct because** the 1945 division has no founding-population, institutional, linguistic or
ecological basis whatever. It was two officers, a National Geographic map and a few hours (2.6).

**The case against.** Seventy-five years of divergence in political economy, education, nutrition, and in
practice language, is not nothing, and a unified Korea inherits a reintegration problem rather than a solved
one. But per the Latin America survey's test, that divergence is entirely state-made and sits on top of
states a collapse removes first.

---

#### 3. Japan *(canon — not proposed)*

**Founding character.** Yamato Japonic, with **two absorbed peoples at the extremities** — Ainu in the north
(a language isolate, absorbed 1799–1899) and Ryukyuan in the south (a Japonic branch with its own
four-and-a-half-century kingdom, absorbed 1609–1879).

**Characteristic institution.** ⚠️ Not researched in depth by this pass, which treated Japan as canon. The
obvious candidates — the *ie* household, the domain system, the Meiji prefectural reorganization — are named
from general knowledge and are **not verified here**.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Honshu. Domain: the four main islands. Sphere: Hokkaido and the Ryukyus,
which is precisely the point at issue.

**Durably distinct because** Huntington, in the most-cited civilizational taxonomy there is, places Japan
**outside** the Sinic world as a civilization of its own — the only such carve-out in his scheme.

**The case against.** Not against Japan; against Japan's edges. See entries 11 and 12.

---

#### 4. Manchuria / the Northeast

**Founding population.** Han, from **Shandong and Zhili/Hebei**, arriving by land through the Shanhai Pass
and by sea on the Yantai–Lüshun ferry, in a documented net migration of **8.7 million between 1891 and
1942**, over a Tungusic substrate the Qing had been holding in reserve since 1668. **This is the survey's
only literal Doctrine of First Effective Settlement case.**

**Characteristic institution.** First the **Willow Palisade** and the banner-only closure; then, after 1860,
state-sponsored settlement as a **counter-annexation measure against Russia**; then Japanese-built heavy
industry under Manchukuo, inherited wholesale by the PRC as the planned economy's flagship.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang. Domain: the five eastern Inner Mongolian
prefectures already formally assigned to the Northeast under the 2003 plan — Hulunbuir, Hinggan, Tongliao,
Chifeng, Xilin Gol. Sphere: the Russian Far East (out of scope) and the Korean border.

**Durably distinct because** it has everything a successor state needs already assembled: **107.4 million
people**, the largest arable plain in China and over 20% of national staple food, steel, automobiles,
shipbuilding, aircraft, oil at Daqing, a coherent regional identity with its own cultural moment, and a
hundred years of thinking of itself as the country's most important place followed by twenty-five of being
told it is not. **The grievance is real, documented and currently worsening** — lowest-but-one provincial GDP
per head, fastest population decline in China.

**The case against — and it is the strongest case against in this list.** It is Han, and has been
Han-majority since about 1900. **Skinner puts it inside his scheme as macroregion 10.** Its language is
Northeastern Mandarin, mutually intelligible across all three provinces and with Beijing. By the author's own
tiered-identity criterion it is not a different people; it is the same people in a different place with a
different economic history. **The merge candidate is the Sinian Federation, and the merge is defensible.**
The counter-argument is the Doctrine itself: the Northeast's founding institutions — closure, then
counter-Russian settlement, then Japanese industrialization, then Soviet-model heavy industry — are not the
Federation's, and Woodard's whole method says founding institutions outlast the population's origin.

---

#### 5. Mongolia

**Founding population.** Mongolic, pastoral nomadic, on the steppe's own side of the Hu Line and the 400 mm
isohyet.

**Characteristic institution.** The banner system under Qing administration, which fixed territorial
boundaries and forbade movement between banners; and, beneath that, the older institution Lattimore
describes — a mobile pastoral economy whose taxable asset walks away, which is why no settled empire ever
absorbed it and why the Wall stops where it does.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Mongolian state. Domain: itself. Sphere: Inner Mongolia's eastern
prefectures, Buryatia (out of scope) and the Oirat populations of Xinjiang.

**Durably distinct because** it is already a sovereign state with a century of separate institutional
history, UN membership since 1961, and a territory nobody else can profitably use.

**The case against.** None as a polity — it exists. The real question is entry 6.

---

#### 6. Inner Mongolia

**Founding population.** Contested by construction. **4.1 million Mongols — the largest Mongol population in
the world, larger than Mongolia's own — inside a region that was 83.6% Han at its founding in 1947 and is
78.7% Han today.**

**Characteristic institution.** The autonomous-region compact itself: a legally guaranteed Mongol chairman, a
Mongol share of the regional congress **fixed at 37–39% since 1954** against a 17.7% population share, and a
Han party secretary from elsewhere who outranks the chairman. **A formal minority-overrepresentation
arrangement inside a Han-majority territory is a genuinely distinctive institution and there is nothing else
like it in the survey.**

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the eastern prefectures — **Hinggan League, Tongliao, Chifeng** — where
**over 70% of Inner Mongolia's Mongols live on under 18% of its territory**. Domain: the rest of the
autonomous region. Sphere: Mongolia, and the Mongol populations of Xinjiang and Qinghai.

**Durably distinct because** identity is outliving language. Mongolian is down to roughly 2.1 million
speakers against 4.1 million ethnic Mongols, use has been declining since 1995, Mandarin has been the sole
official language since 2021 — and people who have entirely lost the language, like the Tumets, remain
registered and self-identifying Mongols, as do the children of the roughly 40% of Mongol-involving marriages
that are mixed. That is durable ethnic identity with the cultural content stripped out, which is precisely
Woodard's claim about how nations persist.

**The case against — two of them, pulling opposite ways.** (a) **Merge into Mongolia.** Pan-Mongolism is a
real defeated project — Choibalsan wanted it and the 1950 Sino-Soviet Treaty killed it; Mao wanted the
reverse and was rebuffed in 1949 and 1954. The case against the merge is that Inner Mongolia is
four-fifths Han and its Mongols are a different, sedentarized, intermarried, Chinese-speaking population.
(b) **Merge into Manchuria (#4).** The Mongol core prefectures are already administratively assigned to the
Northeast, and the eastern division of Inner Mongolia is conventionally treated as part of Dongbei. This is
the quieter option and may be the more realistic one. **The survey does not choose.**

---

#### 7. East Turkestan

**Founding population.** Turco-Persianate, over an Indo-European and Tibeto-Burman substrate: Tocharian at
Turfan and Kucha, Saka at Shule and Khotan, then Turkic and Islamic layers. **Founding character is not Sinic
and never was.**

**Characteristic institution.** The **oasis city-state** — Kashgar, Khotan, Yarkand, Aksu, Turfan — a chain
of irrigated market towns on the caravan routes, each a small polity dependent on meltwater, with only 9.7%
of the land habitable. Layered over it, the **XPCC**: 3.6 million people, 14% of the population, 19.7% of the
economy, created in 1954 as a settler-garrison corporation. Two founding institutions, seven centuries and
one civilization apart, both still operating.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the southwestern Tarim — **Kashgar, Khotan, Kizilsu, Aksu**, holding about
80% of Xinjiang's Uyghurs at about 90% of local population, plus Turpan. Domain: the Tarim rim entire.
Sphere: Dzungaria, which is Han- and Kazakh-majority and would be contested ground.

**Durably distinct because** the founding population never assimilated and the intermarriage evidence is
explicitly the opposite of Inner Mongolia's. The name, the territory and the precedent all exist: the Second
East Turkestan Republic held Ili from 1944 to 1949.

**The case against.** **Xinjiang is one province and contains two countries.** Dzungaria is 55% of the Han
population, urban, industrial, and demographically restored to roughly its early-Qing balance; the Tarim is
95% non-Han and half as rich. The whole-subdivision rule forbids separating them, so any East Turkestan drawn
here **inherits a Han-plurality north it cannot govern and did not found** — a 44.96%/42.24% region, which is
not a majority for anybody. The alternative readings are: leave Xinjiang whole and contested; or, if the
author ever relaxes the rule, split at the Tian Shan, which is the real border and which Lattimore's method
would draw without hesitating.

---

#### 8. Tibet

**Founding population.** Tibetic. A distinct civilization with its own script, its own branch of Buddhism and
a state tradition running from Ü-Tsang's dynasties through the **Ganden Phodrang (1642–1951)**, outside the
Qing provincial system when the dynasty fell and functionally independent from 1912 to 1951.

**Characteristic institution.** Monastic-aristocratic government — a theocracy in the literal sense, in which
the Lama church held land, revenue and political authority, and the emperor of China's relationship to Tibet
ran through his role as **wheel-turning king** rather than through a governor.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the **Tibet Autonomous Region** — 1.2 million km², 3.6 million people,
86.0% Tibetan, 3 per km². Domain: **Qinghai**, if the author takes it, which is Amdo. Sphere: the Kham
portions of Sichuan, Yunnan and Gansu, which **cannot be drawn** without splitting provinces — so the sphere
is real and permanently unattainable, which is a good problem for fiction.

**Durably distinct because** of the numbers and the water. **86% Tibetan in the TAR** with the lowest
intermarriage rates in China; and the **Tibetan Plateau holds the headwaters of the Yellow, Yangtze, Mekong,
Salween, Brahmaputra and Indus**, is the **Third Pole** with the largest freshwater reserve outside the polar
ice, and is warming four times faster than the rest of China with 80% of its glaciers already in retreat.
**Whoever holds Tibet holds Asia's water, and the water is running out.** No other region in this survey has
a comparable strategic asset.

**The case against.** **The TAR is about half of cultural Tibet, and roughly half of all Tibetans — 7.06
million nationally against about 3.1 million in the TAR — live outside it.** A TAR-only Tibet is a rump that
its own people would not recognize as Tibet. Adding Qinghai fixes some of that and creates a different
problem, since Qinghai's demography is not the TAR's. ⚠️ Qinghai's 2020 ethnic composition was **not
retrieved in this session** and should be obtained before the author decides. The merge candidate, if Tibet
is rejected, is absorption into the Sinian Federation as the frontier the Qing never provincialized — which
is historically wrong but administratively what happened.

---

#### 9. Taiwan

**Founding population.** Four layers (2.5): Austronesian, then Dutch-recruited Han labor from Fujian from the
1620s, then two centuries of Qing-era Hoklo and Hakka settlement that added over two million people and
flipped the island to Han majority, then a Japanese colonial state from 1895, then the 1945–49 *waishengren*.

**Characteristic institution.** ⚠️ The most distinctive one is the **sworn brotherhood** — immigrant Han
uniting under a common surname with a manufactured genealogy, functioning as the kinship organization the
mainland provided and the frontier did not, and serving as the mechanism by which plains Indigenous people
acquired Han lineages. The consequence is documented: **Taiwanese family books are largely unreliable as
ancestry evidence.** Later: the Japanese colonial developmental state, and forty years of KMT martial law.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the western plain. Domain: the island. Sphere: Penghu, Kinmen, Matsu, and
the Hokkien-speaking diaspora of Southeast Asia (out of scope).

**Durably distinct because** the current ethnographic consensus is not that Han replaced Indigenous or that
Indigenous became Han, but that **both shifted into a hybrid**, and the explicit conclusion drawn from it is
that people who count as Han on Taiwan **demonstrate major cultural differences from Han elsewhere.** That is
a founding-population argument for separateness, independent of any twentieth-century politics.

**The case against — and it is sharper than expected.** **Skinner puts Taiwan inside the Southeast Coast
macroregion**, as one of its five subdivisions alongside the Min basin and Zhang-Quan. Economically and
linguistically Taiwan is the Min nation's island wing; Taiwanese *is* coastal Southern Min. If the Sinian
Federation is a federation of linguistic nations (3.1), the tidiest reading of Taiwan is **a province of the
Min nation inside that federation** — which is, awkwardly, also Beijing's reading. The counter is the
hybridity finding above, fifty years of Japanese institutions, and the plain fact that in polls most people
in Taiwan identify as Taiwanese only.

---

#### 10. Yungui **— CONTINGENT ON 3.3**

**Founding population.** Non-Han highland peoples across six language families, with a Southwestern Mandarin
administrative overlay applied late. Yi (9.83M), Miao (11.07M), Bouyei (3.58M), Dong (3.50M), Hani (1.73M),
Bai (2.09M), Dai (1.33M), Lisu, Wa, Naxi, Gelao, Lahu, Sui.

**Characteristic institution.** **State evasion itself** (1.7): dispersal, swidden, orality, segmentary
organization, mobility — read by Scott as deliberate adaptations by populations fleeing valley state-making.
Against it, the Chinese *tusi* chieftaincy system. ⚠️ *Tusi* is named from general knowledge and was not
confirmed in the retrieved sources.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: **Yunnan and Guizhou**, i.e. Skinner's ninth macroregion. Domain: western
Sichuan's highlands (undrawable — Sichuan cannot be split). Sphere: the Southeast Asian Massif, out of scope.

**Durably distinct because** it is **East Asia's Far West case** — a region whose character was set by
terrain's hostility to state-building rather than by any settler group's values — and the Part Four checklist
calls for at least one. The massif has never been politically unified as an empire, as feuding kingdoms, or
even as a zone of harmonized political systems, which is a durable negative fact about it.

**The case against.** **Yunnan and Guizhou were both inside the Qing Eighteen Provinces**, and their
administrative language is Southwestern Mandarin. By the author's stated constraint they are inside the
Sinian Federation. Drawing them out requires the decision at 3.3. ⚠️ Also: Scott's flight-from-the-state
reading is contested, and the massif's fragmentation is severe enough that some scholars doubt it coheres as
a unit at all.

---

#### 11. Hokkaido / Ainu Mosir **— CONTINGENT, author's call**

**Founding population.** Ainu, a language isolate, with a Jōmon substrate, a Japanese-contact layer and a
strong northern Okhotsk element — displaced by Yamato settler colonization from 1799 and formally
dispossessed by the 1899 Act.

**Characteristic institution.** The **Matsumae trade monopoly**, then explicit settler colonization (拓殖) —
Ainu land redistributed to Yamato farmers on a Western industrial-agriculture model, with flour mills,
breweries, mines, roads and railways built over it through 1904. **Hokkaido is Japan's Manchuria**: a
frontier closed, then opened, then settled by a state worried about Russia.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Hokkaido, especially the southeastern Hidaka region and towns like
Nibutani. Domain: the prefecture. Sphere: Sakhalin and the Kurils — **deferred with Russia, and left
unclaimed**; the Ainu populations there are in any case effectively gone, about 300 people in Russia as of
2021.

**Durably distinct because** the founding population is unrelated to the Yamato by language, and the
documented drop from about 80,000 in the eighteenth century to about 15,000 by 1868 was a colonial event with
a date, not a fade.

**The case against — and it is heavy.** **Official surveys got 11,450 responses in 2023.** Hokkaido Ainu is
likely extinct with no known native speakers. Assimilation was near-total, intermarriage was actively sought
by Ainu families to spare their children discrimination, and the honest estimate range — 11,450 counted
against an unofficial 200,000 who may not know their ancestry — tells you the identity is largely latent.
**An Ainu successor polity would be a 5.2-million-person Japanese prefecture with an Indigenous name.** The
merge candidate is Japan, and the merge is the realistic answer. A revival scenario is available but has to
be written as a revival, not assumed.

---

#### 12. Ryukyu / Okinawa **— CONTINGENT, author's call**

**Founding population.** Ryukyuan — a **branch of the Japonic family**, not a Japanese dialect — itself split
between a Northern (Amami, Okinawa) and a Southern (Miyako, Yaeyama) population that were culturally and
genetically differentiated and only unified in the tenth century.

**Characteristic institution.** **Dual subordination.** From 1609 to 1879 the kingdom was simultaneously a
Satsuma dependency and a Ming/Qing tributary, and the arrangement suited all three parties because Tokugawa
Japan was barred from direct trade with China. **A polity whose characteristic institution is being formally
subordinate to two powers at once, and profiting from it, is an unusual and very usable design object**, and
it is the tributary system (1.6) working exactly as designed.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Okinawa Prefecture, **over 1.4 million people**. Domain: plus the Amami
Islands — which are in **Kagoshima Prefecture**, so under the whole-subdivision rule they are **not
available**. Sphere: the Ryukyuan diaspora.

**Durably distinct because** a 450-year kingdom with its own capital at Shuri, its own maritime trade empire,
its own religion with matriarchal elements and its own language branch was annexed only in **1879**, within
living memory of the present republic's founding; because Ryukyuans are **not recognized as a minority by
Japan** and are on the retrieved assessment the **largest unrecognized ethnolinguistic minority in the
country**; and because the resentment is live — over the US bases and over Tokyo's handling of them — and
publicly expressed, as when Taro Aso's 2020 claim that Japan had lasted 2,000 years with one language and one
ethnic group drew strong criticism from the Ryukyuan community.

**The case against.** Ryukyuan is Japonic; the population is genetically closest to mainland Japanese
(roughly 80% admixture); the Amami portion of the domain is administratively unreachable; and 1.4 million
people on small islands is a small polity by this survey's standards. The merge candidate is Japan, which is
also the status quo. **But it is the best-founded of the three contingent entries**, because unlike Yungui it
does not require reopening the Federation's border, and unlike Hokkaido its people and language are still
here.

---

### 3.3 The southern-border problem — RESEARCHED, COSTED, AND MARKED

**Per the author's explicit instruction, this section sets out the options and what each costs, and does not
decide.**

**The problem.** "The Sinian Federation is the Han core, roughly the Eighteen Provinces inside the Wall" is
clean in the north, where the Wall, the 400 mm isohyet, the Hu Line and Lattimore's frontier all agree. **It
is not clean in the south, because the Qing Eighteen already contained the largest non-Han populations in
China proper.**

The retrieved source states the collision directly: **present-day Han regions do not correspond well to the
Qing Eighteen Provinces.** Much of the southwest — Yunnan, Guangxi, Guizhou — was inside successive Han
dynasties and inside the Eighteen, and **was and still is populated by non-Han groups**: Zhuang, Miao,
Bouyei and others. Conversely, Han are now the majority in most of Manchuria, much of Inner Mongolia, many
areas of Xinjiang and parts of Tibet — all *outside* the Eighteen. **The historical definition and the
ethnic definition point in opposite directions in both hemispheres of the problem.**

**The populations at stake, from the 2020 census.**

|---|---|---|---|
| **Zhuang** | **19,568,546** — China's largest minority | **Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region** (~14.5M there), plus Yunnan, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hunan | **Yes.** Guangxi was one of the eighteen |
| **Miao** | 11,067,929 | Guizhou, Hunan, Yunnan, Sichuan, Guangxi | **Yes** |

**That is roughly 72 million non-Han people inside the proposed Federation's borders**, of whom about 19.6
million are a single Tai-speaking nation with its own first-order autonomous region.

**A second, separate problem: the Qing eighteen are not a drawable modern boundary.** Per 1.8, seven modern
first-order subdivisions did not exist in the Qing scheme — Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Chongqing, Hainan,
Ningxia — and **Qinghai was never one of the eighteen at all**, being the Kokonor frontier domain. Since
whole first-order subdivisions is absolute in this project, the author is not choosing between two maps; the
author is choosing a rule for translating a 1750s map onto a 2020s one.

**Evidence pulling one way — the ethnolinguistic case for EXCLUDING Yunnan, Guizhou and Guangxi.** The
author's own language map, read in the companion extraction, shows those three provinces as a **shatter zone
of non-Han peoples, not Han territory with minorities in it.** The map labels, in that area alone: **Zhuang,
Bouyei, Dong, Miao, Hmong, Nuosu, Bai, Naxi, Lisu, Lipo, Nasu, Lolopo, Axi, Nisu, Lahu, Hani, Honi, Biyo, Lu,
Bugan, Drung, Tai-Nua, Riang, Parauk, Vo-Wa** — plus **Hlai** on Hainan. That is roughly twenty-five distinct
peoples across three provinces. And decisively: **Zhuang is drawn as a large solid block in Guangxi,
comparable in map area to Wu or Gan.** It is a territory, not an enclave, and it is the same map-area order of
magnitude as two of the Federation's own federal members.

**Evidence pulling the other way — the material case for INCLUDING them.** From the material companion's
§2.6, which costed this out independently:

| Province | Population (mid-2022) | What the Federation loses by excluding it |
|---|---|---|
| **Gansu** | 24.92M (32.20M with Ningxia) | **Over 90% of China's nickel reserves** — and therefore all stainless steel. Also antimony, chromium, cobalt, platinum, tungsten. Plus **the Hexi Corridor**, the only overland route from the core to Xinjiang |

**Total population of the four: 160.9 million — more than Japan, more than any European country.**

**The trade, stated once and left standing:** *a Federation drawn on strict Qing lines is materially complete
and ethnically incoherent; a Federation drawn on Han-majority lines is ethnically clean and cannot make
stainless steel, bronze or fertilizer from its own ground.* Note the partial escape the material file
identifies: **the four are separable.** Gansu has the weakest ethnolinguistic case for exclusion and the
strongest material case for inclusion. Guangxi is the hardest to keep and the cheapest to lose, because its
tin and manganese overlap with Yunnan's. Yunnan and Guizhou can be argued either way.

**The three options, with costs.**

**Option A — Strict Qing Eighteen.** The Federation is the eighteen and their modern successors: Hebei,
Shandong, Shanxi, Henan, Shaanxi, Gansu, Jiangsu, Anhui, Jiangxi, Fujian, Zhejiang, Hubei, Hunan, Sichuan,
Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Guizhou, plus Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Chongqing, Hainan and Ningxia.
Qinghai excluded.
- **Costs:** the Federation contains ~72 million non-Han including the Zhuang entire; **Yungui (entry 10) is
  not available**, so the survey's only Far West case disappears and the Part Four checklist item goes unmet;
  and the Federation acquires two first-order *autonomous regions* named for minorities it has absorbed
  (Guangxi Zhuang, Ningxia Hui), which is a standing internal contradiction.
- **Gains:** historically precise; needs the fewest arbitrary calls; produces the cleanest single border;
  and — genuinely useful — gives the Federation real internal minority politics rather than a monoculture.

**Option B — The Han-majority line.** The Federation is those first-order subdivisions with a Han majority,
regardless of Qing status.
- **Costs:** this **adds Manchuria, most of Inner Mongolia and arguably parts of Xinjiang back in**, because
  Han are the majority there now. It is the option that most directly contradicts the author's settled
  decision that the Federation stops at the Wall, and it makes the map a snapshot of twentieth-century
  migration policy rather than of founding character — which is the exact error Woodard's method exists to
  prevent.
- **Gains:** demographically honest about the present; easiest to defend to a reader who looks at a census.

**Option C — The eighteen minus the autonomous regions.** The Federation is Option A, less **Guangxi** and
**Ningxia**, on the grounds that the PRC itself designated those as minority homelands at first-order level.
Yunnan and Guizhou are ordinary provinces and stay in; or, in a stronger variant, Yungui also leaves.
- **Costs:** it uses a *PRC administrative* criterion inside a scheme otherwise built on Qing and
  ethnographic ones, which is a category mix; Guangxi is only about 32% Zhuang, so the region is not a Zhuang
  nation-state in any straightforward sense; and it strands Ningxia, which holds only 20% of the Hui — the
  other 80% are dispersed across every province and cannot be territorialized at all.
- **Gains:** it is the only option that gives the Zhuang, at 19.6 million, a polity; it makes Yungui
  available in the stronger variant; and it matches the map the PRC itself drew, which is a defensible
  evidentiary basis in the same way the county ancestry map was.

**The Hui are the case none of the three options solves, and that should be said plainly.** 11.4 million
people, **no non-Sinitic language**, dispersed across every province, concentrated nowhere except Ningxia
where they are a fifth of their own nation. Lattimore already identified the shape of the problem when he
described a **Muslim pale of Gansu and Ningxia** as the inner frontier zone of the Chinese Turkestan sector.
**The Hui are a nation without a territory, inside the Han core, defined by religion.** Under the
whole-subdivision rule they are undrawable. Per METHOD rule 6 — *seams get marked, not resolved* — the
recommendation is to mark the **Gansu Corridor**, which is Skinner's own sub-macroregion and the historical
Hexi route to Central Asia, as a corridor rather than to assign it.

**MARKED FOR THE AUTHOR. Not decided by this survey.**

### 3.4 Deliberately excluded, per Woodard's own scoping practice

Named rather than silently dropped, following the Part Four checklist.

- **Hong Kong and Macau.** Yue-speaking, with a century and a half of separate colonial institutions and
  their own legal systems, and — as the retrieved source notes — **outside the mainland ethnic-classification
  system entirely.** Under the whole-subdivision rule they are first-order units and therefore drawable.
  Folded into the Yue nation inside the Federation here on grounds of size alone. **Flagged as a real
  omission**, and the closest East Asian analogue to Woodard's New Netherland — a dense, commercially
  complex, cosmopolitan port city whose governance needs differ from everyone else's.
- **Hainan.** Carved from Guangdong; holds 1.6 million **Li (Hlai)**, Tai-Kadai speakers with a separate
  history from the Zhuang, plus the several thousand Austronesian-speaking **Utsuls** classified as Hui.
  Folded into the Federation. Arguably deserves its own entry.
- **The Gansu Corridor** — see 3.3.
- **Qinghai / Amdo** as its own region rather than as an appendage of Tibet. ⚠️ Its demography was not
  retrieved and the call cannot be made responsibly here.
- **The primate cities as their own nations.** Shanghai, Beijing, Chongqing and Tianjin are first-order
  municipalities holding populations larger than several entries above, and Woodard's New Netherland
  precedent says a single dense commercial city can be a nation. Not pursued. The Latin America survey
  flagged the same omission for Mexico City and São Paulo, so this is now a **consistent gap across two
  continents** and probably wants a decision at the project level rather than per-survey.
- **The Tarim/Dzungaria split** — see entry 7. Excluded only because the whole-subdivision rule forbids it.
- **Kham's Sichuan and Yunnan portions** — excluded for the same reason, and noted because roughly half of
  all Tibetans live outside the TAR.
- **Vietnam**, the fourth Sinosphere member, and the **Zhuang/Nùng** continuity across the Sino-Vietnamese
  border — the same Tai people recognized under two names in two states, together numbering over 15 million.
  Out of scope, but it means the Federation's southern frontier is a *divided nation's* frontier, which is
  worth knowing before drawing it.
- **The Russian Far East, the Amur basin and Sakhalin** — deferred entirely; see 3.5.

### 3.5 The Russian seams — deferred, and marked unclaimed

**By instruction, Russia is not researched and Siberian ground is left blank.** The Latin America survey
checked its Falklands corridor at the end of Part 3 rather than leaving the adjacency implicit; this is the
equivalent pass. Three seams touch this survey's ground, and in each case the East Asian side is described
above and **the ground beyond it is unclaimed on the map.**

1. **Outer Manchuria** — north of the Amur, east of the Ussuri. Lost by the Qing under the **Treaty of Aigun
   (1858)** and the **Convention of Peking (1860)**, and that loss is the direct cause of the policy reversal
   that produced Chuang Guandong (2.1). **It is therefore the one Russian seam this survey cannot avoid
   discussing**, because the Manchurian region's founding population exists *because of* it. Entry 4's
   northern and eastern border is the modern Heilongjiang and Jilin provincial line, and everything past it
   is blank.
2. **Sakhalin and the Kurils** — traded between Japan and Russia by the **Treaty of St. Petersburg (1875)**,
   which handed Sakhalin to Russia and the Kurils, with their Ainu inhabitants, to Japan. The Ainu range ran
   across both. Entry 11's sphere stops at the Hokkaido prefectural boundary.
3. **Buryatia and Tuva**, on Mongolia's northern flank. Mongolia's independence was a Soviet project from
   1921 and its guarantor until 1990, and Buryat intellectuals — Tsyben Zhamtsarano, Erdene Batkhaan — were
   central to building the Mongolian People's Republic's institutions. **Entries 5 and 6 are therefore
   incomplete in a way the author should know about**: the Mongolic world extends north across a border this
   survey is not allowed to look past, and any pan-Mongol project in the setting has a northern dimension
   that is currently undefined.

**Recommendation, offered and not pressed:** handle these the way the North American map handled the DMZ —
mark the ground rather than assign it. All three seams sit where a deferred power's territory meets a
described one, which is the definition of incoherent ground under METHOD rule 6.

## Part 4 — Indigenous and non-Han successor candidates

Following the Latin America survey's Part 5.2, which treated this as **the frame rather than a subsection**.
The finding there was that Woodard's framework, ported unadjusted, systematically under-weights Indigenous
continuity, because ten of his eleven nations are settler cultures and First Nation is the exception.

**East Asia sits between the two cases, and the difference is worth stating precisely.** Latin America's
non-settler populations are *substrates* — conquered in place, taxed, layered over, and still half the
population of Spanish America as late as 1935. East Asia's are **frontier peoples**: not conquered substrates
beneath a colonial society, but neighboring civilizations on the far side of an ecological boundary, absorbed
late, by a state that had spent two thousand years explicitly *not* absorbing them because absorption did not
pay (1.3). That produces a different kind of claim — **weaker demographically and much stronger
territorially**, because the Qing kept these places administratively separate and the PRC inherited the
separation as the autonomous-region system.

### 4.2 Reading the table

**Six findings, in order of how much they should change a design decision.**

1. **The Uyghurs have the strongest claim in East Asia, and it is not close.** 11.77 million and growing at
   63% since 1990 — the fastest growth of any large minority. A first-order autonomous region named for them.
   Four contiguous prefectures where they are about 90% of the population, holding 80% of their own nation. A
   distinct language family, a distinct religion, a distinct script history, a founding civilization that was
   never Sinic, and — decisively — a **twentieth-century precedent of independent statehood** in the Second
   East Turkestan Republic of 1944–49, with a name, a flag and a territory. Low intermarriage, explicitly
   contrasted with the Mongol case. **In Latin America the comparable case was the Aymara Altiplano. This is
   stronger, because the territory is first-order and the precedent is within living memory.**
2. **The Tibetans have the strongest *strategic* claim and a demographically awkward one.** 7.06 million,
   growing 54% since 1990, 86% of the TAR, the lowest intermarriage rates in China, a state tradition running
   to 1951 and a government-in-exile still operating — **and about half of them live in provinces that cannot
   be drawn.** Offset entirely by the water: headwaters of six major river systems, the Third Pole, the
   largest freshwater reserve outside the poles. **A Tibet that controls the water does not need to be
   populous to matter.**
3. **The Manchus are the negative case, and they are the most instructive entry in the table.** 10.42
   million — the fifth-largest minority, more people than there are Tibetans — and **essentially zero
   claim**: no first-order territory, fewer than 100 fluent speakers of a language that is functionally
   extinct, a flat-to-declining population while every comparable group grows, and a homeland that has been
   Han-majority for over a century. **This is what it looks like when the Doctrine of First Effective
   Settlement runs to completion against an existing people.** For the author it is the single clearest
   illustration in the survey of what a successful settlement actually does, and it is the reason entry 4
   (Manchuria) is a *Han* region rather than a Manchu one.
4. **The Chosŏnjok are shrinking, and that is a finding rather than a statistic.** **1.70 million, down from
   1.92 million in 1990 — the only large minority in absolute decline.** Yanbian went from about **60% Korean
   in 1952 to 32% by 2000**, and stands at **35.5% Korean against 60.2% Han in 2022** on a registered
   population of 2.015 million. Korean-language primary schools in Yanbian fell from **256 to 183 between
   1996 and 2000** with enrollment halved, while Chosŏnjok attendance at Han schools more than doubled
   between 1987 and 2000 — because Korean-school graduates have lower Mandarin fluency and worse economic
   mobility. **The decline is emigration and assimilation toward opportunity, not suppression**, and much of
   the emigration has been to South Korea. ⚠️ That last clause is inference from the pattern, not a retrieved
   finding. For the setting: a unified Korea acquires an obvious interest in a shrinking co-ethnic population
   sitting on the Manchurian border, and Yanbian is a prefecture inside Jilin, so it is **not drawable**.
5. **The Mongols are the ambiguous case and the most interesting one to write.** 6.29 million in China,
   4.1 million in Inner Mongolia — the world's largest Mongol population, larger than the sovereign state's —
   with a first-order region, a legally guaranteed chairmanship and fixed overrepresentation in the regional
   congress, but **17.7% of their own region's population, roughly half of them no longer speaking the
   language, and 40% of their marriages mixed.** The identity is surviving the loss of its own content (2.2).
   That is a genuinely unusual condition and it is not well represented in fiction.
6. **The Zhuang are the largest minority in China and have the weakest identity-claim in the table**, which
   is why 3.3 is a problem rather than an answer. 19.57 million, a first-order autonomous region, an old and
   documented political history — the Nong clan's eleventh-century polities, **Nong Zhigao's** Kingdom of the
   Great Succession (1042) and Kingdom of the Southern Heavens (1048), defeated by the Song in 1055 — and a
   separate literary tradition in **Sawndip** script. Against that: the retrieved source records mid-century
   fieldwork finding that Guangxi's "local people" **described themselves as "Han who speak the Zhuang
   language,"** and that the unified Zhuang ethnicity is explicitly a **product of the 1950s ethnic
   identification project**. They are about 32% of Guangxi. **The Zhuang have the territory and the numbers
   and, on this evidence, the thinnest self-conscious nationhood of any large group here** — which makes them
   the hardest case in the survey and the one the author most needs to decide deliberately rather than by
   default.

**Three smaller entries with disproportionate design value.**

- **Taiwanese Indigenous peoples.** 600,303 recognized in 16 peoples, over 800,000 including the Pingpu, and
  possibly over a million counting mixed ancestry — on an island where the scholarly consensus is that Han
  and Indigenous produced a **hybrid** rather than one replacing the other. Small in share, foundational in
  character, and the reason Taiwan is arguably not a Min province.
- **Ryukyuans.** Over 1.4 million, a **first-order prefecture**, a 450-year kingdom ended in 1879, a distinct
  Japonic branch, live political grievance — and **no recognition at all**. This is the largest unrecognized
  minority in the survey and the one whose claim is most disproportionate to its visibility.
- **Ainu.** 11,450 counted against perhaps 200,000 unaware, a first-order prefecture, and a language that is
  effectively gone. The weakest claim in the table by population and the strongest by principle: they are the
  only people here whose land was taken under a law that granted them citizenship **specifically in order to
  deny them Indigenous status**.

### 4.3 The one structural difference from Latin America, stated for the record

In Latin America the decisive asset was **contiguous, mapped, legally held territory that the surrounding
population already recognizes** — Brazil's 13%, Nicaragua's two autonomous regions — because in a collapse
the boundary does not have to be invented.

**East Asia has more of that asset, not less, and it is at a higher administrative level.** Five first-order
subdivisions are named for minority nations — Xinjiang Uyghur, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, Guangxi Zhuang, Ningxia
Hui — and two more first-order units (Hokkaido, Okinawa) hold unrecognized peoples. Under this project's
absolute whole-subdivision rule, **that is the single most important fact in Part 4**: the drawing units for
non-Han successor states already exist, were drawn by the states themselves, and are the correct size.

What East Asia has *less* of is the demographic dominance inside those units. Bolivia is 62% Indigenous and
four Guatemalan departments are above 89%. Against that: Tibet 86%, southwestern Xinjiang about 90% but
Xinjiang as a whole 44.96%, Inner Mongolia 17.7%, Guangxi about 32%, Ningxia a Hui minority. **Only Tibet and
the southern Tarim are majority-homeland territories in the Latin American sense.** Everywhere else, the
successor nation would be a large, organized, territorially recognized minority taking control of a unit it
does not demographically dominate — which is a much more interesting political situation than a clean
inheritance, and considerably harder to hold.

  and this map takes the more-divisions view; and it shows **distribution, not population**, so a large area
  may hold very few people — which is the Hu Line finding restated.
