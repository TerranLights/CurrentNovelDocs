# Russia — Candidate Names for Countries A, C and D (research)

**Research pass, 2026-09-27.** A menu of real-world historical/geographic/cultural names for the three
still-unnamed composite nations from the round-1/round-2 reorganization (`y-files/Map Files/Russia/02
reorganization/02 follow-up/subdivisions.csv` is the current source of truth for exact territory). This is
options for the author to choose from, not a recommendation — "research proposes, author disposes," the same
posture as every other survey in this project. Every candidate below is checked against the *actual* current
territory, not the idealized version of what each name originally meant — all three composite nations are
real mergers with real internal incoherence (round 1's own decision doc says so explicitly), and forcing a
tidy-sounding name onto that would misrepresent the fit.

---

## Country A — Sakha + the old Russian Far East + Irkutsk Oblast

12 subdivisions: Sakha (Yakutia), Amur, Buryatia, Chita (Zabaykalsky Krai), Chukotka, Kamchatka, Khabarovsk,
Magadan, Primorsky Krai, Sakhalin, Yevrey (Jewish AO), Irkutsk. Pacific-facing, spans Lake Baikal to the
Bering Strait.

### 1. The Far Eastern Republic (also: "the Chita Republic")

**What it is.** The real 1920-22 buffer state the RSFSR itself created to avoid direct war with Japan —
already the load-bearing historical precedent for this entire nation's existence in the Stage 1 survey. Its
own contemporaries also called it the Chita Republic, after its second and final capital.

**Fit.** Strong but not exact. The real FER's territory (Amur, Transbaikal, Kamchatka, Sakhalin, and briefly
Primorye/the Maritime Province, which seceded in May 1921 to form the smaller "Priamurskii Zemskii Krai")
covers most of this composite nation's western and central mass. It never administered Sakha (a separate,
much later Soviet-era republic) or meaningfully governed Chukotka (barely settled by Russians at the time) as
part of its own structure — both are real gaps between the historical entity and this map's actual borders.

**Tone.** Serious, institutional, buffer-state — reads as a genuine successor-state name, not a placeholder.
**Confidence:** high (real, specific, already cited in this project's own Stage 1 survey).

### 2. Priamurye

**What it is.** A real, older unofficial term (Приамурье, "along the Amur") for the Russian territories along
the Amur River basin, used informally in the late Russian Empire — not the name of a specific political
entity, a geographic descriptor.

**Fit.** Narrower than the FER — centers the Amur basin specifically (Amur Oblast, parts of Khabarovsk) and
doesn't naturally suggest Kamchatka, Sakhalin, Chukotka, or Sakha's inclusion at all. Reads as evocative but
under-inclusive if used as this composite nation's actual name.

**Tone.** Regionalist, geographic, softer than a state name. **Confidence:** medium (real term, real
historical usage, but a looser fit than option 1).

### 3. Transbaikalia / Zabaykalye

**What it is.** The real, standing geographic term for the region "beyond (trans-) Lake Baikal" — modern
Zabaykalsky Krai (the old Chita Oblast) is its direct namesake.

**Fit.** Too narrow to serve as the whole nation's name — it describes one subdivision (Chita/Zabaykalsky
Krai) precisely, not the Pacific coast, Kamchatka, or Sakha. Better suited as a sub-regional/poetic reference
within a larger name than as the nation's own name.

**Tone.** Evocative, place-specific. **Confidence:** high as a real term, low as a fit for the whole territory.

### 4. Sakha Sire

**What it is.** The real Sakha (Yakut) self-designation for their own homeland — "Land of the Sakha," their
own endonym for Yakutia, distinct from the Russian exonym "Yakut."

**Fit.** Weak for the composite as a whole. Sakha is one of twelve subdivisions here, not a majority
population across the merged territory — using this name would foreground an ethnic identity that's
demographically a minority within its own composite nation, the same kind of naming mismatch this project has
flagged elsewhere (e.g. Bashkortostan's Bashkirs being only third-largest in their own titular republic).
Worth listing because it's a real, meaningful term and might appeal if the author wants to foreground the
diamond-wealth/Sakha-cultural angle over the Far East's own regionalist history — but the demographic mismatch
should be a known, deliberate choice, not an accident.

**Tone.** Indigenous, poetic, would read as a surprising/bold choice given the territory's actual makeup.
**Confidence:** high as a real term, low as a literal fit.

### 5. Pacific Russia / The Pacific Republic (constructed, no direct historical precedent)

**What it is.** Not a revived historical name — a plain descriptive option built from the territory's own
defining feature (it's the only nation on this map with a genuine Pacific coastline, from Vladivostok to the
Bering Strait).

**Fit.** Covers the whole territory honestly without claiming a historical entity it doesn't quite match.
Avoids the ethnic-specificity problem Sakha Sire raises entirely.

**Tone.** Plain, modern, geographic rather than historical-revivalist. **Confidence:** n/a (constructed, not
researched — flagged as the "no perfect real name exists, so don't force one" option).

## Country C — the largest and most diverse: Volga-Ural Turkic + Finno-Ugric + Ural industrial + Arctic + four ex-core-Russia oblasts

27 subdivisions (the biggest merge on the map): Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, the old Volga Republic minus
Volgograd/Astrakhan (Chuvashia, Mari El, Mordovia, Udmurtia, Kirov, Nizhny Novgorod, Penza, Samara, Saratov,
Ulyanovsk), the old Urals Republic (Chelyabinsk, Kurgan, Orenburg, Perm, Sverdlovsk), the old Northern
Republic minus Karelia/Murmansk (Arkhangelsk, Komi, Nenets, Vologda), and four ex-core-Russia oblasts
(Yaroslavl, Vladimir, Ivanovo, Kostroma). Per round 1's own decision doc, this diversity is "by design, not by
accident" — no candidate below papers over that.

### 1. Idel-Ural

**What it is.** "Idel-Ural" means "Volga-Ural" in Tatar. A real, short-lived 1917-18 political project: Tatar,
Bashkir and Chuvash leaders convened in Kazan in June 1917, built a national council (the Millät Mäclese) in
Ufa, and proclaimed the Idel-Ural State on 1 March 1918 — defeated by the Bolsheviks the same month. Modern
academic usage (per a Finno-Ugric studies source cross-checked in this research pass) describes "the region of
Idel-Ural" as the six republics of Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Chuvashia, Mari El, Mordovia and Udmurtia — three
Turkic, three Finno-Ugric.

**Fit — the honest version.** This is a real, specific, and evocative historical name, and its modern regional
usage maps almost exactly onto six of this nation's 27 subdivisions (Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Chuvashia, Mari
El, Mordovia, Udmurtia — roughly 22% of the total). **It does not cover, and was never historically claimed
to cover, the other 21**: the Arctic north (Arkhangelsk, Komi, Nenets, Vologda), the Ural industrial belt
proper (Chelyabinsk, Kurgan, Orenburg, Perm, Sverdlovsk), the Slavic-majority Volga oblasts (Kirov, Nizhny
Novgorod, Penza, Samara, Saratov, Ulyanovsk), or the four ex-core-Russia oblasts. Using it as this composite
nation's actual name would be a real stretch — evocative and thematically resonant for the ethnic-coalition
core of the territory, but it would silently claim two-thirds of the map that the real Idel-Ural project never
touched.

**Tone.** Serious, ethnic-coalition, historically loaded (it's an irredentist project's name, not a neutral
administrative label). **Confidence:** high as a real, specific historical fact; low-to-medium as a literal
territorial fit for the actual composite.

### 2. Volga-Ural (plain English rendering, used descriptively rather than reviving Idel-Ural specifically)

**What it is.** The same geographic concept as Idel-Ural's own name, used as a neutral descriptive label
rather than a direct revival of the 1918 political project's specific claim.

**Fit.** Same core/periphery problem as option 1 — genuinely describes the Tatar/Bashkir/Volga heartland, not
the Arctic north or the Ural industrial belt, though "Ural" in the name at least gestures toward the
Chelyabinsk/Sverdlovsk/Perm cluster in a way "Idel-Ural" alone doesn't as clearly.

**Tone.** Reads as administrative/geographic rather than as reviving a specific 1918 nationalist claim — a
softer choice if the author wants the geographic resonance without the ethnic-coalition history attached.
**Confidence:** n/a (constructed from real geography, not itself a historical entity's name).

### 3. A plain compound geographic name (constructed — no strong real precedent found)

**What it is.** This research pass checked Rosstat's own 12-economic-region framework for a name that already
covers this composite's real territory — none does. The **Volga-Vyatka Economic Region** (Chuvashia, Kirov,
Mari El, Mordovia, Nizhny Novgorod) and the **Ural Economic Region** (Bashkortostan, Chelyabinsk, Kurgan,
Orenburg, Perm, Sverdlovsk, Udmurtia) between them cover 12 of the 27 subdivisions, but Tatarstan, Samara,
Saratov, Ulyanovsk and Penza actually belong to a *third*, separate real economic region (see Country D's
research below — the real "Volga Economic Region" groups them with Astrakhan/Kalmykia/Volgograd instead,
which this project's own round-1 split sent to Country D). The Arctic north and the four ex-core oblasts
belong to two more distinct real economic regions (Northern; Central) that this nation also doesn't fully
match. **No single real-world label exists for this exact assembled territory** — any name will be a
construction, not a revival. Flagged here as the honest baseline rather than a specific candidate.

**Confidence:** n/a — this is a negative finding (no name exists), included because it matters for evaluating
options 1 and 2 honestly.

### 4. A purely administrative/pragmatic name (no historical precedent — a genuine option, not a cop-out)

**What it is.** Given the territory's real, designed-in diversity and the absence of any single historical or
economic-region name that fits, a plain administrative-sounding name (something that reads like a pragmatic
federation rather than an ethnic-coalition or nostalgia-driven choice) may honestly be the best-fitting *tone*
even without a specific historical name to point to — the same logic that gave Siberia its own plain
geographic name in round 1 rather than reaching for a historical label that wouldn't fit either.

**Confidence:** n/a — a tonal recommendation about the *kind* of name that fits, not a specific candidate;
left for the author to originate.

## Country D — Southern Russia + Kalmykia + Volgograd + Astrakhan

7 subdivisions: Krasnodar Krai, Adygea, Rostov, Stavropol Krai, Kalmykia, Volgograd, Astrakhan.

### 1. Kuban

**What it is.** The real Kuban People's Republic, proclaimed by the Kuban Rada on 28 January 1918, declared
independence 16 February 1918, covering "the entire territory of the former Kuban Oblast" — occupied by
Denikin's White forces November 1919, annexed by the Soviets in spring 1920. Modern Krasnodar Krai and Adygea
are both carved from the old Kuban Oblast.

**Fit.** Strong for Krasnodar + Adygea specifically (2 of 7 subdivisions) — weak to nonexistent for Rostov
(historically Don Cossack territory, not Kuban), Stavropol, Kalmykia, Volgograd, or Astrakhan. Using "Kuban"
alone as this nation's name would accurately name less than a third of its actual territory.

**Tone.** Serious, Cossack-specific, real precedent for actual independence (however brief).
**Confidence:** high as history, low as a full-territory fit.

### 2. The Don Republic / Don-Kuban Union / "The Southeastern Union"

**What it is.** The real Don Republic (Cossack, 1918-1920, roughly the Don Host territory — modern Rostov
Oblast) was the Kuban Republic's only diplomatic recognition from another government. More broadly, at the
end of 1917 the Don, Kuban and Terek Cossack Hosts formed the real **South-Eastern Union of the Cossack Host,
Mountain Peoples and Free Peoples of the Steppe** (Юго-Восточный союз) — an explicit federation of Cossack
territories *with* non-Cossack mountain and steppe peoples, proclaiming its members "independent states of a
future Russian Democratic Federal Republic." It dissolved by March 1918 once Soviet power was declared across
all three Host territories.

**Fit.** The Don+Kuban portion (Rostov+Krasnodar+Adygea) fits well — three of seven subdivisions with strong
historical grounding. The "Terek + mountain peoples" portion of the real Southeastern Union corresponds to
territory that is *already its own set of independent nations on this map* (Chechnya, Ingushetia, North
Ossetia-Alania, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay-Cherkessia) — so reviving this name would gesture at a historical
union this map has *already chosen not to draw*, which is worth knowing before picking it. It also doesn't
touch Stavropol, Kalmykia, Volgograd, or Astrakhan at all.

**Tone.** Serious, federative, historically resonant — but carries real narrative implications about the
North Caucasus republics that aren't actually part of this nation.
**Confidence:** high as history, medium as a fit (good for 3 of 7 subdivisions, silent on the rest, and
gestures at territory this map draws separately).

### 3. Ciscaucasia

**What it is.** Предкавказье ("Hither Caucasia" / "before-Caucasus") — a real, standing geographic term for
the plains and uplands north of the Greater Caucasus range, running from the Sea of Azov to the Caspian Sea,
bounded south by the Caucasus foothills and cut by the Kuma-Manych Depression. Real sources place Kalmykia
within or immediately bordering this same geographic band (it borders Stavropol Krai directly).

**Fit — the best pure-geographic match of the three Country D candidates.** Unlike Kuban or the
Don/Southeastern Union options, Ciscaucasia is defined by geography, not by Cossack ethnic/cultural identity —
which means it doesn't have the same "does Kalmykia even belong here" problem those two carry (Kalmykia is
genuinely Mongolic/Buddhist, not Cossack, not Slavic, and a Cossack-derived name would sit oddly attached to
it the same way Idel-Ural's name would sit oddly attached to Country C's Arctic north). Ciscaucasia's own
real geographic scope comfortably includes Krasnodar, Adygea, Stavropol, and Kalmykia. Rostov sits at its
northern edge (more usually associated with the Don region specifically than with Ciscaucasia's own core
definition, though not a hard exclusion). Volgograd and Astrakhan, further north along the lower Volga, are
the weakest geographic fit of the seven subdivisions — genuinely outside Ciscaucasia's usual real-world
definition, which centers on the Caucasus foothills rather than the Volga delta.

**Tone.** Geographic, neutral, no ethnic-coalition baggage — but also no precedent as an actual state or
nation's name; it has only ever been used as a descriptive geographic region, so using it as a nation's proper
name would be a novel usage, not a revival.
**Confidence:** high as a real, standing term; medium as a full-territory fit (5-6 of 7 subdivisions, weakest
on Volgograd/Astrakhan).

### A cross-cutting note, not a candidate: the real "Volga Economic Region" already spans Countries C and D

Checked while researching Country C above, worth surfacing here too: Rosstat's real **Volga Economic Region**
groups Astrakhan, Kalmykia, Penza, Samara, Saratov, Tatarstan, Ulyanovsk and Volgograd together as one unit —
which is interesting because this project's own round-1 split sent Tatarstan/Penza/Samara/Saratov/Ulyanovsk to
Country C and Astrakhan/Kalmykia/Volgograd to Country D. Not a suggestion to undo that split (the round-1
decision doc's own reasoning for keeping Volgograd/Astrakhan with the Southern Russia/Kalmykia bloc stands on
its own terms) — just a real-world data point worth knowing: the actual Russian government's own economic
geography doesn't draw this particular line the same way this map does.
