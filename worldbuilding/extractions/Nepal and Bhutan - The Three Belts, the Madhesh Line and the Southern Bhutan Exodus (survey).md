# Nepal and Bhutan - The Three Belts, the Madhesh Line and the Southern Bhutan Exodus (survey)

**Stage 1 frameworks survey. Started 2026-09-29, first research pass only - not a finished analysis.**
Companion material-base survey not yet started (Stage 2). No map-data package exists yet (Stage 3).
Covers **Nepal and Bhutan** - the two Himalayan states of the newly opened **South Asia** region. The other
members of the region (Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the Northeast India slice including Sikkim,
Darjeeling/Kalimpong and Arunachal) are parallel passes; this file treats Sikkim and the Gorkha hills **only as
seams** (Section 9). **The rest of India is permanently out of scope**: where Nepal touches Uttarakhand, Uttar
Pradesh and Bihar this survey covers Nepal's side only and flags the seam in a sentence.
**Latin script only, by design.** All names are romanized.

**Status of the evidence.** Most facts below were pulled from Wikipedia through a summarizing fetch tool and
cross-checked against a small number of institutional or press sources (Nepal Rastra Bank via search, Kathmandu
Post, Carnegie, Human Rights Watch as cited on Wikipedia). Where two sources disagreed, both figures are given
and marked. Figures for Bhutan's ethnic shares in particular are **not** from a state ethnic count (see
Section 8.3). Nothing here is a creative decision; every fork is marked ⚠️ open.

## Headline findings

1. **First effective settlement half-holds in both countries, and in both it holds at the level of the
   landscape belt rather than the state.** Nepal's Terai was settled first (effectively) by the Tharu, whose
   malaria tolerance let them live where no one else could; Bhutan's southern foothills were settled first
   (effectively) by Nepali-speaking farmers whom the Drukpa state had left alone. Woodard's doctrine would
   award both belts their settlers' culture. **In Nepal the later arrivals (Madhesi caste Hindus, hill
   migrants) overlaid the Tharu; in Bhutan the state reversed the doctrine by force in the 1990s.** Both are
   ⚠️ this survey's inference, not a sourced scholarly claim. See Section 1.
2. **Nepal is three countries stacked by altitude, and the stacking is the map.** 2021 census: **Terai 53.61%
   of the population (15,634,006), Hill 40.31% (11,757,624), Mountain 6.08% (1,772,948)** of 29,164,578.
   A majority of Nepalis now live in the plain, and Madhesi-caste Nepal (20.83%) is the second-largest
   caste-ethnic bloc after the Khas-Arya (38.71%). See Sections 2 and 4.
3. **The 2015 province map is a compromise between identity federalism and the hill core, and every
   identity claim it overrode is still on the record.** The State Restructuring Commission of 2012 split
   over 10-11 identity-named provinces (Limbuwan, Kirat, Tamsaling, Newa, Narayani, Tamuwan, Magarat, a Dalit
   state) versus 6 provinces with names left to parliament; the constitution of 20 September 2015 produced
   seven provinces instead. **Only eight of the Terai's roughly twenty districts sit in Madhesh Province**,
   while **Madhesh Province holds only about 39% of the Terai's population** (6,114,600 of 15,634,006). See
   Section 3.
4. **The Madhesh line is the real fracture, and it is a live one.** Three Madhesh movements (2007, 2008,
   2015-16); **more than 50 killed in 2015-16**; a 135-odd-day border blockade (23 September 2015 to February
   2016) that cost Nepali business an estimated **Rs 202.5 billion (about US$1.96 billion)** and pushed Nepal
   toward China. Yet in March 2026 the winning party took **30 of 32** first-past-the-post seats in Madhesh
   Province, so the fracture has been partly absorbed by a national anti-establishment wave. See Section 6.
5. **Remittances are 28.6% of Nepal's GDP (FY2024/25) - Nepal is the Central Asian labor-export case at
   South Asian scale**, and its army-recruitment tradition (the Gurkhas) is the cleanest real-world example of
   Lattimore's "reservoir" in this project's corpus. See Section 7.
6. **Bhutan's central ethnic fact is the 1990s expulsion of the Lhotshampa: over 100,000 left by 1996, 112,800
   were later resettled abroad (about 85% to the United States), and only ~11,762 remained in Nepal's camps in
   2016.** It was carried out by a small Buddhist monarchy that
   simultaneously promotes Gross National Happiness as its state ideology (⚠️ 'ethnic cleansing' is the
   characterization of the refugee-side and human-rights sources cited, not a neutral finding). See Section 8.4.
7. **Bhutan has no border with the rest of India under the author's pinned line.** Bhutan borders only Sikkim,
   West Bengal (Kalimpong/Alipurduar/Jalpaiguri area), Assam and Arunachal Pradesh, and **every one of those
   is inside the breakaway landmass.** Both of the first India-Bhutan rail links (Kokrajhar-Gelephu and
   Banarhat-Samtse, MoU 29 September 2025) also run into breakaway territory. Bhutan's whole hydropower
   export economy (roughly 63% of exports by one source) is pointed at a customer that would now be separated
   from it by the Siliguri Corridor. ⚠️ This is a geographic inference from the pinned line, flagged for the
   author. See Section 9.
8. **Bhutan lost the Duars in 1864-65, and the Duars are inside the breakaway landmass.** The Assam and Bengal
   Duars (which include today's Alipurduar/Jalpaiguri and the lowlands of western Assam) were ceded under the
   Treaty of Sinchula. A dormant, unclaimed historical seam that becomes non-trivial once India's hold on the
   region dissolves. See Section 9.3.

## Section 1 - Method: does first effective settlement hold, fail, or need replacing?

Per `METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md`, Woodard's doctrine needs an arriving population that founds a
self-perpetuating society in territory that is effectively open. The project has so far recorded four ways the
doctrine fails (East Asia: no founding moment; Mainland Southeast Asia: the culture was chosen; Central Asia:
borders were an outside power's engineering; Russia: an old imperial core annexing populated neighbors).
Nepal and Bhutan add a **fifth**: **both are old highland states whose core was never colonized, wrapped
around lowland or foothill belts that were.**

### 1.1 Nepal - a conquest state with an internal settlement frontier

- **The state was founded by conquest, not settlement.** Prithvi Narayan Shah's Gorkha kingdom took the
  Kathmandu Valley in 1768 (the Gorkhali army entered Kathmandu on 25 September 1768, during Indra Jatra) and
  spent the next decades absorbing the small hill principalities. That is a
  core annexing populated neighbors - the Russia/East Asia pattern - not a founding-settler moment.
- **The doctrine does bite on the Terai.** The Tharu have lived in the Terai "since at least the 10th century"
  (per the Tharu page's own dating) and are notable for a malaria tolerance tied to thalassemia genes. That is
  a textbook Woodard first effective settlement: a population that alone could make the lowland
  self-perpetuating. ⚠️ **Inference:** the later layers - Madhesi caste Hindus in the central and eastern
  Terai, then hill migrants after malaria eradication (background knowledge, not fetched this pass) - are the
  "later immigrants of other backgrounds" whom the doctrine says should not change the founding character.
  The Tharuhat/Tharuwan claim (Section 6.3) is precisely that argument made by a living movement.
- **Tier-1 structure.** Ethnic identity works better than settlement here, per the METHOD file's own
  refinement. Proposed tiers, same table format as the Central Asia survey:

| Tier 1 | Example Tier 2 identities | Where | Historical mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Khas-Arya (Indo-Aryan hill caste Hindu)** | Chhetri (16.45%), Hill Brahman/Bahun (11.29%), Thakuri, Sanyasi; **plus Hill Dalits (Kami, Damai, Sarki; 8.59%)** counted inside "Khas-Arya" by the census | Middle hills, west to east, and every town | The Gorkha ruling stratum; Nepali is its language |
| **Hill Janajati (Tibeto-Burman)** | Magar (6.90%), Tamang (5.62%), Rai (2.20%), Limbu (1.42%), Gurung (1.86%); Newar (4.60%) is counted separately | Hills; Kirat east, Magar west-central, Tamang around Kathmandu | Pre-Gorkha hill polities; the Gurkha recruiting pool |
| **Bhotiya / Himalayan Buddhist** | Sherpa (0.45%), Thakali, Lo/Mustang and Dolpo peoples | High Himal and northern borderland | Trans-Himalayan trade and Tibetan Buddhist culture |
| **Terai Adivasi** | Tharu (6.20%), and smaller groups | Terai, especially the west | First effective settlers of the plain |
| **Madhesi (Terai Indo-Aryan)** | Yadav (4.21%), Teli, Kushwaha, Kurmi, Musahar, plus Madhesi Dalits (4.78%); Maithili/Bhojpuri/Bajjika speakers | Central and eastern Terai | Continuous with the Gangetic plain to the south |
| **Muslims** | 4.86% | Terai, concentrated in the central and western plain | Continuous with the Gangetic plain |

**⚠️ This is a simplification, and the census categories are themselves political.** The 2021 census recorded
142 caste/ethnic groups (125 in 2011); the "Khas-Arya", "Adivasi-Janajati", "Madhesi" and "Dalit" headings are
policy categories from the affirmative-action system, not clean ethnolinguistic families. Dalits cut across
Hill and Madhesi lines (13.4% combined by this survey's arithmetic on the census categories: 8.59% Hill plus
4.78% Madhesi).

### 1.2 Bhutan - a chosen-state culture with a settler foothill belt

- **The state was built by a religious refugee, not settlers.** Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal (1594-1651) arrived
  in 1616 from Tibet, repelled Tibetan invasions (1629, 1631, 1639, 1647 per the Wikipedia timeline), built a
  dzong network (Simtokha Dzong, 1629), and set up a **dual system** of secular Druk Desi and religious Je
  Khenpo. His purpose was "a distinct Bhutanese cultural identity separate from the Tibetan culture from which
  it was derived." **That is the Mainland Southeast Asia 'chosen culture' pattern**: an institution imported
  from outside, spread over already-present peoples (the Sharchop, Bumthangpa, Kurtoep, Khengpa and
  others, see Section 8.3), and the Nyingma and Sakya schools were permitted to continue in the east.
- **The doctrine bites in the south, in reverse.** Nepali-speaking settlers moved into southern Bhutan's
  "uninhabited areas," with a first small phase in the late 16th-early 17th century, a much larger one after
  1885, and another from the 1960s when development projects needed labor (Wikipedia, Lhotshampa page).
  The settlers built farms in malarial foothills the highland Buddhist state did not want. **By Woodard's rule
  that makes the Nepali-speaking south a settled, self-perpetuating society with its own founding character.**
  The state's answer, from 1985, was to define it out of existence (Section 8.4).
- **The state's own fear reads like Woodard.** In a 1992 interview Bhutan's foreign minister Dawa Tsering
  said a growing Nepali population would make Bhutan "another Sikkim and Darjeeling." In Sikkim the
  Nepali-speaking majority became demographically decisive before the 1975 annexation. Bhutan's policy is
  therefore an acknowledged attempt to stop a first-effective-settlement dynamic from finishing.

### 1.3 Replacement: buffer state + three-belt vertical model + sphere overlays

The Mainland Southeast Asia survey's mandala/Zomia frame does not transfer cleanly. **Zomia's core idea is
ungoverned highlands whose peoples avoid the state (Scott).** Nepal and Bhutan are the opposite: both are
**states with old, centralized cores** (Gorkha-Kathmandu; the Zhabdrung's dzong network), the same objection
the East Asia survey recorded when Michaud excluded Tibet from the Southeast Asian Massif "because Tibet had a
centralized, religiously unified core." Scott's own stated range runs "from the Central Highlands of Vietnam
to northeastern India" and never reaches Nepal or Bhutan in the text this project quotes. ⚠️ Whether Nepal
and Bhutan belong to Zomia at all is disputed and was **not verified** this pass (van Schendel not read).
Some scholars extend it west to Tibet and northeast India (per the Mainland survey), which would touch
Bhutan's east and Nepal's northern borderlands, but not their state cores.

What this survey proposes instead - **three linked pieces**, none of them a decision:

1. **Three-belt vertical model.** Terai / Hill / Himal are Nepal's actual organizing geography, and Bhutan has
   a matching three-zone structure (southern foothills or Duars strip, inner valleys, high Himalaya per the
   Wikipedia Bhutan page). The belts sort languages, religions, states and diseases. Section 2 gives the
   numbers.
2. **Buffer-state logic.** Both countries have survived as buffers between larger neighbors: Nepal between
   the British Raj and Qing Tibet, then between India and China; Bhutan between Tibet and British India, then
   between India and China. Their survival mechanism is not internal unity but being cheaper to keep neutral
   than to absorb. That logic is carried by the project's **core/domain/sphere** vocabulary, and it is
   exactly Lattimore's frontier concept.
3. **Sphere overlays across the Himalayan rim** rather than borders: a **Nepali-speaking / Gorkha sphere**
   (Nepal, Sikkim, Darjeeling/Kalimpong, southern Bhutan), a **Kirat sphere** (Rai/Limbu across eastern Nepal,
   Sikkim and Darjeeling), and a **Bhotiya/Tibetan-Buddhist rim** (Sherpa, Mustang and Dolpo peoples, Bhutan's
   Ngalop, Sikkim's Bhutia). ⚠️ **Open:** whether any of these enter the sphere layer of the map CSVs at all.
   Nepal's Gurkha system also fits **Lattimore's "reservoir"** - the frontier belt as the recruiting ground of
   whoever rules the core - very literally (Section 7.3).

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Hill | Pahad | **40.31%** (11,757,624) | 68% | 1,000-3,000 m, terraced farming, both the Kathmandu and Pokhara valleys, and the inner-Terai dun valleys |

⚠️ **The area and population figures use different definitions.** The area column is physiographic (from the
Geography page); the population column is the census's own ecological-belt count. Other sources give the
Terai a larger area share under the ecological definition; that figure was **not** verified this pass, so the
table should not be read as "17% of the land holds 54% of the people" without checking the definition.
Whatever the definition, the direction is not in doubt: **most Nepalis now live in the Terai.**

- **Urbanization:** 66.17% urban (19,296,788) per the 2021 census - high because of a generous municipal
  definition (2015 restructuring created 753 local bodies, many rural in fact). ⚠️ Treat as a definitional
  figure.
- **Borders:** with India, about 1,690 km on the Geography page (other sources cite about 1,751 km); with
  China, 1,236 km on the Geography page vs 1,389 km on the China-Nepal border page. ⚠️ **Length discrepancies
  are unresolved and probably reflect different measurement bases.** Crossings named: Birgunj-Raxaul,
  Biratnagar-Jogbani, Bhairahawa-Sunauli/Nautanwa (India); Kodari-Zhangmu (operating since 1968),
  Rasuwagadhi-Gyirong (opened for commerce 2014), Hilsa-Burang, and Korala/Lo Manthang (opened 2023) on the
  Tibet side.
- **Absent abroad:** 2,190,592 people, about 7.5% of the population, were recorded as absent from Nepal in
  the 2021 census (International Migration report). The largest diaspora stock is in India (about 2.93
  million per the Demographics page), then the UAE (400,000) and Saudi Arabia (297,000). ⚠️ These stock
  figures come from different dates and sources.
- **Rivers:** the Koshi, Gandaki, Karnali and Mahakali all cross the Terai into the Ganges basin. This is the
  same shape as the Central Asia upstream/downstream problem, and it is where Nepal's hydropower ambition
  (Section 7.4) meets India's flood and irrigation interest. ⚠️ India's water-management stake was not
  researched here - that would mean studying India.

### 3.2 How they were drawn, and the controversy

- **Before 2015:** 14 zones grouped into five development regions (Provinces of Nepal page). The provinces
  were created on 20 September 2015 with the constitution, initially numbered (Province 1 to 7) with names left
  to each provincial assembly by a two-thirds vote (Article 295(2)). **Province 2 became Madhesh Province on
  20 January 2022 (the Provinces page and the Madhesh Province page date the rename to January 2022);
  Janakpur became its capital.** Koshi (Province 1), Bagmati (3), Gandaki (4), Lumbini (5), Karnali (6),
  Sudurpashchim (7) took their names as their assemblies decided.
- **The 2012 identity-versus-capability split.** The State Restructuring Commission split into two reports:
  a faction led by coordinator Madan Pariyar (including Stella Tamang, Malla K Sundar, Bhogendra Jha,
  Krishna Hachhethu and Surendra Mahato) proposed **11 states** named Limbuwan, Kirat, Tamsaling, Newa,
  Narayani, Tamuwan, Magarat and a non-geographic Dalit state (among others); a faction led by Ramesh Dhungel,
  Sabitri Gurung and Sarbaraj Khadka proposed **six provinces with names for parliament to fix**. A separate
  committee had proposed 14. Sources describe the commission's report as backed by six non-Khas-Arya
  members. ⚠️ The search result gave 11 states in one place and "10-province model" in another; treat "10 to
  11" as the accurate range. The first Constituent Assembly then dissolved in 2012; the second was elected in
  2013. Maoist leader remarks recorded in the same source suggested merging Limbuwan with Khumbuwan, and Newa
  with Tamsaling, "for economic viability."
- **The 2015 result.** The 2015 constitution passed with 538 of 598 votes on the Wikipedia constitution page
  (⚠️ some other accounts give about 507 in favor; the difference was not reconciled). It set seven
  provinces, 165 first-past-the-post plus 110 proportional House seats (275), and 753 local levels.
- **What the Madhesh movement said the map did.** Only eight of the Terai's twenty-odd districts formed a
  Madhesh province while fourteen hill districts became a province of their own (the Madhesh movement page's
  count; it also says "Terai's 20 of 75 districts house 50% of population"). The fetched claim that Terai
  districts are only "8" and hill districts "14" refers to the initial province arrangement, and
  ⚠️ **was not independently checked** against the 2015 boundaries. What is checkable: Koshi, Lumbini and
  Sudurpashchim each cut a Terai piece off from Madhesh and pair it with hills.
- **The identity-named provinces that did not happen:**
  - **Limbuwan** (Limbu; the area between the Arun and Tamur/Koshi rivers and the Mechi/Kanchenjunga east).
    The Limbuwan page describes ten historic Limbu kingdoms integrated into Nepal after the Limbuwan-Gorkha
    war of 1771-1774 by treaty (the kipat system left autonomous land rights with Limbu headmen, or Subbas).
    Limbuwan is also described as having lost land to Sikkim earlier. A "Federal Limbuwan State Council"
    exists. ⚠️ The Limbuwan Wikipedia page carries neutrality and citation warnings, and no 2015-2025
    activism detail was retrieved.
  - **Khumbuwan** (Rai) and **Kirat** (the two together, under the Kirat name in the 2012 list).
  - **Tharuhat / Tharuwan** (Tharu; the west-central Terai, Section 6.3).
  - **Madhesh** as a single Terai-wide province from Jhapa to Kanchanpur - the "one Madhesh, one province"
    demand. What emerged was Madhesh as a **narrow central strip** of eight districts.
  - **Tamsaling** (Tamang), **Newa** (Newar), **Magarat** and **Tamuwan** (Magar and Gurung in the western
    hills). Many hill Janajati identity maps were absorbed into mixed hill provinces.
- **The August 2015 violence.** The Tikapur (Kailali) attack of **24 August 2015** killed **eight people -
  seven police officers and a two-year-old child** per the Tikapur page (a search result summarizing another
  source said 20; ⚠️ unreconciled, the eight-death figure is the one on the dedicated page). A Rastriya Janata
  Party Nepal MP, Resham Lal Chaudhary, and 11 others were convicted; the main perpetrators got life terms in
  March 2019, and **Chaudhary was pardoned by the President in May 2023.** The violence was tied to a Tharuhat
  Struggle Committee and to the demand to keep Kailali and Kanchanpur out of a far-west province and inside a
  Tharuhat unit. (The Tikapur page does not itself spell out the Kailali-Kanchanpur placement complaint; that
  detail comes from the wider reporting and should be confirmed.)
- **Amendments.** Two amendments since 2015 (2016 and 2020, per the Constitution page); the Madhesh page says a
  second attempted amendment failed in August 2017 for want of two-thirds support. ⚠️ The 2020 amendment
  referenced on the constitution page is (from the Kalapani page) the June 2020 amendment granting legal
  status to Nepal's new map - unrelated to internal federal boundaries.

### 3.3 The federalism question in 2026

**The provincial structure is the live constitutional target.** The RSP (the party that won March 2026,
Section 5) has been publicly split over provinces: at its first general convention in Bharatpur, Chitwan, the
finance minister (Swarnim Wagle) presented a **policy of abolishing provincial assemblies**; party
chairperson Rabi Lamichhane presented a reform (not abolition) position, and the general secretary said the
chair's position is official. Prime Minister Balendra ("Balen") Shah had campaigned in Janakpur on making
provinces stronger. A Kathmandu Post column on the government's first 100 days (completed 4 July 2026) found
declining fiscal transfers to provinces and local governments and little progress on provincial civil-service
and police legislation. **Reading:** no formal proposal to redraw or abolish provinces has passed; the risk
to identity and Madhesh claims is centralization by neglect. ⚠️ Not verified whether any formal
constitutional-review commission exists as of September 2026.

### 4.2 Language (mother tongue), 2021 census

Nepali 44.86% (13,084,457), **Maithili 11.05%** (3,222,389), **Bhojpuri 6.24%** (1,820,795), Tharu 5.88%
(1,714,091), Tamang 4.88% (1,423,075), Bajjika 3.89%, Magar Dhut 2.78%. **124 mother tongues** recorded. (2011
figures on the Demographics page: Nepali 44.6%, Maithili 11.7%, Bhojpuri 5.98%, Tharu 5.77%.) So Nepali is the
mother tongue of **less than half** the country, and **the Indo-Aryan Terai languages (Maithili, Bhojpuri,
Bajjika, Awadhi, plus Urdu) together exceed 20%** on the visible numbers. The constitution names Nepali in
Devanagari as the official language but recognizes all mother tongues as "languages of the nation"; provincial
language commissions recommend provincial official languages (Koshi: Maithili and Limbu, per the province-wise
search result).

### 4.3 Religion, 2021 census

Hinduism 81.19% (23,677,744), Buddhism 8.21% (2,393,549), Islam 5.09% (1,483,066), **Kirat Mundhum 3.17%

## Section 5 - Nepal's political history, to September 2026

- **Unification.** Gorkha's Prithvi Narayan Shah began the conquest of the small hill principalities in the
  1740s (Nuwakot in 1744), entered Kathmandu on 25 September 1768, and consolidated the Shah dynasty by 1769.
  Limbuwan was taken by treaty after the 1771-74 war.
- **Wars.** A war with Tibet and its Qing patron in 1788-1791 (Wikipedia gives 1788-1791; ⚠️ other
  chronologies date the settlement to 1792 - unverified). The **Anglo-Nepalese War, 1814-1816**, ended with
  the **Treaty of Sugauli (signed 4 March 1816)**: Nepal ceded the Kali-to-Rapti lowlands, all hill territory
  west of the Kali River (Kumaon, Garhwal), and the eastern lands toward the Mechi and Darjeeling. The
  **Boundary Treaty of 1860** returned a portion of the western Terai in gratitude for Gurkha help in 1857
  (⚠️ the return of the four "Naya Muluk" districts Banke, Bardiya, Kailali and Kanchanpur is background
  knowledge, not fetched this pass). The unclear river boundary still drives the **Kalapani (about 35 km2) and
  Susta (20-140 km2)** disputes.
- **The Ranas, 1846-1951.** Jung Bahadur Rana began 105 years of hereditary prime-ministerial rule; slavery
  was abolished in 1924. The kings lived as figureheads.
- **Modern politics.** King Tribhuvan fled to India in 1950, triggering the armed revolt that ended Rana rule;
  1959 elections; King Mahendra dissolved democracy in 1960 and imposed the partyless Panchayat system. The
  **1990 Jana Andolan** restored multiparty democracy.
- **The Maoist war, 1996-2006.** About **17,000 deaths** (Wikipedia History page). 1 June 2001, the royal
  massacre that killed King Birendra and family. The **2006 Jana Andolan** ended direct royal rule; the
  Constituent Assembly declared the country a federal democratic republic in 2008 (the fetched history page
  rendered the abolition as 2006, ⚠️ a conflation - the author's own scope statement and standard accounts
  put the abolition of the monarchy at 2008). First Constituent Assembly 2008-2012; second 2013-2015; the
  April 2015 earthquake delayed the constitution; promulgated 20 September 2015.
- **Pro-monarchy revival, 2025.** After former king Gyanendra's 19 February 2025 video message, **28 March
  2025** saw a pro-monarchy rally in Kathmandu led by Durga Prasai and Nabaraj Subedi; **2 deaths, over 110
  injured or arrested**. The demand: a restored constitutional Hindu monarchy. A pro-monarchy alliance
  announced further protests from 29 May 2025. The 2026 election produced a party (RSP) with no monarchist
  mandate; RPP, the main monarchist party, won 5 seats (3.05% PR).
- **The September 2025 Gen Z protests.** After the government banned 26 platforms on 4 September 2025, mass
  protests began 8 September; police killed at least 19 that day; **9 September** Prime Minister K.P. Sharma
  Oli resigned and protesters set fire to parliament, Singha Durbar, the Supreme Court, the presidential
  residence and party offices; **76 dead in total** (22 protesters, 3 police, 10 prisoners shot in prison
  breaks, 41 others) with **2,660 confirmed injured** (May 2026); over 13,500 prisoners escaped; the army took
  Tribhuvan Airport on 9 September; **12 September** former chief justice **Sushila Karki became interim prime
  minister** (first woman PM), chosen by a Discord poll of 100,000-plus members; parliament dissolved.
- **The March 2026 election.** Held **5 March 2026**, turnout 59.08% (FPTP). **Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP)
  182 of 275 seats** - about 122 FPTP plus about 60 PR - two short of a two-thirds majority; Nepali Congress
  38, CPN (UML) 25, Nepali Communist Party 17, Shram Sanskriti Party 7, RPP 5. Communist vote share fell from
  about 40% to 21%. **Balen Shah**, former Kathmandu mayor, became prime minister, **sworn in 27 March 2026**,
  and beat Oli in Oli's home district of Jhapa. Carnegie describes him as "from the Madhes" (⚠️ that
  characterization was taken from one summary; his origin was not independently checked). The government
  later arrested former PM Oli and former home minister Ramesh Lekhak (Wikipedia; ⚠️ no source dated this).
  Madhesh-based parties (PSP-N, Janamat) underperformed; **RSP won 30 of 32 FPTP seats in Madhesh Province.**
  **Reading:** the 2026 map is anti-establishment, not ethnic; Madhesh identity parties were outpolled by a
  national urban-youth party in their own province. That may prove temporary, and no dated evidence yet
  shows the identity claims of Section 6 have dissolved.

## Section 6 - The Madhesh line and other fracture claims

### 6.1 What Madhesh is

Madhesh is the Terai as a political and cultural identity: the Indo-Aryan-speaking plain along the Indian
border, **20.83% of Nepal's population by the census categories** (Madhesi castes plus Madhesi Dalits),
concentrated in the eight districts of Madhesh Province but spread along the whole Terai. The terrain,
language (Maithili, Bhojpuri, Bajjika, Awadhi) and Hindu-Muslim caste society are continuous with the Gangetic
plain to the south, and **there is no natural line between the Terai and the Indian state on the other side of
the border.** (Cross-border kinship - the "roti-beti" pattern of marriage across the border - is standard
background knowledge, not fetched this pass. ⚠️ This survey does **not** research Bihar or Uttar Pradesh; the
seam is flagged in one sentence: **Madhesh borders Bihar along its full length, outside the breakaway line.**)

### 6.2 The three movements (Madhesh movement page)

| Movement | Date | Trigger | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | January 2007 | Interim Constitution ignored federalism; Madhesi Janaadhikar Forum burned constitution copies at Maitighar Mandala | Federalism entered politics |
| II | 2008 | MJF-Nepal, Terai Madhes Loktantrik Party and Sadbhavana Party; demands for federalism, proportional representation and population-based constituencies | Federalism written into the 2008 Interim Constitution |
| III | 2015-16 | 2015 Constitution "backtracked"; Madhesi and Tharu protest and boycott | **More than 50 killed**; blockade; first amendment attempt January 2016; second amendment failed in August 2017 |

Core demands: **one unified Madhesh province; proportional representation in state organs; population-based
delimitation** (the "20 of 75 districts hold 50% of population" claim); gender-equal citizenship rights; and
inclusion for marginalized Madhesi groups (17 groups get **45% of state jobs**, criticized for bundling
historically marginalized Madhesis with dominant upper castes).

## Section 7 - Nepal's dependencies: remittances, army, water, India and the Tibet border

### 7.1 Remittances

- **28.6% of GDP in FY2024/25** (Nepal Rastra Bank annual data via search), **Rs 1,723.27 billion, up 19.2%**
  from the year before; 25.3% in 2023/24; 23.2% in 2022/23. The Kathmandu Post reported (May 2026) that Nepal
  receives about Rs 7 billion in remittances daily.
- **Scale of migration:** 2,190,592 absent in the 2021 census (7.5%).
- **The structural analogue:** Tajikistan (about 30% of GDP, 98% from Russia) and Kyrgyzstan (about 28%).
  ⚠️ **What was not found:** the destination breakdown of Nepal's remittances (Gulf, Malaysia, India). India
  as a single point of failure cannot be asserted from this pass. Gulf and Malaysian labor markets are the
  standard account but were not fetched.

### 7.2 Gross figures (Wikipedia Economy page)

GDP about **US$43.67 billion (nominal, 2024)**; agriculture 21.9% of GDP and 43.1% of employment (2020);
tourism about 6.7% of GDP (11.58 million tourist arrivals cited for 2025 - ⚠️ an unusually high figure not
cross-checked); 20.3% below the national poverty line (2022).

### 7.3 The Gurkhas

- **Origins:** recruitment began after the Anglo-Nepalese War; about 5,000 men entered British service by
  1815. The British at first recruited only Gurung and Magar men for Gorkha units; modern recruitment adds Rai
  and Limbu.
- **1947 Tripartite Agreement (UK, India, Nepal):** ten regiments split - **six regiments (12 battalions) to
  the Indian Army, four (eight battalions) to the British Army.** Signed 1 May 1947 and ratified 9 November
  1947 (search result).
- **Current:** Indian Army about 42,000 in seven regiments (**as of 2009 - dated**); British Army 4,010
  (undated); Singapore Police Gurkha Contingent; Brunei about 500. ⚠️ Recall from memory suggests the Indian
  Army figure is lower today; all these are stale or undated.
- **The 2022 rupture:** after India's **Agnipath** short-service scheme, Nepal told India that recruiting
  Nepali soldiers under it did not fit the 1947 agreement, and **no Nepal-domiciled Gorkha has enlisted in the
  Indian Army since** (ThePrint/ORF per search). ⚠️ Whether this has been resolved by mid-2026 was not
  checked.
- **Why it matters here:** the Gurkha system is the **"reservoir"** of this project's Lattimore vocabulary.
  The frontier belt (Magar, Gurung, Rai, Limbu of the mid-hills) supplies the fighting men of the core's
  patron. The **Nepali-speaking diaspora in Darjeeling, Sikkim and the Northeast** (Gorkha identity) is partly
  a product of this recruitment (background knowledge, ⚠️ not fetched).
- **Casualties:** about 20,000 in WWI and about 32,000 in WWII (Gurkha page).

### 7.4 Hydropower

- **Installed capacity:** about **4,105 MW** (Economy page) versus about **3,435 MW** in a 2025 US Commercial
  Service summary, both with over 95% from hydro. ⚠️ **Two figures, unreconciled**, probably different dates.
  Technically viable potential: about 42,133 MW (Economy page). Under construction about 4,063 MW; awaiting
  construction about 16,029 MW.
- **Exports:** India approved purchase of an additional 200 MW in August 2025, taking the approved export to
  India to **1,140.7 MW**; Nepal has approval to export up to 1,200 MW to India and Bangladesh. **January 2024
  power trade agreement: 10,000 MW to India over ten years.** **Bangladesh: 40 MW annually from 15 June to
  15 November** via Indian territory, with a further 20 MW from June 2026 (total 60 MW). Export earnings
  **Rs 29.32 billion in FY2025-26 vs Rs 17.45 billion the year before**; the Kathmandu Post reports an
  electricity trade surplus of Rs 18.76 billion for FY2025-26.
- **Structural point:** every megawatt Nepal exports crosses the **Indian grid**, and the Bangladesh sale
  crosses it twice. In the pinned-line world that grid is mostly *outside* the breakaway landmass and outside
  this project's research scope. ⚠️ Flagged as a seam only.
- **Nepal's monsoon dependency (dry-season shortfalls)** is the standard hydro-import cycle. It was not
  measured this pass.

### 7.5 Trade dependence on India, transit and the China alternative

- **2024-25:** **67% of exports go to India** (of US$3.74 billion) and **71% of imports come from India**
  (of US$17.78 billion); **17% of imports come from China** (Economy page). ⚠️ The import total looks high for
  a US$43 billion economy - kept as sourced, unverified.
- **The 2015-16 blockade** is the natural experiment. It began **23 September 2015**, ran to February 2016;
  Nepal imported **all** its petroleum through India; schools, markets and public transport shut; losses to
  private sector **Rs 202.5 billion (about US$1.96 billion)** (Kathmandu Post, June 2016). India denied
  imposing a blockade and called it an obstruction by Madhesi protesters. In 2016 Nepal signed the **Transit
  Transport Agreement with China**, giving access to **four Chinese seaports (Tianjin, Shenzhen, Lianyungang,
  Zhanjiang) and three land ports (Lanzhou, Lhasa, Shigatse)**. ⚠️ The Kathmandu Post reported that seven
  years on (April 2023) **no shipment had moved under the deal.** The alternative exists only on paper.
- **Land route, eastern end:** Nepal's Jhapa-Ilam frontier (Kakarbhitta) opens to the **Siliguri Corridor**,
  the route toward Kolkata and, past the corridor, Bangladesh. This is the same Nepal-Bihar-West Bengal
  tripoint that the pinned line starts from (Section 9).

### 7.6 The Tibet border

- **1961 boundary treaty** (which "formally recognised Tibet as a part of China"); Everest on the line;
  crossings listed in Section 2. Nepal has documented alleged **Chinese encroachment in Humla District** (2021),
  with a leaked 2022 report of a fence around a border pillar. ⚠️ Not independently confirmed.
- **In this project's world**, Tibet is the **Tibet Autonomous Region and nothing else, and it never joins the
  Sinian Federation** (STATUS tracker, settled). So Nepal's northern neighbor is **an independent Tibet, not
  a Chinese province.** The Kodari, Rasuwagadhi and Hilsa crossings become Nepal-Tibet crossings. See
  `East Asia - Frontiers, Civilizational Spheres and the Han Core (survey).md`, Section 2.4, for the Tibet
  frame, and note that Tibet's water (the Plateau's rivers) and the Tibetan population in Nepal are that
  file's business. The Trans-Himalayan railway plan (Kerung to Kathmandu) is flagged but was not researched.

### 8.3 Ethnic layers and language geography

- **Attributed ethnic shares** (Wikipedia Demographics page, labeled 2017 census): **Ngalop 35.67%,
  Sharchop 30.41%, Lhotshampa 24.32%, other Tibeto-Burman groups 6.20%, Indo-Aryan and other minorities about
  3.40%.** The Ethnic groups page gives a coarser split: Ngalop plus Sharchop combined 63%, Lhotshampa 22%,
  indigenous and migrant groups 15%. **⚠️ Bhutan's census, as far as this pass could tell, counts mother tongue
  rather than ethnicity, so these are not state ethnic counts.** The **US State Department estimated the
  Lhotshampa at about 35% of the population in 2008** (Lhotshampa page) - a sharply different number, and the
  gap between roughly 22-24% and 35% is itself the ethnic story (Section 8.4). The 24.32% share applied to
  727,145 would be about 177,000; the 35% share about 254,000. The Lhotshampa page also cites an estimate of
  about **242,000** Lhotshampa in the country as a whole.
- **Languages** (Wikipedia Demographics page; year not stated): **Tshangla 28.0%, Dzongkha 24.0%, Nepali 22.0%,
  other 26.0%.** So **Tshangla (Sharchopkha) is the largest mother tongue** and the "national" language
  Dzongkha is the first language of a quarter of the population. Dzongkha was declared the national language
  in 1971 and is compulsory in school; the Languages page lists roughly 160,000 native Dzongkha speakers
  (⚠️ inconsistent with 24% of the population, which would be about 175,000 - unresolved), Tshangla about
  138,000 (⚠️ again inconsistent with 28%), Kheng 40,000, Bumthangkha 30,000, Kurtoep 10,000, and **Nepali the
  only native Indo-Aryan language**. About **two dozen** languages are spoken.
- **Where each group sits** (from the Ethnic groups and Language pages, and the Samtse and Sarpang pages):
  - **Ngalop** - "people of Tibetan origin who migrated to Bhutan as early as the 9th century," western and
    northern valleys; Dzongkha speakers (Thimphu, Paro, Punakha, Wangdue Phodrang, Haa, Gasa).
  - **Sharchop** - the east ("populations of mixed Tibetan and Southeast Asian descent"), Tshangla speakers,
    "largely assimilated into the Tibetan-Ngalop culture": Trashigang, Trashiyangtse, Mongar, Pemagatshel,
    Samdrup Jongkhar. The name means "easterners."
  - **Central minorities** with their own languages: Bumthangpa (Bumthang), Kurtoep (Lhuentse), Khengpa
    (Zhemgang), Layap (Gasa), Brokpa (Merak and Sakteng in Trashigang), each a Bodish-family language
    community. (Layap and Brokpa placements are background knowledge, not fetched.)
  - **Southern Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa** in **Samtse, Sarpang, Tsirang (Chirang)** primarily, and by
    extension Dagana and Chhukha. **Samtse** also holds **Lepcha (about 2,000, northeastern Samtse) and Lhokpu
    (about 2,500 along the Chukha border)** and the **Lhop/Doya (about 2,500)**, possible aboriginals with
    animist beliefs; it has 15 gewogs. **Sarpang** has 12 gewogs, Nepali as the dominant language, and the
    Kheng language (an East Bodish language) in the northeast.
  - **Arithmetic on the five south-facing dzongkhags** (Samtse, Chhukha, Sarpang, Dagana, Tsirang): together
    **224,901 people, 30.9% of Bhutan** at the 2017 count. That is not a Lhotshampa count - Chhukha and Dagana
    are mixed and the exodus emptied part of the south - but it bounds the south's weight.
- **Religion** (Demographics page): **Buddhism 74.7%, Hinduism 22.6%, Bon 1.9%, Christianity 0.5%, Islam
  0.2%.** Hinduism at 22.6% corresponds almost one-for-one with the Nepali-speaking share.

### 8.4 The central ethnic fact: the 1990s expulsion

- **The legal turn.** The **1985 Citizenship Act** used **1958** as the cutoff year for proving residence; the
  **1988 census** reclassified many Lhotshampa as "Non-nationals: Migrants and Illegal Settlers"; in **1989**
  the state imposed **Driglam Namzha** (national dress and etiquette code, enforced in public during business
  hours) and a "One Nation, One People" assimilation policy, and **Nepali was removed from schools in favor of
  Dzongkha**.
- **The crisis.** Protests and clashes began in **1990** (the Bhutan Peoples' Party, Lhotshampa-led, mounted a
  campaign against the government); interethnic conflict flared again in **1992**, "prompting a peak in
  Lhotshampa departures, totaling **over 100,000 by 1996**." Many Lhotshampa say the military forced them to
  sign "Voluntary Migration Forms"; Human Rights Watch (as cited) says state forces "forcibly removed ethnic
  Lhotshampa from their homes and coerced them into renouncing their citizenship." Bhutan's official line
  (not sourced this pass) is that many were illegal immigrants or left voluntarily. ⚠️ The document
  records both sides only through the refugee-favorable sources on Wikipedia.
- **The camps.** **Seven camps in Nepal's Jhapa and Morang districts** (main ones Beldangi, Sanischare, Timai,
  Goldhap, Khudunabari). In **January 2010, about 85,544** refugees lived in Nepal's camps. **Resettlement
  began in 2007**; by 2019 about **112,800** had been resettled abroad, **about 85% to the United States**,
  the rest to Australia, Canada, Norway, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Denmark. By **2016 only 11,762**
  remained, in two camps (Beldangi and Sanischare). **Repatriation talks between Bhutan and Nepal have failed
  repeatedly since 2000.** As of 2021 far more Lhotshampa live in the resettlement countries than in Nepal.
- **Why this matters for the map.** (a) It shows that **Bhutan's map-relevant Nepali-speaking population is
  now smaller than its 1988 self**, and the exodus is the reason the 2017 counts are hard to use as a guide to
  the south's 1985 composition. (b) It is the **clearest documented case in this region of a first-effective-
  settlement culture being removed by the state that had not settled the land.** (c) **The refugees
  transited India and settled in Nepal's Jhapa and Morang - the Koshi Terai - directly across the
  Siliguri Corridor from where they left** (⚠️ route inferred from geography, not sourced), which links the
  Bhutanese, Nepali, Sikkimese and Darjeeling stories. (d) Any post-collapse Bhutan in which the monarchy or
  the Drukpa state loses control could see the south reassert itself; ⚠️ this is speculation, not a claim.

### 8.5 Religion, monarchy and the dual system

- **Buddhism** entered in the 7th century under Songtsen Gampo (per the Wikipedia timeline); **Drukpa Kagyu**
  became dominant by the 16th century and is the state school. The Zhabdrung system split power between the
  **Druk Desi** and the **Je Khenpo**; the Zhabdrung's death was concealed for 54 years, until 1705. The
  **Je Khenpo** remains the head of the state religion under the dual-system logic.
- **Hereditary monarchy from 1907**, when **Ugyen Wangchuck** became the first Druk Gyalpo. **1910** Treaty of
  Punakha with Britain (subsidy doubled to 100,000 rupees); **1949** Treaty of Friendship with India
  (subsidy 500,000 rupees); **1971** UN membership; **1972** Jigme Singye Wangchuck (the fourth king) took the
  throne at 16; **2005** draft constitution; **2006** the fourth king abdicated; **2008** Jigme Khesar Namgyel
  Wangchuck was crowned and the first elections were held.
- **The 2008 Constitution (18 July 2008):** a "democratic constitutional monarchy" resting on Buddhist
  philosophy; Article 3 makes Buddhism "the spiritual heritage of Bhutan"; **the King retires at 65** and can
  be removed by a two-thirds parliamentary vote; **60% forest cover forever**; the Je Khenpo and monastic
  institutions barred from politics; bicameral parliament (**National Council 25, National Assembly 47**);
  naturalization after 15 years of residence with fluency in Dzongkha. Dzongkha is the national language.
- **Politics now.** Prime Minister **Tshering Tobgay** (PDP victory in the 2024 election - the Wikipedia
  extract said only that a PDP win was "mentioned in context"). Bhutan was **delisted as a least developed
  country on 13 December 2023**. Prime Minister Modi received the **Order of the Dragon King in 2024**, the
  first non-Bhutanese so honored.
- **Gross National Happiness (GNH).** The concept dates to the fourth king (1972); **four pillars**:
  sustainable development, cultural preservation, environmental conservation, good governance; a GNH index
  measures it. **This survey's reading (⚠️ inference): GNH is the state ideology of Ngalop-Drukpa cultural
  preservation, and the same state applied Driglam Namzha and the 1985 Citizenship Act in its name.** The
  "cultural preservation" pillar and the 1988 census belong to the same decade.

### 8.6 Economy: hydropower, India, the peg, the exodus of the young

- **Hydropower.** Installed capacity **1,614 MW** on the Economy page (older), but a 2025 report says
  **Punatsangchhu-II (1,020 MW) was completed and Bhutan's capacity rose 40% to over 3,500 MW.** ⚠️ Two
  figures from different dates; treat 3,500 MW as the current level pending a check. Plants: Chukha 336 MW
  (1986-88), Kurichhu 60 MW (2001-02), **Tala 1,020 MW (2007)**, Mangdechhu 720 MW (2019), Punatsangchhu-I
  (1,200 MW, under construction 2025), Kholongchhu (600 MW, under construction). **India committed in 2007 to
  import at least 10,000 MW from Bhutan** (target date 2021 in the source; not met).
- **Weight in the economy (⚠️ conflicting):** hydropower about **63% of exports**, **more than 17% of GDP**
  in one source and about **12.8%** in another, **at least 24% of direct government revenue**, and about
  **70% of generation exported to India**. Nominal GDP is about US$3.86 billion (2026 projection; Economy
  page) with GDP per capita about US$4,867.
- **India dependence:** **India takes 98% of Bhutan's exports and supplies 90% of imports** (India-Bhutan page)
  or **87.6% and 74.9% (2022)** (Economy page) - ⚠️ a real discrepancy between two pages of the same
  encyclopedia. Indian aid was **US$985 million in 2015-16**, Bhutan the largest beneficiary of Indian aid
  (dated). The **ngultrum is pegged to the Indian rupee** at parity (background knowledge - the fetched
  pages did not confirm it; ⚠️ verify). The **Indian Military Training Team (IMTRAT) of about 2,000** sits in
  western Bhutan. The **2007 treaty**, in force from **1 January 2011**, removed India's guidance role in
  Bhutan's foreign affairs.
- **Counter-insurgency precedent:** in 2003-04 the Royal Bhutan Army cleared ULFA bases from Bhutanese
  territory (Operation All Clear, India-Bhutan page). That is the region's precedent for Bhutan acting against
  a Northeast Indian insurgency - relevant to the breakaway landmass.
- **Out-migration.** Over **65,000 Bhutanese abroad (about 9%)**; **over 25,000 in Australia** (World Bank,
  October 2025); **13,211 students enrolled in Australian institutions as of July 2024**, close to 1 in 50
  Bhutanese citizens; Prime Minister Tobgay has called the outflow of working-age people an **"existential
  threat"** given the falling birth rate. Gelephu Mindfulness City is partly a response. **This is the
  Bhutanese analogue of Central Asia's labor export**, but going outward to Australia rather than to a
  neighbor.
- **Tourism.** Sustainable Development Fee of US$100 per person per day (Economy page; regional nationals
  exempted), **"high value, low impact"** since 2008.

### 8.7 The Chinese border and Doklam

- **No diplomatic relations** between Bhutan and China. **Border talks began in 1984**; the Wikipedia page
  names four disputed areas: **Doklam (89 km2), Sinchulumpa and Gieu (about 180 km2)**, and two more along
  the ridges from Gamochen to Batangla and Amo Chhu. **China claims areas totaling 1,504 km2.** A **1996
  exchange proposal** - Pasamlung and Jakarlung in the north for Sinchulumpa, Dramana and Shakhatoe in the
  west - was accepted by Bhutan in principle. **25 rounds** of boundary talks have occurred (25th round
  joint press release on the Bhutan MFA site); an **MoU on a three-step roadmap** was signed **14 October
  2021**; an Expert Group Meeting in Beijing was described as held "after a gap of two years" (⚠️ year not
  pinned). The border is **477 km** (BhutanWiki) and **no final agreement has been reached** as of the
  latest search.
- **The 2017 standoff:** on **29 June 2017** Bhutan protested Chinese road construction in Doklam; on
  **30 June** India and China each put about 3,000 troops on the ground; Bhutan rejected Beijing's claims on
  10 August 2017. (The standoff lasted about 73 days per general knowledge, not fetched.) **Satellite imagery
  analyses show China built at least 22 settlements inside disputed territory by 2024.**
- **Why it is a Siliguri fact.** Doklam sits at the Bhutan-China-Sikkim area; the Siliguri Corridor sits
  below it. The corridor survey records this as the reason India treats the Siliguri line as its strongest
  defensive position. See Section 9.

### 9.2 The pinned Siliguri line and Nepal's east

  project does not research.**
- **Nepal's eastern land route to the sea crosses the breakaway landmass.** The Kakarbhitta (Jhapa) border
  post opens on the Siliguri area, and the road toward Bangladesh's Banglabandha-Panchagarh crossing lies

- **The corridor's 20-22 km narrowest span** (STATUS tracker, quoting the Siliguri Corridor page) is measured
  from Nepal to Bangladesh - both of this region's own members. **Nepal and Bangladesh face each other across
  the Northeast landmass's mouth.** That is the geometry behind their transit ambitions.

### 9.3 Sikkim, Darjeeling and Kalimpong - the Nepali-speaking ring (seam only)

- **Kingdom of Sikkim (1642-1975).** Invaded by both Gorkha Nepal and Bhutan in the 18th century (Wikipedia
  says it was "under both the Gorkha and the Bhutanese rule for more than 40 years" - ⚠️ dated vaguely).
  **Treaty of Titalia, 1817:** the British settled the Sikkim-Nepal territorial dispute (Wikipedia: Nepal ceded Sikkim to British India). **Darjeeling was ceded to
  the British in 1835.** A large Nepali migration into Sikkim followed (the Kingdom page cites about 180,000
  Nepalis 1775-1815 - ⚠️ a suspiciously large early figure for so small a time and place, left unverified),
  and **allegations of discrimination against Nepali Hindus fed resentment against the Chogyal** before the
  1975 merger (STATUS tracker: 200,000 people in 1975 under 20,000-40,000 Indian troops). The **South Asia
  survey already lists Sikkim as a restoration case** and Darjeeling/Kalimpong as a **Gorkhaland** question.
- **The Nepali-speaking ring is a single cultural fact in four places:** Nepal, southern Bhutan (the
  Lhotshampa), Sikkim (Nepali-speaking majority; ⚠️ the commonly cited figure of about 62% was not
  re-verified this pass and belongs to the Northeast pass), and the Darjeeling/Kalimpong hills (Gorkhaland;
  a 1909 petition, the 2012 Gorkhaland Territorial Administration, per the South Asia survey). Bhutan's foreign
  minister named the Sikkim/Darjeeling precedent in 1992 as the thing Bhutan feared.
- **Other Nepal-linked communities in the same belt** (Limbu, Rai in Sikkim and Darjeeling; Tamang, Gurung;
  and the Lepcha, an older non-Nepali people of Sikkim, Darjeeling and Samtse) belong to the Northeast pass.
- **Darjeeling as a Nepali-lost territory:** Nepal held the Darjeeling-Siliguri area before the 1816-17
  settlements. Any "Greater Nepal" irredentism is background knowledge, **not** researched this pass, and no
  government of Nepal claims Darjeeling. ⚠️ Recorded so it is not lost, not as a live claim.
- **Bhutan's lost Duars.** The **1864-65 Duar War** and **Treaty of Sinchula (1865)** cost Bhutan the **Assam
  Duars, Bengal Duars and Dewangiri**, with Bhutan getting a **50,000 rupee** annual subsidy in return.
  **Those Duars lie in Alipurduar/Jalpaiguri and lowland Assam - inside the pinned breakaway landmass.**
  Kalimpong was also part of that story (background knowledge, not fetched; the Wikipedia extract lists
  only the three items). ⚠️ **Inference:** Bhutan has never publicly raised this loss in the sources read, and
  this is a dormant historical seam that a post-collapse Bhutan (or a successor Northeast state) could use.
  **The author decides whether it is worth a note.**

### 9.4 Bangladesh (sibling in the region)

- **Power:** Nepal exports **40 MW (rising to 60 MW)** to Bangladesh via Indian territory in the June-to-
  November season - a real, small, first-of-its-kind trade flow whose route runs through the rest of India.
- **Transit and location:** Nepal and Bangladesh face each other across the corridor mouth (Section 9.2).
- **Bhutan-Bangladesh** trade routes (background, not researched) run through the breakaway landmass.
- **Refugee mirror:** the 1990s Bhutanese refugees crossed India to reach Nepal; the Rohingya mirror belongs to
  the Bangladesh pass.

### 9.5 Bhutan's border with the breakaway landmass

Bhutan borders **Sikkim** on the west, **West Bengal** (the Kalimpong, Alipurduar and Jalpaiguri area) on the
south-west, **Assam** along the south, and **Arunachal Pradesh** on the east - **all inside the breakaway
landmass** (⚠️ geography from general knowledge, not fetched this pass; verify against the author's map).
The two rail links agreed on **29 September 2025** for **Rs 4,033 crore** (about 89 km total) are **Kokrajhar-
Gelephu (69 km, Assam-Bhutan, six stations, about four years)** and **Banarhat-Samtse (20 km, West Bengal-
Bhutan, two stations, about three years)**; **both terminate in breakaway territory** and were to give Bhutan
"access to India's 150,000 km railway network." **The Northeast slice therefore becomes Bhutan's only
neighbor other than Tibet.** The successor states' identity (Assam splits, Sikkim's restoration, the Bodoland
and Kuki-Zo questions) is the parallel survey's business; the fact that Bhutan's border set is entirely inside
them is this survey's contribution.

## Section 10 - Where real ethnic geography breaks "whole first-order subdivisions only"

### 10.1 Nepal

The rule can draw Nepal as **seven provinces or fewer**, but it cannot draw the identity claims without
splitting provinces:

| Claim | What a whole-province rule does | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Madhesh as the whole Terai** | Madhesh Province is only eight central districts holding about 39% of Terai population. The rest of the Terai sits in Koshi (Jhapa, Morang, Sunsari), Lumbini (Rupandehi, Kapilvastu, Banke, Bardiya, Parasi) and Sudurpashchim (Kailali, Kanchanpur) and Bagmati (Chitwan) | Cannot be drawn without splitting **Koshi, Lumbini, Sudurpashchim** (and Bagmati or Gandaki for the Inner Terai) |
| **Tharuhat / Tharuwan** | Straddles Lumbini (Bardiya, Banke, Dang) and Sudurpashchim (Kailali, Kanchanpur) | Splits two provinces |
| **Limbuwan / Khumbuwan / Kirat** | Sits inside Koshi's hills; Koshi also contains Jhapa, Morang and Sunsari, which are mixed Terai | Splits Koshi |
| **Newa / Tamsaling** | Inside Bagmati | Splits Bagmati |
| **Khas-Arya far west (Karnali plus hills of Sudurpashchim)** | Fits Karnali plus a Sudurpashchim hills group; Sudurpashchim's Terai does not | Splits Sudurpashchim |

**So Nepal as a single polity is drawable; every internal separatist claim breaks the rule.** ⚠️ The Terai
identity is the largest such break.

**District-level evidence** ranks the largest Tharu concentrations (Bardiya 53.3%, Kailali 41.9%, Dang 29.7%,
Kanchanpur 25.8%) and the Madhesh eight; the rule could be relaxed to **districts** for Nepal if the author
wants a Terai split. That would be a new project rule (whole first-order subdivisions plus, for Nepal only,
whole districts), the same kind of design decision the Central Asia survey flagged for Fergana.

### 10.2 Bhutan

The rule fits Bhutan cleanly: twenty dzongkhags, and the ethnic map largely follows dzongkhag lines
(Ngalop west, Sharchop east, Nepali-speaking south in Samtse, Sarpang and Tsirang). **The break is that the
south is not uniform** (Chhukha and Dagana are mixed) and that **Gelephu Mindfulness City, at 2,500 km2, is
declared as a Special Administrative Region inside a dzongkhag** whose own listed area is 1,946 km2. If the
south secedes or reasserts, the line follows whole dzongkhags (Samtse, Sarpang, Tsirang, perhaps Dagana and
Chhukha). ⚠️ No source found says anything about such a claim today; the Lhotshampa movement was for
citizenship and return, not for secession, in the sources read.

**A scale fact:** Bhutan is 38,395 km2 and about 0.78 million people; the project's target is "roughly ten to
twelve polities" for a region, and **Bhutan cannot be a polity on the scale of Kazakhstan or the Sinian
Federation.** Whether it stands alone, joins another state, or is drawn as a buffer sphere is the decision
that matters.

## Section 11 - The options menu (author disposes)

✅ **SETTLED 2026-09-29 (author): Nepal and Bhutan both stay intact countries, exactly at their current borders.** Nepal: option 1, unchanged (options 2-5 ruled out; the Madhesh line and the other internal claims — Tharuhat, Limbuwan — are carried as internal tensions, not partitions). None of these is recommended; each is a fork with its main cost.

**Nepal**
1. **One Nepal, as it is.** Cost: leaves the Madhesh line, the best-documented fracture, inside; no rule violation.
2. **Terai separated from the hills.** Only possible by splitting Koshi, Lumbini and Sudurpashchim (Madhesh
   Province is only 39% of Terai population). Cost: breaks the absolute rule.
3. **Madhesh Province as its own polity** (8 districts, 6.11 million, 9,661 km2, 633 per km2). Cost: a landlocked
   lowland with Bihar along its whole south; leaves the other Terai districts with the hills. Benefit: a
   whole-province drawing that matches the strongest movement.
4. **Split along existing province groupings** (east, center, west). Cost: none of the groupings matches the
   three-belt or ethnic logic.
5. **Allow whole districts for Nepal only.** Cost: a new project rule.

**Bhutan**
6. **Stand-alone microstate of twenty dzongkhags.** Cost: far smaller than any other polity in the atlas and
   dependent on a customer that may be cut off from it.
7. **Join a Himalayan Buddhist sphere** (Sikkim or Tibet). Cost: Bhutan defines itself against Tibet.
8. **Detach the Nepali-speaking south** (Samtse, Sarpang, Tsirang, perhaps Dagana and Chhukha; about 31% of
   the population by the arithmetic above). Cost: no source shows secession as a live claim.

**Spheres**
9. **Add sphere layers** for the Nepali-speaking ring, the Kirat and the Himalayan Buddhist rim (Section 1.3).
   Cost: the ring crosses four polities and the sphere CSVs were built for larger bodies.
