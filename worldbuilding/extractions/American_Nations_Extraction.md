# American Nations — Distilled Extraction

**Source:** *American Nations: A History of the Eleven Rival Regional Cultures of North America*, Colin
Woodard (Viking/Penguin, 2011). Source PDF: `worldbuilding/source-books/American Nations-- A History
of the Eleven Rival Regional Cultures of North America - Colin Woodard [2011].pdf`.

**Provenance note:** this file is a comprehensive distillation written in original wording, not a reproduction
of Woodard's text — consistent with this project's own established practice for every other mined source (the
Enneagram material, the 21+ craft-theory books). No passage here is copied or lightly reworded from the book;
everything is restated from an actual read-through of the source, organized for practical use rather than
reading order.

**Purpose:** this is the analytical basis the author used to design the post-apocalyptic "New North America"
successor nations for the Tepenian-universe novels (Midwestland, Cascadia, Appalachia, the CSA, New England,
etc. — see `worldbuilding/nations/World-Nations-Roster.md`).
It exists here both to document the reasoning behind those already-drawn nations and to serve as a repeatable
method for designing the post-war nations of Russia, China, Europe, Oceania, and Latin America, none of which
Woodard's own book covers.

## Part One: The Eleven Regional Cultures

Woodard's core claim: North America was never one culture, or even three (US/Canada/Mexico) — it was settled
piecemeal by distinct population groups, each of whom planted a durable cultural template that has persisted,
spread, and clashed with its neighbors for four centuries, largely independent of the state/provincial borders
later drawn across the continent. Each profile below covers: who founded it and when, its core values and
political instincts, how and where it physically spread, its characteristic economy, and its historical
conflicts and alliances.

### El Norte

**Founding population:** Spanish colonists, soldiers, and Franciscan missionaries, from the late 1500s onward
(the oldest of the eleven nations) — Santa Fe was founded in 1610. Unlike the Atlantic-coast nations, El Norte
was never well-supplied or well-populated by its parent empire; it was a chronically neglected, isolated
frontier of a distant, declining Spanish colonial administration, reachable only by resupply caravans that
sometimes took years to arrive.

**Core values:** shaped almost entirely by extreme remoteness and neglect rather than by any deliberate
colonization plan. Authority was patriarchal and hierarchical (the *patrón* system — personal loyalty owed to
a local elite figure who in turn owed protection and provision to his dependents), but day-to-day isolation
also bred real self-sufficiency, adaptability, and a tolerance for racial mixing unusual for the era (Spanish
soldiers took Indigenous wives in large numbers; a mixed-race, *mestizo* population became the demographic
majority early on). A culture of independence and resistance to central authority runs alongside the
patriarchal formal structure — El Norte repeatedly tried to secede from more centralized rule, from colonial
Mexico City's own oversight through several 19th-century independence schemes.

**Geographic extent/spread:** a strip roughly 100+ miles on either side of the modern US–Mexico border, from
south/west Texas through southern California, spreading from Spanish mission and presidio settlements
(Monterrey, Santa Fe, San Antonio, Tucson, San Diego, San Francisco).

**Economy:** originally mission-and-ranching based; the open-range cattle industry (and the vaquero/cowboy
culture, including the vocabulary — lasso, rodeo, bronco, ranch — later adopted continent-wide) originated
here, transplanted from Spain's own arid-country ranching techniques.

**Conflicts/alliances:** historically caught between larger powers rather than a major aggressor itself —
squeezed by Anglo-American expansion from the north and east, and by central Mexican authority from the south.
Its own people have often felt more kinship across the modern US–Mexico border than with either Washington or
Mexico City.

### New France

**Founding population:** French colonists from the early 1600s, founded on two competing visions held
simultaneously by its two founders — one a conventional feudal-aristocratic transplant, the other (the more
influential, lasting one) a genuine partnership-and-intermarriage model with the Indigenous nations of the
region, treating them as equals rather than subjects or obstacles.

**Core values:** down-to-earth, egalitarian, consensus-driven, and — critically — genuinely hybridized with
Indigenous culture rather than merely coexisting alongside it; a large mixed-descent population (*métis*)
emerged from intermarriage, and French settlers who fled the colony's attempted feudal control system
("coureurs de bois," woodsmen who took up an Indigenous-adjacent lifestyle) became a real demographic and
cultural force in their own right. New France is Woodard's most liberal-leaning nation by later polling, and
the most overtly nationalistic (it holds a genuine nation-state-in-waiting in Québec).

**Geographic extent/spread:** the lower St. Lawrence Valley (Québec), the Maritime provinces (Acadia), and —
via a long fur-trade and river-based migration route — the Acadian/Cajun enclaves of southern Louisiana.

**Economy:** originally fur trade, requiring genuine cooperative relationships with Indigenous trading
partners rather than conquest or plantation agriculture.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the attempted imposition of rigid French feudal control (under Louis XIV) largely
failed against the pull of Indigenous-adjacent frontier life. Historically an underdog culture within the
British Empire and then within Canada, its influence flows into modern Canadian federal multiculturalism and
negotiated-consensus governance more broadly.

### Tidewater

**Founding population:** English colonists at Jamestown (1607) — initially a chaotic, poorly planned corporate
military outpost, not a farming colony, that only found its footing once tobacco cultivation took hold. Its
lasting character was set by a second wave: "Cavalier" Royalist gentry (and their descendants) who fled
England during/after the English Civil War, explicitly intending to reproduce semi-feudal English manor
society in the New World, complete with an indentured-servant-then-slave underclass.

**Core values:** hierarchical, deeply conservative, oriented around inherited authority and "libertas" (liberty
as a privilege belonging to the few, not a universal right) rather than the Germanic/Anglo-Saxon "freedom as
birthright" tradition that shaped Yankeedom and the Midlands. Extremely status-conscious; personal honor was
defended violently; political power was hereditary and concentrated in a closely intermarried planter
aristocracy.

**Geographic extent/spread:** the lowlands of Virginia, Maryland, southern Delaware, and northeastern North
Carolina — geographically hemmed in by the Appalachian Mountains, which blocked its westward expansion and
was a major factor in its later relative decline.

**Economy:** tobacco plantation agriculture, initially worked by indentured servants (English poor, roughly
80-90% of 17th-century immigrants), transitioning to race-based chattel slavery after the 1670s as the supply
of willing indentured labor dried up.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the natural ideological and political rival of Yankeedom from the moment both were
founded (they backed opposite sides in the English Civil War, and this rivalry runs continuously through
Revolutionary-era politics, the Civil War, and into the present). Over time, largely subordinated itself to and
allied with the more powerful, more radical Deep South.

### Yankeedom

**Founding population:** Puritan Calvinists at Massachusetts Bay (1620s-1630s), arriving in complete, stable,
educated, middle-class family units — a demographic profile unlike any other colony's, which let the
population grow rapidly through natural increase rather than needing continuous fresh immigration.

**Core values:** a "city on a hill" religious-utopian mission with a deep, still-persisting faith that
government can and should be used deliberately to improve society and enforce the common good, even at the
cost of individual self-denial. Extremely high value on education (public schools were mandatory from the
colony's earliest years, unique among the colonies) and on genuine local self-government (the New England town
meeting, where every resident has direct say, rather than representative-only democracy). Also historically
prone to a missionary, assimilationist certainty about its own rightness — a conviction that its own values
were the correct ones for everyone else to adopt too, which is a major source of friction with its neighbors.

**Geographic extent/spread:** from its Massachusetts Bay core, spread west and north across upstate New York,
the northern strips of Pennsylvania/Ohio/Indiana/Illinois/Iowa, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, and the
Canadian Maritimes — later also colonizing coastal Oregon/Washington (see Left Coast) via missionary and
maritime trade networks.

**Economy:** originally mixed small-farm/craft/mercantile; later the historical center of American
industrialization, finance, and intellectual/educational institutions.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the other historical superpower alongside the Deep South; its natural, durable allies
are the Left Coast and (in the 20th century onward) New Netherland — together forming the "Northern
alliance"/"Blue" coalition described in Part Three below. Its natural rival is the Deep South.

### New Netherland

**Founding population:** Dutch colonists (1624), governed initially by the Dutch West India Company as a
purely commercial trading-post venture, not a religious or utopian project.

**Core values:** commercial, materialistic, pluralistic, and tolerant — not out of idealism but out of hard
Dutch commercial self-interest (intolerance was bad for trade). Extraordinarily diverse from its very founding
— multiple European ethnicities, the first Jewish community in English/Dutch North America, a large African
population (both enslaved and free) — with no single ethnic or religious group ever fully dominant. This
tolerance-for-trade's-sake model is the direct ancestor of the American Bill of Rights' religious-freedom and
free-inquiry provisions.

**Geographic extent/spread:** never expanded much beyond Greater New York City and the surrounding area (the
five boroughs, lower Hudson Valley, northern New Jersey, western Long Island, southwestern Connecticut) — a
dense urban core rather than a spreading agricultural culture.

**Economy:** global commercial trade and finance from its very founding; the natural heir to Amsterdam's role
as a world financial capital.

**Conflicts/alliances:** historically opportunistic/independent, swinging its political alignment based on
immediate self-interest (allied with the Deep South against tariffs in the 19th century; allied firmly with
Yankeedom and the Left Coast by the 20th century once its urban-governance needs converged with the Northern
alliance's own priorities).

### The Midlands

**Founding population:** English Quakers under William Penn (1680s), followed immediately by a massive wave of
German-speaking peasant/craftsman immigrants (Palatinate refugees) — Pennsylvania was, uniquely among the
English colonies, majority non-English from very early on.

**Core values:** pluralistic, tolerant, middle-class, and deeply skeptical of both aristocratic privilege and
centralized government power — but for different reasons than Yankeedom's version of anti-authoritarianism: the
Midlands' skepticism of government stems from a desire to be left alone, not from a mission to remake
government into an active force for good. Politically moderate to the point of apathy; historically the
continent's swing-vote "kingmaker" nation precisely because it never fully aligns with either of the two
superpower cultures. Quaker pacifism proved to be a genuine governance liability (the colony's government
repeatedly failed to defend itself or its frontier), which opened the door for a large-scale Scots-Irish/German
population influx that eventually broke Quaker political control entirely.

**Geographic extent/spread:** from its Philadelphia/Delaware Bay hearth, spread through central Ohio, Indiana,
Illinois, northern Missouri, most of Iowa, and the more fertile eastern halves of the Dakotas/Nebraska/Kansas;
also a major extension into southern Ontario (forming the demographic core of English-speaking Canada).

**Economy:** small-scale, prosperous family farming (German-Palatinate settlers were renowned for
soil-conservation and selective-breeding skill) and craft trades.

**Conflicts/alliances:** shares a strong moral objection to slavery with Yankeedom (the first formal
anti-slavery protest in North America was issued by Germantown Quakers in 1712) — this shared position pulled
the Midlands toward the Northern side in the Civil War era, but its own instinct is toward moderation and
compromise rather than firm alliance with either superpower bloc.

### Greater Appalachia

**Founding population:** the last of the colonial-era nations to form — "Borderlander" immigrants from the
war-ravaged borderlands of lowland Scotland, northern England, and Scots-Irish Ulster, arriving in five
increasingly large waves between 1717-1776, driven out by economic collapse and rack-renting at home rather
than recruited by any colonial project. Unlike every other nation on this list, Appalachia was not founded as
a deliberate colonization effort — it was an unplanned mass movement of refugees who moved directly to the
frontier, effectively founding a stateless society beyond the reach of any colonial government.

**Core values:** a clan-based warrior culture forged by centuries of border warfare — fierce personal and
family honor, deep suspicion of all outside authority (aristocratic, religious, or governmental), a strong
individual-liberty/personal-sovereignty ethic, and a preference for self-administered justice (blood feuds,
vigilante "Regulator" movements) over formal courts, which were often days away and controlled by rival
nations' elites. Economically indifferent to wealth accumulation in the conventional sense — prioritized
personal freedom and mobility over fixed property (portable wealth: livestock, and whiskey as a de facto
currency).

**Geographic extent/spread:** spread explosively along the Great Wagon Road down the spine of the Appalachian
Mountains, through the Carolina and Georgia backcountry, and later across the southern tiers of Ohio, Indiana,
Illinois; the Ozarks; eastern Oklahoma; and the Texas Hill Country — physically cutting Tidewater and the Deep
South off from unimpeded westward expansion.

**Economy:** subsistence hunting, herding, and small-scale slash-and-burn farming; a "portable wealth" economy
(livestock and whiskey) rather than fixed-property accumulation.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the single most historically volatile and cross-cutting nation on the list — capable of
allying with or fighting against every one of its neighbors depending on the specific issue. Fought violent
insurgencies against both Midland Quaker governance (the Paxton Boys' march on Philadelphia) and Tidewater/Deep
Southern elite rule (the Regulator movements in the Carolinas) in the colonial period. Later became the crucial,
genuinely contested "swing" partner in the Deep South's post-Reconstruction "Dixie bloc" coalition — its own
populist, anti-aristocratic instincts sit in real tension with the Deep South oligarchy it's nonetheless
often allied with.

### The Deep South

**Founding population:** transplanted colonists from Barbados (1670s) — not settlers straight from Europe, but
the sons and grandsons of an already-established, extraordinarily brutal West Indian slave-plantation
aristocracy, bringing a fully-formed slave-society model with them rather than developing one gradually.

**Core values:** an explicit, caste-based (not merely class-based) hierarchy built on radical concentration of
wealth and power in a tiny planter oligarchy, maintained by state-sponsored terror against an enslaved
supermajority. The least democratic of the eleven nations historically — voting rights, political
participation, and social mobility were deliberately and severely restricted, not just for the enslaved
population but for the poor white majority as well. Politically and socially the most consistently
authoritarian, aristocratic-nostalgic culture on the continent; embraced "classical republicanism" (a
Greek/Roman model where liberty is a privilege of a ruling minority, not a universal right) rather than the
Anglo-Saxon "freedom as birthright" tradition.

**Geographic extent/spread:** from its Charleston beachhead, spread along the coastal lowlands through most of
South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Florida, Louisiana, western Tennessee, and the southeastern
portions of North Carolina, Arkansas, and Texas — an aggressively expansionist culture, checked geographically
mainly by Greater Appalachia's own settlement of the highland interior.

**Economy:** large-scale export-plantation agriculture (rice, indigo, later cotton and sugar) worked by an
enslaved population that in some areas outnumbered the free population nine to one.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the other historical superpower alongside Yankeedom; the primary architect and driving
force behind the American Civil War. Its natural junior allies are Tidewater (a declining, culturally similar
but historically less radical partner) and — with much more difficulty and internal tension — Greater
Appalachia, forming the "Dixie bloc"/"Red" coalition described in Part Three.

### The Left Coast

**Founding population:** a 19th-century hybrid — Yankee missionaries, merchants, and intellectuals arriving by
sea intending to found "a second New England on the Pacific," combined with Appalachian/Borderlander farmers
and frontiersmen arriving overland by wagon and settling the surrounding countryside while Yankees controlled
the towns and civic institutions.

**Core values:** a genuine fusion — Yankee faith in active government, social reform, and the "common good,"
combined with a more individualistic, self-exploration-oriented, experimental sensibility that Yankeedom itself
never fully embraced. Explicitly idealistic and reformist (this is the historical cradle of the American
environmental movement, and later the countercultural and tech-utopian movements).

**Geographic extent/spread:** a narrow coastal strip from Monterey, California, to Juneau, Alaska — hemmed in
by the Coast/Cascade mountain ranges, which sharply separates it culturally from the arid Far West interior
just inland.

**Economy:** originally maritime trade, missionary/educational institution-building, and (once gold was
discovered) a self-conscious "civilizing" reaction against an unruly Gold Rush population; later the historical
center of the environmental movement and, in the modern era, the global technology industry.

**Conflicts/alliances:** the closest, most reliable ally of Yankeedom of any of the eleven nations — Woodard
calls this the most durable coalition on the continent. Its natural rival/opposite is the Far West, which sits
immediately inland across the same mountain range.

### The Far West

**Founding population:** unlike every other nation, the Far West was not founded by any single ethnocultural
settler group — its founding population is corporate and institutional. Two small, genuinely independent
settler waves arrived first (Yankee-descended Mormons in Utah, and individualistic Appalachian-style "Forty-
niner" gold miners in California/Nevada), but neither achieved lasting cultural dominance; the region's actual
colonization was directed almost entirely by outside capital — railroads, mining corporations, and the federal
government — because its extreme aridity made traditional farming settlement impossible without massive
industrial infrastructure.

**Core values:** a persistent sense of colonial dependency and resentment toward the distant capital/corporate
interests (and federal government) that actually control the region's economy, combined — somewhat
contradictorily — with a reflexive hostility toward the same federal government whose subsidies the region
has always depended on. Politically this produces a libertarian-corporatist alignment: anti-regulation
rhetoric that coexists with reliance on federal/corporate largesse.

**Geographic extent/spread:** the entire arid interior west of roughly the 100th meridian — the high plains,
the Great Basin, the Rocky Mountain interior, and the interior portions of the Pacific Northwest and
California — an area defined entirely by environmental/climatic boundaries rather than any settler group's
own cultural boundary.

**Economy:** railroad-and-mining-corporation-directed extraction economy; historically an "internal colony"
of the wealthier coastal nations, dependent on outside capital for irrigation, transport, and infrastructure.

**Conflicts/alliances:** its economic libertarianism and hostility to federal regulation align it with the Deep
South/Dixie bloc on most modern political questions, even though it shares no cultural-founding lineage with
the South at all — a case where shared present-day economic interest overrides any original settler-culture
kinship.

### First Nation

**Founding population:** not a settler nation at all — the Indigenous peoples of the far northern boreal
forest, tundra, and glacier regions, who never lost control of their own territory in the way other Indigenous
nations elsewhere on the continent did, and who have in the modern era been actively reclaiming political
sovereignty and territorial control (autonomy in Alaska and Nunavut; near-full independence for Greenland from
Denmark).

**Core values:** distinct, still-living traditional cultural practices and knowledge systems adapted
specifically to survival in an extremely hostile climate that no colonizing culture successfully displaced.

**Geographic extent/spread:** rapidly expanding its effective control over what had been the northern fringes
of the Far West — much of the Yukon, the Northwest Territories, Labrador, all of Nunavut and Greenland, the
northern tiers of Ontario/Manitoba/Saskatchewan/Alberta, northwestern British Columbia, and northern
two-thirds of Québec.

**Conflicts/alliances:** Woodard frames this as simultaneously the oldest and the newest of the eleven
nations — ancient in origin, but only now re-emerging as an active political force after centuries of being
treated as scenery rather than an agent in the continent's history.

## Part Two: Woodard's Theory and Method

This is the section most directly reusable for continents Woodard's book doesn't cover.

### The core claim: "nation" ≠ "state"

A **state** is a sovereign political/administrative entity (a government, a set of borders, a seat at the
United Nations). A **nation** is a group of people who share, or believe they share, a common culture, ethnic
origin, language, historical experience, and set of symbols — entirely independent of political borders. Most
of the world's actual political conflict, Woodard argues, is better explained by looking at nations than at
states: a federated state (the US, Canada, Belgium, Switzerland, Malaysia) can contain several rival nations
that never fully merge; a nation can exist without ever holding its own state (the Kurds, the Québécois).

### The Doctrine of First Effective Settlement

The single most important mechanism in the whole book, credited to cultural geographer Wilbur Zelinsky
(1973): **whichever group is the first to establish a viable, self-perpetuating society in a given territory
sets that territory's lasting cultural character — disproportionately, regardless of how small that founding
group was, and regardless of how many later immigrants of other backgrounds arrive.** Later arrivals
overwhelmingly assimilate into the culture that's already there rather than replacing it or creating a genuine
blend. This is why, for example, a region can be culturally "Dutch" or "Puritan" centuries after the actual
Dutch or Puritan-descended population has become a small minority of current residents — the *institutions,
values, and social norms* those founders established are what persists, not the literal bloodline.

**Why this matters for worldbuilding:** a post-catastrophe nation's founding character isn't determined by
whoever ends up with the largest population eventually — it's determined by whoever first re-establishes
stable, self-perpetuating institutions and social norms in that specific territory, even if that founding group
was small. Later population influxes (refugees, migrants, other settlers) will tend to assimilate toward the
founders' existing norms rather than overwrite them, unless something disrupts that process entirely (total
depopulation and re-founding, forced cultural suppression, etc.).

### Nations persist through zones, not hard borders

Cultural geographers (and Woodard, following them) describe a nation's territory in three concentric layers:
a **core/nucleus** (where its power and identity are strongest and most unambiguous), a **domain** (a zone of
lesser but still clear influence), and a **sphere** (a wide zone of mild, sometimes-overlapping influence
shared with neighboring nations). These zones shift over time, and there are real historical examples of a
nation losing dominance over even its own core and effectively ceasing to exist as a distinct culture. Borders
between nations are not crisp lines — border regions (Woodard's own examples: Alsace-Lorraine, Istanbul, or
several specific American counties) are often under the genuine simultaneous influence of two or more nations
at once, and that ambiguity is itself realistic, not a flaw in the framework.

### Mobility reinforces rather than dissolves difference

A finding Woodard treats as counterintuitive but important: modern transportation and communication don't
homogenize a continent's regional cultures — they let people **sort themselves** more efficiently into
communities of people who already share their values, which measurably *increases* regional political and
cultural polarization over time rather than reducing it (he cites Bishop & Cushing's *The Big Sort* data on
Americans relocating into politically like-minded counties). Applied to worldbuilding: don't assume that
easier travel/communication in a setting will naturally erode regional cultural distinctness — it can just as
plausibly sharpen it, as people increasingly choose to live among others who already share their values.

### Nationhood is acquired, not inherited

Woodard is explicit that membership in one of his eleven nations is a matter of **culture, not genetics or
ancestry** — someone whose ancestors came from a totally different founding population can become a "real"
Midlander, Yankee, or Borderlander by growing up in and adopting that region's norms, exactly the way someone
can become fully Hungarian or French without any ancestral tie to those nations' founding populations. This
matters for worldbuilding because it means a fictional nation's population doesn't need ancestral purity or
even majority-descent from its founders to remain culturally coherent — assimilation into the founding
template is the actual mechanism, not bloodline.

### Deliberate scope limits, worth noting as a method choice

Woodard explicitly excludes several real regional cultures from his eleven (South Florida's Cuban-diaspora
culture, Hawaii's Polynesian culture, several distinct cultures within central Mexico and Central America, a
proposed African-American-diaspora "Creole nation" spanning the Caribbean/Brazil) — not because they don't
exist, but as a practical decision to stop the analysis at a level of resolution that still captures the
dominant, territory-defining cultural forces without infinitely subdividing ("peeling the onion," in his own
framing). **This is a legitimate and necessary method choice for any application of this framework** — the
goal is to capture the handful of cultures whose values actually dominate a given territory and drive its
politics, not to exhaustively catalog every enclave or minority culture within it.

## Part Three: How the Nations Actually Group — Alliance and Conflict Patterns

This is the part of the book most directly useful for modeling how a set of successor nations would actually
behave toward each other once drawn, not just how each one is individually characterized.

### The two historical superpowers

Since the nations' founding, **Yankeedom and the Deep South** have been the two most culturally self-aware,
wealthiest, and most consistently opposed of the eleven — every other nation has, at different points in
history, had to decide which of these two poles (or neither) to align with. Their opposition isn't a specific
policy dispute; it traces back to their founding values (communal moral mission and faith in active government,
versus hierarchical authority and resistance to external interference) and shows up consistently across
completely different eras and issues: the English Civil War (opposite sides), the Revolutionary and early
Republic period, the Civil War itself, and — per Woodard's own 20th/21st-century voting and legislative-record
analysis — nearly every major national political question into the present day.

### The "Northern alliance" / Blue coalition

**Yankeedom + Left Coast** is the single most durable bilateral alliance among the eleven nations, formed in
the 1840s and unbroken since. **New Netherland** joined this coalition later (once its own governance
needs — as a dense, diverse, commercially complex urban center requiring heavy public investment — converged
with Yankee priorities), despite having no shared founding-culture lineage with either partner; its earlier
19th-century alignment had actually been with the Deep South, on pure economic self-interest (opposition to
tariffs). This is a useful case study: **alliance can shift due to present-day structural self-interest, even
between nations with no historical/cultural kinship**, once material realities (New Netherland's own need for
strong, competent government) push a nation toward a different pole than its founding values alone would
suggest.

### The "Dixie bloc" / Red coalition

**Deep South + Tidewater** is a comparatively stable pairing (a declining aristocratic partner following the
lead of a more powerful, more radical one). **Greater Appalachia** is the crucial and genuinely unstable third
member — its own populist, anti-aristocratic, anti-authority instincts run directly counter to Deep Southern
oligarchic interests, and historically the two have been enemies as often as allies (opposite sides in both the
Revolution and the Civil War). The Deep South has held Appalachia in this coalition primarily through two
levers that have nothing to do with shared economic interest: **shared racial anxiety** (a fear campaign about
racial mixing/integration) and **shared religious tradition** (a socially conservative Protestant tradition
opposed to secularism, feminism, and modern scientific consensus). Woodard is explicit that this coalition
serves the Deep South's economic interests far more than Appalachia's own, and that Appalachia has repeatedly
produced its own populist and progressive political figures who complicate the alliance from within.

### The swing nations

**The Midlands** is the closest thing to a true swing/moderate nation — pluralistic and middle-class by
founding character, it tends to side with whichever coalition's specific position seems less extreme on a
given issue, rather than reliably aligning with either pole; its historical role is "kingmaker" precisely
because it doesn't have a fixed alliance.

**The Far West** aligns with the Dixie bloc on most modern political questions (a shared anti-federal-
government, anti-regulation political style) despite having **zero shared founding-culture lineage** with the
South — the alignment is driven entirely by a shared present-day economic/ideological interest (resistance to
outside regulation), not by any common origin. This is the clearest example in the whole book of alliance being
driven by current structural position rather than historical kinship.

### The general lesson for nation/alliance design

Alliances between regional cultures in this framework are driven by a mix of (1) genuinely shared founding
values, (2) shared present-day material/economic interest even absent shared origins, and (3) deliberately
cultivated fear or grievance campaigns that hold together a coalition whose members don't actually share
interests. All three mechanisms coexist and can pull a given nation in different directions on different
issues — a nation's "alliance" isn't a fixed, permanent fact, it's a live political contest with a real history
of shifting.

## Part Four: How to Apply This Method to Other Continents

Synthesis, not Woodard's own content — a practical checklist for extending this framework to Russia, China,
Europe, Oceania, and Latin America, none of which the source book covers.

1. **Identify the actual founding/first-effective-settlement population for each candidate region, not the
   current majority population.** Per the Doctrine of First Effective Settlement, the group that first
   established lasting, self-perpetuating institutions in a given territory is disproportionately what defines
   that territory's later cultural character — this could be a genuinely small founding population, and it
   will very often *not* be whichever group later became numerically dominant.
2. **Separate "when/how they arrived" from "who they are now."** Track founding population, timing, and route
   of arrival (by sea vs. overland, planned colonization vs. unplanned refugee flight, etc.) as the primary
   variable — later demographic change is expected and doesn't by itself overwrite the founding cultural
   template, per the same doctrine.
3. **Define each culture along the same core axes Woodard uses**, since these are the axes that actually
   predict later political/social behavior: attitude toward authority and hierarchy; individualism vs.
   collective/communal orientation; the specific *type* of religious or ideological worldview (not just
   "religious" or "secular," but what kind, and how it treats outsiders and dissent); attitude toward equality
   and democratic participation vs. deference to an established elite; and characteristic economic base (since
   economic structure and cultural values reinforce each other, as with the Deep South's slave-plantation
   economy and its caste system).
4. **Map geographic spread as driven by the founding population's own settlement logic**, not by uniform
   expansion — El Norte's isolation-driven remoteness, the Deep South's aggressive coastal-to-inland
   plantation expansion, and the Far West's externally-directed corporate colonization are three genuinely
   different spread *mechanisms*, not just different outcomes; pick the mechanism that fits the founding
   population's actual means and motive for expanding.
5. **Expect boundaries to be zones, not lines** — core, domain, and sphere, per the cultural-geography model
   above — and expect genuine border regions under simultaneous multi-nation influence, not clean partition.
6. **Build in at least one Far-West-style case**: a region whose eventual culture is defined by environmental/
   geographic hostility to normal settlement rather than by any one settler population's own values — this is
   a genuinely different design pattern from the other ten nations and worth deliberately including if the

7. **Design the alliance structure as a separate pass from the nation profiles themselves**, using the three

8. **Treat any Indigenous/surviving-pre-catastrophe population as its own nation on this same footing**, not as

   functional survival knowledge/institutions specific to its own territory is itself one of the nations in the
   framework, with its own trajectory (including the possibility of re-emerging political relevance long after
   being sidelined), not merely a demographic fact about someone else's nation.
