# Oceania — Coastal Cohesion and the Import Lifeline (survey)

**Stage 1 frameworks survey. Started 2026-09-26, first research pass only — not a finished analysis.**
Companion material-base survey not yet started (Stage 2). No map-data package exists yet (Stage 3). Hawaii is
the one part of Oceania already settled, as its own nation on the North America map — see
`nations/World-Nations-Roster.md`'s Oceania section. This survey covers **Australia, New Zealand, and the
Pacific Islands** — "Oceania beyond Hawaii," per the roster's own phrase for what's still open.

**Author-supplied working hypotheses (2026-09-26), the starting point for this research, not its conclusion:**
Australia is extremely unlikely to fracture into smaller countries, since its cohesion rests on the coastal
settlement pattern and a breakaway piece likely couldn't survive alone; Oceania's isolation means it likely
sees no significant direct attacks in the war; New Zealand being absorbed into Australia is possible but
unlikely, independence being the more likely outcome; a North/South Island split for New Zealand is also
possible but unlikely. The author's own bottom line: Australia and New Zealand are "largely unaffected...
infrastructurally," and the real design question is **supply lines** — how dependent Oceania is on imports
from the rest of the world.

---

## Headline findings

1. **Australia's cohesion hypothesis is well-supported, but the reason is settlement pattern, not lack of
   separatist sentiment.** 87% of Australians live within 50km of the coast, ~95% within 100km — a
   dramatically higher concentration than the ~39% world average. Real secessionist sentiment has existed
   (Western Australia's 1933 referendum passed 66% in favor of leaving the Commonwealth) and never fully
   died out, but it has never been about a coastal population that couldn't feed or fuel itself — it's been
   about resentment of the federal government's Depression-era economic policy. **The author's "couldn't
   survive alone" logic is a different, additional argument the historical secession movements never had to
   test** — worth being explicit that this survey is extending, not just confirming, the historical record.
2. **New Zealand's independence from Australia is a real, deliberate, 125-year-old choice, not an accident of
   geography.** NZ was invited into the 1901 Australian Federation and explicitly declined — Premier Richard
   Seddon wanted to lead an independent country, not become an Australian state; NZ's trade ran through the
   UK, not Australia; public sentiment favored staying separate. This is a strong precedent for staying
   independent post-collapse too.
3. **New Zealand's North/South Island tension is real and has an actual name — "South Island nationalism" —
   but has never come close to an actual split.** It traces to 1860s gold-rush-era prosperity in Otago/the
   West Coast colliding with the North Island's costly New Zealand Wars, and resentment that Wellington
   (chosen as capital in 1865 partly *to* head off a South Island breakaway) exploits southern resources
   without fair return. A minor political party existed as recently as 1997. Real grievance, but a genuine
   split has never had serious traction — consistent with the author's own "possible but unlikely" read.
4. **Australia's resource profile is sharply two-sided, and this is the survey's central finding.** Australia
   is food-secure and resource-rich in raw energy, but critically import-dependent on *refined* fuel and on
   pharmaceuticals — both immediately relevant to what a war-driven global supply collapse would actually
   break:
   - **Food: a major structural strength.** Self-sufficiency above 200% by FAOSTAT aggregates; a net food
     exporter selling 65-70% of farm production overseas; over 90% of fresh produce, meat, milk and eggs sold
     domestically is domestically produced. Australia would not go hungry on its own account.
   - **Energy, raw: a major structural strength.** World's largest or near-largest LNG exporter (~80 million
     tonnes/year, ~73% of gas production exported), among the largest coal exporters. Raw energy wealth is
     real.
   - **Fuel, refined: a severe, specific vulnerability.** 80-90% of refined-fuel consumption (petrol, diesel)
     is import-reliant — only two of Australia's four oil refineries remain open (BP Kwinana and ExxonMobil
     Altona both closed), and Australia exports 75% of its own crude rather than refining it domestically.
     Australia produces vast raw energy and still can't fuel its own transport network without imports —
     this is the sharpest, most quotable material fact in this survey.
   - **Pharmaceuticals: a second severe, specific vulnerability.** ~90% of medicines imported, mostly from
     China and India; "extremely limited and diminishing manufacturing capacity across all sectors of
     products apart from vaccine manufacture" (Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' own
     characterization: "dangerously dependent"). ~400 medicines already in real-world short supply, 30
     critical, as of the ordinary present day — before any war-driven shock.
5. **The Pacific Islands (excluding Australia/NZ) are a completely different resilience profile, and probably
   need their own separate treatment, not folding into an "Oceania is fine" conclusion.** Small Island
   Developing States are described in the real-world literature as facing "existential threats" from import
   dependency — 80% of food imported on Tuvalu's main island; narrow economies, high debt, little ability to
   absorb prolonged supply shocks (unlike Australia's diversified economy). **Do not generalize Australia's
   food/energy resilience onto the wider Pacific** — the two are not the same case at all.

## Section 1 — Australia: cohesion

### 1.1 The coastal settlement pattern

87% of Australians live within 50km of the coast (up 2% since 2001, over 22 million people); ~80% within
25km; ~95% within 100km, against a ~39% world average. The population is concentrated in two bands: the
south-east/east arc (Adelaide–Melbourne–Canberra–Sydney–Brisbane) and a smaller south-west pocket around
Perth. Tasmania is the most coastal-concentrated state (99% within 50km in 2001), followed by South Australia
and Western Australia (91% each), then Queensland (88%). This is the material basis for the author's "the
coastlines are what keeps it together" argument — if a state's interior is nearly empty, an interior-drawn
partition line has almost nothing to fight over, and the *coastal* cities of any would-be breakaway state
would still be linked to the rest of the continent by the same coastal infrastructure corridor.

### 1.2 The historical secession counter-evidence, and why it doesn't undermine the author's case

Western Australia voted 66% in favor of leaving the Commonwealth in a 1933 referendum — the closest any
Australian secession movement has come to succeeding, driven by Depression-era resentment of federal economic
policy (the Dominion League of Western Australia, founded 1930, led the campaign). It failed on constitutional
grounds: the Constitution's preamble declares the federation "indissoluble," and the British Parliament
refused WA's 1935 independence petition. Secessionism has recurred in WA's politics since shortly after 1901.

This is real desire to leave, not manufactured — so the author's "extremely unlikely to fracture" claim needs
the more specific framing the author themselves already reached for: not that no one would *want* to break
away, but that a post-collapse breakaway state would struggle to **survive** the attempt, given how much of
its own supply chain (see Section 3) runs through infrastructure and imports the rest of the continent
already shares. That's a materially different argument than 1933's, and this survey hasn't yet found
real-world precedent that tests it directly — flagged as the author's own reasoned extrapolation, not a
sourced fact.

## Section 2 — New Zealand: independence and internal tension

### 2.1 Why NZ never joined Australia, and why that argues for continued independence

Fiji and New Zealand were both originally part of the pre-Federation process (the Federal Council of
Australasia) and both opted out. NZ Premier Richard Seddon convened a Royal Commission in 1900 explicitly to
delay and gauge opinion; most submissions opposed union, though farmers worried about new trade barriers if
NZ stayed out. Seddon's own preference was to lead an independent nation rather than become a state within
someone else's federation. NZ's trade at the time ran through the UK, not Australia — the two were seen as
economic rivals more than partners. This is a genuine, considered, century-old choice for a separate national
identity, not a historical accident — a strong precedent for the author's "NZ stays independent" read.

### 2.2 South Island nationalism

A real, named movement ("South Island nationalism") with roots in the 1860s: Otago and West Coast gold rushes
brought southern prosperity just as the North Island was absorbing the costs of the New Zealand Wars,
producing a lasting grievance that Wellington-based government extracts southern resources without fair
return. Serious enough in 1865 that fear of a South Island breakaway was a real factor in relocating the
capital from Auckland to the more central Wellington that same year. Resurfaced as recently as the NZ South
Island Party (founded 1997, focused on fair GDP return and resource control) — but no iteration of this
movement has come close to an actual split. Matches the author's own "possible but unlikely" framing well:
there's a real fault line here (worth keeping as a *sphere*-style cultural/economic distinction the way this
project has handled Isan or Zomia, not necessarily a hard border), but not a strong enough case for a drawn
partition.

### 2.3 Absorption into Australia

No sourced evidence found either way yet for the specific post-collapse-absorption question — this survey
leans on Section 2.1's independence precedent by extension, but hasn't done a dedicated pass on Trans-Tasman
integration (CER, the Trans-Tasman Travel Arrangement, ANZUS) that a fuller treatment should cover before this
is called settled. Flagged as an open item, not a finding yet.

## Section 3 — The import lifeline: Australia's real vulnerability

### 3.1 Food: strength

Self-sufficiency above 200% (FAOSTAT-based aggregate, most recent data to 2021); a 2007 rate of 173% shows
this isn't a one-year anomaly. Net food exporter selling 65-70% of farm production overseas ($31.8B exports
vs. $11.6B imports in 2012/13, a clear surplus). Over 90% of fresh fruit, vegetables, meat, milk and eggs sold
domestically is domestically produced. Australia does still import some categories (coffee, chocolate,
processed fruit/veg, some baking ingredients) but these are not survival-critical.

### 3.2 Energy: two-sided — raw abundance, refined scarcity

Australia is the world's largest or near-largest LNG exporter (rankings vary by year/source — recent data
puts it third behind the US and Qatar, but it briefly held first; ~80 million tonnes/year, ~73% of domestic
gas production exported) and among the largest coal exporters worldwide. This is real, substantial raw-energy
wealth.

But refined fuel is a different story entirely: **80-90% of Australia's refined-fuel consumption is
import-reliant.** In 2025, imports ran roughly 850,000 barrels/day against ~1.1 million barrels/day of total
demand. Only two of Australia's four remaining oil refineries are still operating (BP's Kwinana and
ExxonMobil's Altona both closed) and domestic crude production (~320,000 barrels/day) mostly gets *exported*
(75% of it, chiefly to Indonesia and Singapore) rather than refined at home — what refining capacity remains
skews toward gasoline over the more strategically important diesel. Australia in 2024 was the world's 5th
largest importer of refined petroleum by value ($31.6B). **This is the single sharpest material fact in this
survey**: a country that exports enormous quantities of raw energy still cannot fuel its own trucks, tractors,
or military vehicles without imports.

### 3.3 Pharmaceuticals: a second severe vulnerability

~90% of Australia's medicines are imported, mostly from China and India, and this extends to the *raw
ingredients* (active pharmaceutical ingredients), not just finished products — meaning even attempting
emergency domestic manufacture would hit the same import wall one step back. Domestic manufacturing capacity
is described by industry sources as "extremely limited and diminishing... apart from vaccine manufacture." The
Royal Australian College of General Practitioners calls Australia "dangerously dependent" on imported
medicine. Already, in ordinary peacetime conditions, ~400 medicines are in short supply (30 critical) —
including a 2023 national shortage of the three most commonly prescribed antibiotics, traced to an ordinary
global supply-chain disruption, no war involved. **A war-driven collapse of the same supply chains would very
plausibly be catastrophic for Australian public health specifically**, even with food secure and the
population physically unharmed by direct attack.

### 3.4 The Pacific Islands: a different, more severe case entirely

Small Island Developing States in the Pacific are already, in the real present day, described as facing
"existential threats" from import dependency, independent of any war scenario. On Tuvalu's main island,
Funafuti, 80% of food is imported. These economies are narrow, often carry high debt, and — unlike
Australia's diversified economy — have little capacity to absorb a prolonged shock to food or fuel prices.
Real-world 2026 reporting already describes an active Pacific energy/supply crisis tied to Middle Eastern
conflict and Strait of Hormuz disruption, underlining how fast and how hard supply shocks hit these economies
even without a war reaching the Pacific directly. **This survey's provisional read: the Pacific Islands
proper are likely the part of Oceania where the 2083 war's supply-chain collapse would actually be
devastating**, in sharp contrast to Australia and New Zealand's comparative insulation — the opposite of
where an author's first instinct might land, since it's the *large, developed* nations that turn out fragile
on two specific goods (fuel, medicine) while staying food-secure, and the *small* nations that are fragile
across the board.

### Section 4.1 — Shipping time estimates for the candidate routes

**Added 2026-09-26, author-requested.** Dumont d'Urville and Casey are both already-developed Tepenian subnet
cities in the Inner Tepenia GDD (Janbogo Subnet and Mirny Subnet respectively — see
`games/Inner Tepenia/InnerTepeniaGDD/Worldspace/.../City_Megasheets/`), so these two specific route pairs
were the ones worth calculating:

**Method.** Great-circle (haversine) distance between each pair's real-world coordinates, then converted to a
transit-time estimate using real-world Antarctic resupply-vessel data as calibration — these are Southern
Ocean crossings with no landmass in the way, so great-circle is a legitimate proxy for actual sailing
distance (validated below, not just assumed).

**Calibration, against two real routes these ships already sail today:**
- **RSV Nuyina, Hobart↔Casey**: real sourced distance 1,848 nm; real sourced transit time **7-11 days**
  (varies with ice/weather/science-diversions). This project's own great-circle calculation for the same pair
  came out to 1,849 nm — a 0.05% difference, strong validation that great-circle is a reliable stand-in here.
- **L'Astrolabe, Hobart↔Dumont d'Urville**: real sourced one-way distance ~2,700 km (~1,458 nm); this
  project's own great-circle calculation: 1,448 nm — a 0.7% difference, same validation. L'Astrolabe's own
  specs: 12 knots cruising, 14 knots max, 35-day endurance at 12 knots.
- **Realistic-speed range derived from the Nuyina data point**: 1,848 nm in 7-11 days = **168-264 nm/day**,
  i.e. roughly **7-11 knots sustained average** including ice navigation, weather routing, and any en-route
  science stops — not a constant cruising speed the whole way.
- **Theoretical-minimum reference**: L'Astrolabe's 12-knot constant cruising speed (288 nm/day) if a voyage
  ran with zero delays — useful as a floor, not a realistic estimate on its own (real Hobart-Casey transits
  run 1.1-1.7× longer than this floor even on the real, already-sourced route).

**Distances and estimated transit times, the two requested route pairs:**

| Route | Great-circle distance | Realistic range (7-11kt avg) | Theoretical minimum (12kt constant) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dumont d'Urville ↔ Adelaide (Outer Harbor) | 1,915 nm (3,546 km) | **7.3-11.4 days** | 6.6 days |
| Dumont d'Urville ↔ Melbourne (Port Phillip Heads) | 1,711 nm (3,168 km) | **6.5-10.2 days** | 5.9 days |
| Casey ↔ Perth (Fremantle) | 2,064 nm (3,822 km) | **7.8-12.3 days** | 7.2 days |

**✅ SETTLED, author-confirmed 2026-09-26: for worldbuilding purposes, treat all three routes as roughly
"about a week and a half" (~10-11 days) each way.** That figure sits in the upper-middle of the realistic
range for all three routes (closest to Casey↔Perth's own range, a touch above Dumont d'Urville↔Melbourne's) —
a reasonable single working number to write to, rather than juggling three separate ranges. The per-route
ranges above remain the underlying research if more precision is ever needed for a specific scene or
calculation; this rounded figure is the one to default to otherwise.

**Caveats, flagged per this project's usual convention:**
- These numbers assume a Nuyina/L'Astrolabe-class ice-capable resupply vessel (1,200-3,000 DWT cargo
  capacity), not a large unrestricted-class container ship — appropriate for Antarctic-coastal routes with
  ice exposure near the departure end, but worth revisiting if Tepenia's own fictional shipping capability
  turns out to differ meaningfully from real-world polar-logistics vessels.
- The Adelaide/Melbourne/Perth distances are all *slightly longer* than the real Hobart benchmark routes
  (Hobart being closer to both Antarctic stations than any of the three candidate mainland ports), so these
  estimates lean on extrapolation beyond the calibration data's own distance range, not just interpolation
  within it — a modest but real source of additional uncertainty.
- Real transit times also depend on specific ice conditions each season, which vary year to year and which
  this survey hasn't modeled — the range above is a plausible band, not a guarantee.
- Melbourne's own Port Phillip Heads chokepoint (Section 4's own port-siting discussion above) isn't
  double-counted here — the distance used is to the Heads themselves, i.e. the open-coast candidate sites
  (Jan Juc/Torquay/Flinders) sit at essentially the same distance, not meaningfully further out.

### Section 4.2 — Australia's real-world import lifeline: which countries, and how far

**Added 2026-09-26, author-requested.** Section 3.3-3.4 already established *what* Australia structurally
can't produce (pharmaceuticals, refined fuel, phosphate/fertilizer, plus semiconductors and heavy machinery
via the Inner Tepenia cross-reference in §4). This section identifies *which countries* those goods actually
come from today, and how far each one is — the real-world supply lines a war-driven collapse would sever.

**Method**: real 2024 trade-partner data (WebSearch) for source countries per category; shipping-time
estimates via the same great-circle method as §4.1, calibrated this time against three real, sourced
commercial container-shipping benchmarks instead of the Antarctic-resupply ones — chosen because these are
ordinary trade routes, not ice-affected coastal approaches:
- **Shanghai↔Sydney**: real sourced range 12-21 days typical direct service; this project's great-circle
  distance, 4,256 nm.
- **Los Angeles↔Sydney**: real sourced fastest direct service 24.46 days; great-circle distance 6,509 nm
  (matches a separately-sourced 12,066 km figure almost exactly).
- **Rotterdam↔Melbourne**: real sourced ~40 days ocean transit via the Cape of Good Hope route (the route
  actively in use, not the shorter Suez Canal route — consistent with Red Sea shipping disruption already
  affecting real-world 2026 trade patterns).
- **Calibrated rate: 200-300 nm/day** (roughly 8-12.5 knots sustained average, well under a modern container
  ship's ~18-22 knot nominal service speed — the gap is real-world routing, scheduling and port-call overhead,
  the same pattern §4.1 found for Antarctic resupply vessels). Great-circle is a valid distance proxy for most
  of these routes (open ocean, no landmass in the way); **Morocco and Germany are the exceptions** — both
  require a real sailing route around Africa (the Cape of Good Hope), not a straight line, so those two use a
  waypoint-corrected distance instead of raw great-circle.

**Australia's critical single-country dependencies, ranked by how many "can't do it ourselves" categories
each one covers:**

| Country | What Australia depends on it for | Source port (assumed) → Australian gateway | Distance | Estimated transit time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **China** | Pharmaceuticals (one of two dominant sources); semiconductors (part of the top-3 57% share); vehicles (14.4% market share); some fertilizer; Australia's #1 trading partner overall ($75.7-120B) | Shanghai → Sydney | 4,256 nm | **14-21 days** |
| **Japan** | Vehicles (#1 single source, 31% market share); a growing share of refined fuel (diesel 23%, jet fuel 13%) | Yokohama → Sydney | 4,212 nm | **14-21 days** |
| **South Korea** | Refined fuel (#1 single source by value, $9.18B — 45% of petrol imports, 20% of diesel); vehicles ($16.51B); semiconductors | Busan → Sydney | 4,328 nm | **14-22 days** |
| **Singapore** | Refined fuel (historically the dominant regional source, still ~21-34% depending on fuel type — a trading/storage hub, not always the original refinery); semiconductors | Singapore → Fremantle | 2,112 nm | **7-11 days** (shortest of any major partner) |
| **Malaysia** | Refined fuel ($4.18B); semiconductors (part of the top-3 57% share); **rare-earth processing** — the Lynas Malaysia plant, Australian-owned but physically the only heavy-rare-earth refining capacity outside China at commercial scale | Port Klang → Fremantle | 2,258 nm | **8-11 days** |
| **India** | Pharmaceuticals (the other of the two dominant sources, $532M+); refined fuel ($3.16B) | Mumbai → Fremantle | 3,933 nm | **13-20 days** |
| **Taiwan** | Semiconductors (the world's #1 exporter, part of Australia's top-3 57% share); some refined fuel ($2.61B) | Kaohsiung → Sydney | 3,824 nm | **13-19 days** |
| **Thailand** | Vehicles (#2/3 source, 22.4% market share, $13.50B); Australia's top-5 trading partner overall | Laem Chabang → Fremantle | 2,842 nm | **9-14 days** |
| **Morocco** | Phosphate rock/fertilizer — the dominant single source ($697-814M), effectively irreplaceable domestically given Australia's own ~15% self-sufficiency in this category | Jorf Lasfar → Fremantle, via the Cape of Good Hope | 9,002 nm | **30-45 days** (by far the longest and most exposed of these routes) |
| **Germany** | Vehicles; heavy machinery and industrial equipment | Hamburg → Fremantle, via the Cape of Good Hope | ~9,000 nm (Hamburg sits ~223 nm from the sourced Rotterdam benchmark) | **~40 days** (real sourced figure, not calculated) |
| **USA** | Australia's #2 trading partner overall ($34.7-96.8B); machinery, aircraft, and other high-value manufactured/technology goods | Los Angeles → Sydney | 6,509 nm | **22-33 days** |

**What this table says, structurally**: Australia's *closest* critical suppliers (Singapore, Malaysia,
Thailand — all 7-14 days) are exactly the ones supplying its *most acute* single-point-of-failure goods
(fuel, semiconductors, the one non-Chinese rare-earth processor) — meaning the shortest, most defensible
supply lines are also carrying the most irreplaceable cargo. Its most distant critical supplier (Morocco,
30-45 days, no viable substitute source at any distance) is carrying phosphate — already independently
flagged in the Inner Tepenia cross-reference as directly paralleling Tepenian canon's own phosphorus
chokepoint. A war that disrupted global shipping broadly, rather than targeting Australia specifically, would
hit the Morocco and Europe/USA routes hardest simply by virtue of transit-time exposure — five to six times
longer at sea, and correspondingly more opportunity for a voyage to be interdicted, delayed, or never
completed.

**Caveats**: assumed source ports are each country's largest/most typical export port for the relevant good,
not verified against actual per-shipment routing data; real cargo may transit through a hub port (Singapore
especially) rather than sailing direct from its true country of origin, which the "Singapore = regional
trading hub" note above already partly acknowledges but doesn't fully resolve category-by-category. The
Morocco and Germany Cape-route distances are this session's own waypoint approximation (Morocco) or a directly
borrowed real benchmark (Germany), not independently verified route data.
