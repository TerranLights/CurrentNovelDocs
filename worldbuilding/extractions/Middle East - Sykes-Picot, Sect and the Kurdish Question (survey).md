## Headline findings

1. **This region's Sykes-Picot is East Asia's Qin/Han unification and Central Asia's Soviet national
   delimitation — the single fact that explains why the modern map looks the way it does, and it is at least
   as arbitrary as either.** The 1916 Sykes-Picot Agreement (Britain and France, with Russia's assent) carved
   post-Ottoman Arab lands into zones of influence with no regard for the ethnic, religious, or tribal geography
   underneath; the League of Nations Mandate system that followed (Britain: Iraq, Palestine/Transjordan;
   France: Syria, Lebanon) turned those zones into the borders Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and Israel/
   Palestine still carry today. Unlike Central Asia's delimitation (a single actor, Stalin, executing one
   coherent policy) or East Asia's unification (a single expanding core), Sykes-Picot was two competing
   colonial powers splitting the spoils — the incoherence is the point, not an accident. See §1.
2. **The Kurds are this region's Tibet/Xinjiang/Deep South/Bangsamoro case, and it is currently in the middle
   of the fastest, largest set of changes any stateless-nation case in this project has tracked.** ~45 million
   Kurds, the world's largest stateless nation, split across Turkey (roughly half), Iran, and Iraq (6–7 million
   each), and Syria (~2.5 million) — a direct consequence of Sykes-Picot's line-drawing splitting a
   pre-existing Kurdish-majority zone four ways. **Two of this case's four pieces changed status within the
   last thirteen months of real-world time**: Turkey's Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK), after four decades of
   armed conflict (40,000+ dead since 1984), declared a unilateral ceasefire in March 2025 and formally began
   disarming in mid-2025; Syria's Kurdish autonomous administration (AANES/"Rojava") was fully dissolved and
   integrated into the new Syrian state on 20 August 2026. Only the Kurdistan Region of Iraq (autonomous since
   1991/2005, its own KDP/PUK-run government and Peshmerga) remains a stable, functioning autonomous entity as
   of this survey. See §3.
3. **The Sunni-Shia split is this region's single most load-bearing religious fact, rooted in a specific,
   datable historical event (680 CE) the way the East Asia survey's Mandarin dialect splits or the Southeast
   Asia surveys' Islamization waves were — but it maps onto modern state borders far more directly than either
   of those.** Iraq is 65–70% Shia, Bahrain is Shia-majority under a Sunni monarchy, Lebanon is 28% Shia (one of
   three legally recognized top-tier confessional blocs), Yemen's Houthi movement is rooted in Zaidi Shia
   identity, and Syria carries a 15–20% Shia-aligned (Alawite/Twelver) minority — against Iran as the region's
   major Shia power and Saudi Arabia as its major Sunni one. This is a real fault line with a 1,300-year
   pedigree, not a modern invention layered onto older politics. See §4.
4. **Sykes-Picot's colonial logic and Central Asia's Soviet delimitation are structurally the same move, made
   by different empires forty years apart — worth stating explicitly as the cross-region parallel that
   connects this survey to its companion.** Both drew administrative lines through a pre-existing, more
   ethnically continuous space, for the colonizer's own strategic convenience, with predictable
   border-splits-a-people consequences (Kurds here; Uzbeks/Tajiks/Kyrgyz in the Fergana Valley there). Neither
   region's modern borders are a "first effective settlement" story in Woodard's sense — both need the same
   tiered-ethnic-identity substitute method East Asia and Russia already established for this project.
5. **The Arabian Peninsula runs on a genuinely different logic from the Fertile Crescent and Turkey — tribal
   confederation and oil-monarchy governance, not post-colonial republic-building.** Saudi Arabia's 1932
   unification was a specific, real politico-religious alliance (the House of Saud plus the Wahhabi movement,
   dating to the 1744 Diriyah agreement) executing a 30-year conquest, not a colonial-mandate handover. The six
   Gulf Cooperation Council monarchies (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman) sit on ~30% of the
   world's proven oil reserves and, per 2026 figures, range from Qatar's $87,661 GDP per capita (the region's
   and one of the world's highest) down to Oman's $21,897 — still enormously wealthy by world standards, but a
   real internal tier structure worth keeping distinct from the poorer republics. Yemen, geographically on the
   same peninsula, is the peninsula's own sharpest internal contradiction: one of the world's poorest countries,
   currently fighting an active three-way civil war. See §5.
6. **Turkey and Afghanistan are this survey's two bridge cases, each with a real, documented tie back to the
   companion Central Asia survey's own subject matter — flagged here, not resolved, exactly the way this
   project has always handled cross-region seams.** Turkey's Turkic-language kinship with Kazakhstan,
   Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan (and, more distantly, Azerbaijan) is real and has a real modern
   political expression (pan-Turkism, the Organization of Turkic States) — a live sphere-overlay candidate.
   Afghanistan's Tajik, Uzbek, and Turkmen minorities are the same peoples, across a border, as the titular
   nationalities of three of the five Central Asian republics; its Dari language is a Persian-family language,
   giving it a second real tie to Iran. See §7.
7. **Modern Turkey and modern Saudi Arabia are the region's two clearest "first effective settlement"-style
   founding stories, and they point in opposite directions.** Turkey's 1923 founding under Mustafa Kemal
   Atatürk was a deliberate, total break from the Ottoman-Islamic past — the Caliphate abolished 1924,
   secularism imposed by law, national orientation turned toward Europe. Saudi Arabia's 1932 founding fused
   dynastic conquest with a specific religious movement (Wahhabism) into the closest thing this survey has to a
   real theocratic founding story. Both are real, well-documented, and give this survey two clean anchor cases
   the way Woodard's original settler-nations gave North America its anchors — everything else in the region
   sits somewhere between "colonial-line successor state" (most of the Fertile Crescent) and one of these two
   poles.
8. **Water is this region's version of Central Asia's upstream/downstream tension, and Turkey plays the
   upstream role Kyrgyzstan/Tajikistan play there.** Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP) — 22 dams,
   19 hydroelectric plants on the Tigris-Euphrates headwaters — has cut Iraq's water supply along both rivers
   by an estimated 80% since 1975 and, at full build-out, would stop 40–70% of the Euphrates's natural flow
   before it reaches Syria or Iraq at all. Syria depends on external sources for 80% of its water, two-thirds
   of that from the Euphrates specifically. This is a real, current, quantified grievance in the same category
   as Sabah/Sarawak's MA63 royalties in the Maritime Southeast Asia survey — documented and current, not merely
   historical.
9. **The Strait of Hormuz is this region's Strait of Malacca, and it is currently an active war zone, not a
   peacetime chokepoint.** ~20 million barrels of oil per day (≈20% of global petroleum consumption,
   25–27% of seaborne oil trade) and 19% of global LNG trade transit the Strait in normal times. As of this
   survey, the 2026 Iran war has repeatedly disrupted that traffic outright — a live, active demonstration of
   exactly the chokepoint vulnerability the Maritime Southeast Asia survey discussed for Malacca in the
   abstract.
10. **Israel/Palestine shares Sykes-Picot's own colonial-mandate origin (§1) but is, on its own, this survey's
    other major live-conflict case alongside the 2026 Iran war (§6) — added on a research follow-up pass, not
    in this survey's original scope.** The British Mandate produced the same kind of externally-drawn,
    ethnically-indifferent line-drawing §1 describes for Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Jordan, but it is the one
    case in this survey where that colonial origin fed directly into a still-unresolved, currently-active
    conflict rather than settling into a stable state border. The 1948 Nakba displaced roughly 957,000 of 1.4
    million Palestinians; today, 78% of Gazans and 30% of West Bankers are themselves descendants of that
    displacement — a 78-year-old event with a present-tense demographic and legal status, not closed history.
    As of this survey's date, a fragile Gaza ceasefire (since October 2025) coexists with continued deaths and
    an escalating, separate West Bank crisis. See §2.

## Section 1 — Sykes-Picot and the post-Ottoman settlement

**The agreement itself.** In May 1916, Britain and France — with Russia's assent — secretly agreed to divide
the Arab provinces of the collapsing Ottoman Empire into zones of direct control and zones of influence. Mark
Sykes (Britain) and François Georges-Picot (France) drew a line, since become shorthand for the whole
enterprise, roughly from Acre to Kirkuk: a French-influenced Arab state (zone A, roughly today's Syria); a
British-influenced Arab state (zone B, roughly today's southern Israel, southern West Bank, Jordan, and
western Iraq); direct French control (coastal Syria, Lebanon, southeastern Turkey); direct British control
(most of Iraq, plus Haifa/Acre); and an internationally administered zone (roughly northern Israel and most of
the West Bank). Neither power consulted the Arab leaders (notably Sharif Hussein of Mecca) they had separately
been negotiating with for a postwar independent Arab state — a documented, contemporaneous betrayal, not a
retrospective judgment.

**From agreement to mandate to modern border.** The 1920 San Remo Conference and the subsequent League of
Nations Mandate system converted these zones into formal administration: **France** received the mandates for
**Syria and Lebanon**; **Britain** received the mandates for **Iraq** and **Palestine** (which included
present-day Jordan, split off administratively in 1921 as Transjordan). The mandate boundaries, drawn for
colonial administrative convenience rather than to follow any ethnic, sectarian, tribal, or geographic logic,
became — almost without exception — the international borders these countries hold today. **This is the same
structural move as the Central Asia companion survey's own central fact (Soviet national delimitation,
1924–1936)**: an external power drawing lines through a more continuous space, and modern states inheriting
those lines wholesale at independence under the same customary-international-law principle (*uti possidetis
juris*) already discussed in this project's Maritime Southeast Asia survey.

**Why this deserves the same weight East Asia gave Qin/Han unification.** Every other candidate "first
effective settlement"-style founding force in this region (below) explains at most a few of its fifteen
countries. Sykes-Picot/the Mandate system directly explains the existence and shape of five of them (Iraq,
Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and — via the same British Palestine mandate — Israel/Palestine's borders), and its
consequences (the Kurdish partition specifically) touch two more (Turkey, Iran) that were never themselves
mandated territory. No other single historical event in this survey's scope has that reach.

⚠️ **Confidence: high** on the agreement's content and the mandate assignments (multiple corroborating
sources: Britannica, the ECF, History Hit, Al Jazeera's own retrospective). **Medium** on precise zone
boundary claims beyond the broad A/B/direct-control/international-zone shape, since period maps and modern
retrospectives summarize the same agreement with slightly different boundary emphasis.

## Section 2 — Israel/Palestine

⚠️ **This section is the single most likely part of this entire survey to be outdated by the time anyone
reads it, and it is written with that expectation.** It shares the British Palestine Mandate origin already
described in §1, but everything from §2.5 onward describes an active, fast-moving 2024-2026 conflict. As with
§6's treatment of the 2026 Iran war, this survey takes **no position on the conflict's rights, wrongs, or
eventual resolution** — it records documented historical and demographic facts, cites them, and flags the
live-political material as a perishable snapshot dated 2026-09-27, not settled history. Re-verify current
status before relying on §2.5 or §2.6 in any future session.

### 2.1 Origins: the Mandate and the 1947 Partition Plan

Britain's Mandate for Palestine (§1) governed the territory from 1920 until 1948. Jewish immigration,
organized Zionist settlement, and Arab-Jewish intercommunal tension all grew substantially under the Mandate,
producing repeated violence (the 1936–1939 Arab revolt among the largest instances) that Britain was unable to
resolve. In November 1947, the newly formed **United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 181**, the
Partition Plan, proposing separate Jewish and Arab states with Jerusalem under international administration.
The plan was accepted by Jewish leadership and rejected by Arab leadership in Palestine and the surrounding
Arab states — a rejection with its own real, documented reasoning (the plan allotted the proposed Jewish state
more than half the territory while the Jewish population held a minority share of the total population and
land at the time), reported here as historical fact, not evaluated for merit. **Confidence: high** (widely
corroborated across standard historical sources).

### 2.2 The 1948 war and the Nakba

Israel declared independence on 14 May 1948; neighboring Arab states invaded the next day, beginning the
1948 Arab-Israeli War. By its end, Israel held substantially more territory than the Partition Plan had
allotted it. **An estimated 957,000 of the roughly 1.4 million Palestinians living in historical Palestine
were displaced from roughly 1,300 villages and towns** — the event Palestinians call the **Nakba** ("the
catastrophe"). Most displaced Palestinians resettled in the West Bank, Gaza, and neighboring Arab states
(Jordan's own share of this population is detailed at §5.3). **Confidence: high** on the displacement scale
(Badil Resource Center's figures, corroborated by UN and general historical sourcing); this survey does not
adjudicate the war's causes or conduct, only records its demographic consequence.

### 2.3 The 1967 war and the territories

In June 1967, Israel fought and defeated a coalition of Egypt, Jordan, and Syria in the **Six-Day War**,
capturing the **West Bank and East Jerusalem** (from Jordan), the **Gaza Strip** (from Egypt), and the
**Golan Heights** (from Syria, outside this survey's Israel/Palestine scope but a live Israel-Syria border
question in its own right). An estimated 200,000+ additional Palestinians were displaced in this second wave,
most into Jordan. ⚠️ **One source found this pass cited a figure of "around 2 million" Palestinians displaced
from Gaza specifically in 1967** — this is almost certainly an error or a conflation with a much later or
different statistic, since Gaza's entire population in 1967 was itself only in the hundreds of thousands; that
figure is **not used** in this survey and is flagged here only so a future pass doesn't rediscover and
mistakenly trust the same bad source. **Confidence: high** on the war's outcome and territorial consequences;
**low** on any precise 1967-specific Gaza displacement figure, which this pass could not verify to a credible
source.

### 2.4 Population today

**Israel**: population **10.18 million (2025)** — approximately **76.3% Jewish and other non-Arab groups**,
**21.1% Arab-Israeli citizens** (2.147 million people). **The West Bank**: approximately **3.2 million
Palestinians** (2022 figure, the most recent this pass located), plus **over 750,000 Israeli settlers** (about
15% of the West Bank's total population) across 147 settlements and a record-setting wave of new outposts (54
new settlements officially approved and 86 new outposts erected in 2025 alone, both reported as all-time
records). **Gaza**: approximately **2.2 million Palestinians** (pre-war 2022/2023 baseline; the war
covered in §2.5 has killed and displaced a substantial fraction of this population, detailed there). Roughly
**78% of Gazans and 30% of West Bankers** are themselves registered descendants of 1948 refugees (§2.2) — the
Nakba is not a closed historical chapter for this population, it is an ongoing legal and demographic status.
**Confidence: high** on the Israel figures (Israeli government/Taub Center/press reporting all converge);
**medium-high** on West Bank/Gaza population figures, which are 2022-vintage pre-war baselines, not
independently updated this pass for war-driven displacement or casualties (see §2.5).

### 2.5 The Gaza war and the 2026 status — this section's most perishable material

The current war began after Hamas's attack on Israel on 7 October 2023. By official Gaza Ministry of Health
count, roughly **67,000–75,000+ people had been killed by late 2025/early 2026**; an independent
population-based household survey (published in The Lancet Global Health) estimated a higher toll — roughly
75,200 violent deaths plus 8,540 excess non-violent deaths between October 2023 and January 2025 — flagged
here as a real, documented discrepancy between official and independent counts, not resolved by this survey.
⚠️ **As of this survey's research date (2026-09-27)**: a ceasefire (the second phase of a negotiated peace
deal) has been in effect since **10 October 2025**, but multiple sources describe it as fragile and
co-existing with a "de facto limited war" — reported death counts *since* the ceasefire took effect range from
roughly 450 to over 1,400 additional Palestinians killed, depending on source and count window (this survey
could not reconcile the two figures to a single number this pass and reports both rather than picking one).
**Israel controls over half of Gaza's territory** under the ceasefire's current terms; **Hamas remains
armed**; humanitarian conditions are described as dire. **The West Bank is separately escalating**: as of late
September 2026, a senior Israeli minister (Bezalel Smotrich) publicly called for "war" in the West Bank,
amid what UN officials describe as rising settlement activity and violence. On the political side, Palestinian
Authority President Mahmoud Abbas has designated 2026 a "year of Palestinian democracy," with municipal and
village-council elections planned for April 2026 in both the West Bank and Gaza. **Confidence: medium** on
all of the above — every figure and status claim in this subsection is a snapshot already in flux as this
survey is being written, sourced to 2026 reporting (Al Jazeera, UN press releases, Chatham House, Costs of War
at Brown University), not a stable, settled record.

### 2.6 Core unresolved questions — not resolved here

Matching this survey's posture toward the Kurdish question (§3.7) and Yemen's civil war (§5.4): this survey
records that **the two-state solution, the one-state alternative, Jerusalem's final status, and the legal
status of West Bank settlements are all real, live, competing positions with no resolution as of this survey's
date**, and takes none of them as this project's own eventual answer. On settlements specifically: the
dominant international-legal position (reflected in UN bodies and most governments' official positions) holds
that Israeli civilian settlements in occupied territory violate the Fourth Geneva Convention; Israel's own
government maintains a different legal position regarding the territories' status. Both positions are real and
documented; this survey reports that a live legal disagreement exists without adjudicating it, the same
posture it takes toward the Kurdish question's own unresolved final status.

### 2.7 Post-collapse scenario, in development: mutual nuclear exchange, Jerusalem spared

⚠️ **This subsection documents a scenario the author is actively building for this project's own post-collapse
future-history, not a resolution of §2.6's live real-world questions above.** It is being developed
incrementally and had not yet been applied to the map's own data as of this survey's most recent update.

**The mechanism, grounded rather than invented**: this project's own §6 already documents a live, real "2026
Iran war" (the US and Israel at war with Iran since 28 February 2026) as this region's starting condition. The
author's working scenario extends this into a **mutual nuclear exchange between multiple combatants**, growing
out of that same war, rather than Israel's collapse being caused by any single actor or narrative device.
Real, sourced facts ground the mechanism: **Israel is itself an undeclared nuclear power** (credible estimates
converge around 90-100 warheads, sourced from the Dimona reactor, under a deliberate "nuclear ambiguity"
policy), so any exchange is mutual, not one-sided; Israel's population and state apparatus are genuinely
concentrated in a handful of identifiable strategic nodes — **Tel Aviv/Gush Dan** (its financial/tech
economy), **Haifa** (its major port/industrial center), and **Beer Sheva/Dimona** (the literal source of its
own nuclear deterrent) — meaning a real exchange could plausibly collapse the **state** (government, economy,
military command, the deterrent's own source) without anything close to total population loss, since a large,
genuinely dispersed population survives outside those specific nodes (broad metro-area population statistics
overstate direct casualties, once real missile-defense interception — Iron Dome, David's Sling, Arrow 2/3 —
and actual blast/radiation radii, much smaller than stated metro-area footprints, are accounted for).

**✅ SETTLED, 2026-09-29ish (exact date approximate; developed across this same research session): Jerusalem
itself takes no damage at all.** Author's own words: "the city of Jerusalem is untouched. No damage at all
came to it." Following directly from that: **Jerusalem becomes a truly neutral city under mutually shared
international administration** — author's own reasoning, stated directly: "nobody anywhere wants to cause
harm to it because everybody has a vested-interest stake in its existence." This is not an invented solution —
it is, in effect, a return to the **corpus separatum** concept the 1947 UN Partition Plan itself originally
proposed for Jerusalem (§2.1) — international administration, belonging exclusively to neither a Jewish nor an
Arab state, precisely because of its shared significance to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam — a plan that was
overtaken by the 1948 war before it was ever implemented. In this scenario, the city survives physically
intact (government buildings, religious sites, the whole built environment) even as the modern nation-state
apparatus around it collapses, and the same universal stake that spared it physically becomes the basis for
governing it afterward.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29: the governing body, and the territorial outcome.** Two more pieces resolved in the same
research pass. First, **Hamas does not survive this scenario as a governing force** — the author's own explicit
call ("I really really don't like the idea of Hamas being allowed to exist"), given a real, grounded mechanism
rather than authorial fiat: Hamas is part of Iran's "Axis of Resistance" network, dependent on Iran for
funding, arms, and training despite the Sunni/Shia mismatch (a pragmatic alliance, not a sectarian one); with
Iran itself devastated in the same mutual nuclear exchange, Hamas loses its external patron at the exact moment
Israel's own already-documented military campaign against it (§2.5's "de facto limited war," ongoing even
under the current ceasefire) is already grinding it down. Second, **the Palestinian Authority becomes the
official government**, left as the only functioning Palestinian institution once Hamas collapses, and **all
of the former State of Israel's territory becomes Palestine** — author's own words: "The Palestinian Authority
becomes the official government, and all that land becomes Palestine." This is not a formal population
transfer or negotiated settlement; the mechanism is closer to a one-state outcome arrived at by state collapse
rather than compromise — once Israel's own government, military, and administrative apparatus are gone, the
Palestinian Authority is simply the government left standing over the whole territory, and the population
within it falls under its jurisdiction by default of geography. A real precedent for successful absorption at
this kind of scale already exists within this very project: Jordan's own history (§5.3) of naturalizing
roughly a million Palestinian refugees as full citizens after 1948 — "the only Arab country to fully integrate"
a refugee population at that scale.

**Still not yet resolved, explicitly not decided by the above**: the specific status of Israel's surviving
dispersed population within the new Palestinian state (full equal citizenship, versus some other arrangement —

it); and the specific status of the West Bank's own Israeli settler population (~750,000+, §2.4) given the
occupation history behind their presence there — both raised and deliberately left open pending further

## Section 3 — The Kurdish question

### 3.1 The people and the four-way split

Kurds are the world's largest stateless ethnic group — approximately **45 million people (2026)** — an
Iranic-language people (distinct from Arab, Persian, and Turkic Tier 1 identities, though linguistically
Iranic-adjacent) whose homeland was cut into four pieces by the same post-Ottoman settlement discussed in §1
(the Ottoman Empire held Kurdish-majority territory that was never given its own mandate or state, unlike the
Arab populations around it). Distribution: **roughly half in Turkey**; **6–7 million each in Iran and Iraq**;
**approximately 2.5 million in Syria**. No other ethnic group in this survey's scope is split so evenly across
so many of its countries.

### 3.2 Iraq — the one stable case

The **Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI)** has been autonomous since 1991 (de facto, following the Gulf War's
northern no-fly zone) and since 2005 under Iraq's own post-Saddam constitution — the country's only regional
government with its own presidency, ministers, and parliament. Politics are dominated by two parties, the
Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP) and the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK), each maintaining its own security
force (Peshmerga) rather than a single unified command — a real internal fracture worth noting even within
this survey's one clearly "settled" Kurdish case. **This is the region's Bangsamoro-equivalent**: the lines
already exist, drawn by the state itself, not a hypothetical this survey needs to propose.

### 3.3 Turkey — a forty-year war that just (mostly) ended

The **Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK)**, founded 1978 near Lice by Abdullah Öcalan and a group of Kurdish
students, fought an armed insurgency against the Turkish state from 1984 that killed over 40,000 people —
initially for outright independence, shifting after 2010 to a demand for autonomy and cultural/political
rights within Turkey rather than secession. **⚠️ This changed very recently, within the current year of this
survey's setting**: the PKK declared a unilateral ceasefire on 1 March 2025, and at its 12th Congress
(5–7 May 2025) pledged to end all armed and political operations; a symbolic disarmament ceremony followed in
Sulaymaniyah, Iraq, in mid-2025. Sources describe this as fragile and contested ("transitional or
transactional justice" is a live open question in the sourced commentary, not a settled peace), but as of this
survey's research date it represents the effective end of the longest-running conflict covered anywhere in
this survey. **Confidence: high** that the ceasefire/disarmament process is real and dated correctly (multiple
corroborating sources: Congress.gov, ICTJ, Baker Institute, Small Wars Journal); **medium** on how durable it
will prove, which the sources themselves treat as unresolved.

### 3.4 Syria — autonomy, then dissolution, within eighteen months

The **Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES/"Rojava")** and its military arm, the
Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF), governed a substantial, real, functioning autonomous zone in
northeastern Syria for roughly a decade, born out of the Syrian civil war and the fight against ISIS. **⚠️ As
of 20 August 2026, AANES was formally dissolved and integrated into the new Syrian state** (below, §3.5),
following more than a year of negotiation between the SDF and the transitional government under Ahmed
al-Sharaa. Sourced commentary from early-to-mid 2026 (Manara Magazine, Jacobin, Critical Legal Thinking, OSW
Centre for Eastern Studies) frames this dissolution as contested and precarious for Kurdish political
prospects in the new Syria, not a clean or fully voluntary resolution. **Confidence: high** that the formal
dissolution occurred on the stated date (multiple independent sources converge); **low** on what it will
actually mean in practice for Syrian Kurds' rights and security going forward, which the sourced commentary
itself treats as an open, live question.

### 3.5 The Syrian state Kurds are now integrating into

Bashar al-Assad's government — in power in some form since 1971 (his father Hafez, then Bashar from 2000) —
fell in December 2024 when a rebel offensive led by Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham captured Damascus, ending the
13-year Syrian civil war. **Ahmed al-Sharaa**, the rebel leader, was declared transitional president in
January 2025; a transitional constitution took effect that March, a transitional government formed 29 March
2025, and Syria's first new parliament in the al-Sharaa era assembled in June 2026. Chatham House (cited via
the House of Commons Library) flags the "delayed or non-participation of Druze and Kurdish areas" as a real
risk to the new parliament's legitimacy — meaning the Kurdish integration discussed in §3.4 is happening
alongside, not after, the new state's own institutional consolidation. **This is an extremely young government
by any standard** — barely eighteen months old, its first parliament barely three months old, as of this
survey's date.

### 3.6 Iran — the least-changed of the four, and the least researched this pass

⚠️ **Not independently researched this pass beyond the population figure in §3.1.** Iran's own Kurdish
population and its historical/political relationship to the central Iranian state (including the Kurdish role
in the 1979 revolution's aftermath and periodic unrest since) is a real gap in this survey, flagged rather
than filled, on the same "flag rather than fill" convention the Maritime Southeast Asia survey used for its
own named-but-unread sources.

### 3.7 Why this matters for the map, and what this survey does not decide

The Kurdish case is structurally the closest thing in this entire survey to East Asia's Tibet/Xinjiang or the
Southeast Asia surveys' Deep South/Bangsamoro/West Papua cases: **a single, coherent, non-titular ethnic
nation whose homeland is split by hard state borders that predate and ignore it.** What makes it harder than
any of those precedents is that **its status is not one stable fact but three different, fast-moving
facts at once** — settled autonomy in Iraq, a just-ended war in Turkey, and a just-dissolved autonomy in
Syria, with Iran's piece unresearched. **This survey does not propose a treatment** (sphere overlay across all
four host countries, per-country autonomous-region treatment matching each country's own real status, or
something else) — it surfaces the facts and flags the question as exactly the kind of decision this project's
own convention reserves for the author, with the explicit caution that any treatment chosen should account for
the real possibility that Turkey's ceasefire or Syria's integration could still change again before a map is
drawn.

**⚠️ Border redrawn 2026-09-28, and a standing expectation of violence recorded along the new seam.** The
author used the real Taurus-Zagros mountain belt (§7.3's own pan-Turkic material and independent geological
research this pass) as a cleanup line for the Kurdish Nation/Anatolia border's own jagged province-by-province
shape — the same "clean shape over perfect fidelity" tradeoff this project's North America map already makes
in places. **Tunceli, Bingöl and Muş move from the Kurdish Nation's domain into Anatolia's core.** The
geological case is real: Muş's own plain is explicitly bounded to its south by the Bitlis Mountains, part of
the actual Arabian-Anatolian suture; Tunceli sits in its own separate highland basin (the Munzur Mountains)
rather than on the main mountain spine. **The demographic tension is equally real and not hidden by this
redraw**: Bingöl and Muş are both recorded elsewhere in this survey as straightforwardly Kurdish-majority/
plurality provinces, the same tier as the core domain provinces that remain in Kurdish Nation territory — this
cut is geological, not a claim that Kurds don't live there.

**✅ Author's explicit expectation, recorded here rather than left implicit**: given that cut, **this survey
should expect real ethnic and sectarian violence specifically in the areas where Kurdish and Turkish
populations interact along this redrawn seam** — Tunceli, Bingöl, and Muş themselves, plus the broader
Kurdish Nation/Anatolia frontier generally. This is not a resolved prediction (no specific event, date, or
outcome is asserted) but a standing structural expectation for any future session developing this border
further: a geologically clean line drawn across a population that doesn't itself split cleanly along it is
exactly the kind of seam that produces friction in practice, and this survey's own three other Kurdish-host
countries (Turkey's own 40-year PKK war, Iraq's Kirkuk dispute, Syria's just-dissolved AANES) already show
that pattern playing out at every other Kurdish-national boundary on this map. Treat this the same way the
project treats other flagged-not-built future-development items (Uzbekistan's Samarkand/Bukhara scenario,
Afghanistan's Northern-Alliance-style breakaway premise, Osh's disputed-territory question) — a real, sourced-
enough premise for a later session to design out properly, not something to default into a specific outcome
now.

### 3.8 Iranian/"South" Azerbaijan — a deliberate comparison case, added 2026-09-28

⚠️ **Added on a targeted research pass after the author asked specifically about the Azeri-majority provinces
of northwest Iran (East Azerbaijan, Ardabil, and the Azeri-majority portion of West Azerbaijan) and how they
compare to the Kurdish question.** Researched to the same depth deliberately, since this survey's §1
Tier-1/Tier-2 method and the Kurdish question above already treat Iran's Azeri population as a live comparison
point — this section closes that gap rather than leaving it as a soft, unverified flag.

**The population scale rivals or exceeds the Kurdish case, and is just as contested.** Iran does not publish a
clean ethnic census; estimates for Iranian Azerbaijanis range from a low of 6–6.5 million up to 15–23 million,
or roughly **16–24% of Iran's entire population** — potentially the single largest non-Persian group in the
country by a wide margin. Tabriz itself, East Azerbaijan's capital and Iran's fourth-largest city, is **96.5%
Azeri** (2010 census) — an overwhelming supermajority, comparable to how concentrated the Kurdish population
is in Iraq's KRI governorates.

**A real secessionist state actually existed here once, capital Tabriz — but its own collapse is the load-
bearing fact, not its founding.** On 21 November 1945, with Soviet troops occupying northern Iran at the end
of WWII, Seyid Jafar Pishevari's Azerbaijan Democratic Party proclaimed the **Azerbaijan People's
Government** — a real, functioning, Soviet-backed state with its own armed fedayeen units and a constituent
assembly. This episode, the **Iran Crisis of 1946**, was one of the opening flashpoints of the Cold War and
among the first matters the newly-formed UN Security Council ever took up. **It collapsed within about a
year**, the moment Soviet troops withdrew under UN/US/British pressure — Iranian forces retook the region in
December 1946 with comparatively little organic resistance. **Confidence: high**, multiple corroborating
sources on both the government's founding and its rapid collapse once its external military patron withdrew.

**Why Tehran holds this region, and why the mechanism is structurally different from every other minority case
in this survey**: shared Twelver Shia identity, not coercion, is the integrating force, and it has produced
genuine elite access rather than mere tolerance — **Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei is of Azerbaijani origin, and
Masoud Pezeshkian, Iran's sitting president as of this survey's research date, is also Azerbaijani.** Iranian
Azerbaijanis are broadly represented across the political, military, clerical and intellectual elite. This is
the direct structural opposite of how Iran treats its Sunni minorities: the **gozinesh system**, an
ideological loyalty-vetting process for state employment, structurally locks Kurds and Baloch out of
government jobs and political office; Kurds face forced sedentarization and heavily militarized policing of
their region; the death penalty is documented as disproportionately applied against Kurdish and Baloch
defendants specifically. None of this applies to Azeris in anything like the same way. Real, live grievances
persist regardless — no Azeri-language public education, limited Azeri-language media — and a named
"Azerbaijani separatism in Iran" / "South Azerbaijan" movement never fully disappeared, carrying a pan-Turkist
"Whole Azerbaijan" unification idea that still occasionally surfaces in Tehran-Baku tensions today.

**Direct comparison to the Kurdish question, and the reasoned conclusion this survey draws from it**: Kurdish
nationalism has real indigenous institutional depth independent of any single foreign patron — the KRG has
governed continuously since 1991/2005, the Peshmerga is a real standing force, and the PKK sustained a
40-year insurgency through shifting outside support. **Iranian Azerbaijan has no comparable scaffolding.** No
standing party, no militia, no autonomous administrative apparatus survives from 1946 — any future movement
would have to build these from zero, with none of the KRG's multi-decade head start. Combined with eighty
years of successful elite co-option (a population that includes the literal Supreme Leader is not a
population structurally primed to rise against the state the way a systematically excluded one is), **this
survey's own reasoned conclusion is that Iranian Azerbaijan is one of the weakest separatist cases anywhere in
this survey's scope — weaker than a genuine open question, closer to a settled "stays" than the Kurdish case,
Baloch case, or even Yemen's north/south split.** The 1945–46 precedent specifically depended on an external
military occupier providing the state apparatus itself, not just moral or financial backing; absent a
functional replacement for that role, this region's bargaining position against Tehran is structurally weak.
**Author's own settled read, 2026-09-28**: the northern section (East Azerbaijan, Ardabil, and West
Azerbaijan's Azeri-majority portion) stays part of Iran/Persia — not a live partition question the way the
Kurdish Nation is, though the underlying cultural/linguistic grievance is real and could still be developed
as its own later story (language rights, a resurgent pan-Turkist movement given renewed contact with the
Republic of Azerbaijan and Turkey via this project's own Pan-Turkic sphere) without requiring territorial
separation to be dramatically real.

### 3.9 Khanaqin — a second Kirkuk-class dispute, settled 2026-09-28

⚠️ **Added after the author spotted an odd shape on the rendered map**: a narrow salient of Iraq's Diyala
Governorate reaching northeast up to the Iranian border, wedged between Sulaymaniyah/Halabja (Kurdish Nation
core) and Iran's own Kurdish-flagged provinces (Kurdish Nation domain). That salient is **Khanaqin District**,
and it is not a rendering artifact — Diyala's real-world shape genuinely includes this panhandle.

**Khanaqin carries the identical legal status Kirkuk does.** It is formally listed among the "disputed
territories of Northern Iraq" under **Article 140 of the Iraqi Constitution** — the same three-step
normalization/census/referendum process (deadline nominally 31 December 2007, still unimplemented as of this
survey's research date) that governs Kirkuk's status. It is **de facto split in administration** between
Diyala Governorate (federal Iraq) and Sulaymaniyah Governorate (KRG) — again, structurally the same pattern as
Kirkuk. Population is genuinely mixed: Khanaqin District itself holds roughly 90,000 Kurds, Arabs, and
Turkmens, Sunni and Shia together; the wider disputed area in Diyala runs to an estimated 238,000 people.
**Confidence: high** — Wikipedia's Khanaqin District and Disputed territories of Northern Iraq articles,
Kirkuknow and Rudaw reporting on recent (2025) administrative tensions, and Article 140's own Wikipedia page
converge.

**✅ Settled 2026-09-28: marked Disputed Territory, same tier as Kirkuk — with a disclosed resolution
tradeoff.** Unlike Kirkuk, which is disputed as a *whole governorate* and can be colored as one clean unit,
Khanaqin is only a *district* within Diyala — the rest of Diyala is not disputed territory. This project only
has governorate-level (Natural Earth admin-1) geometry for Iraq, with no district-level shapefile available to
carve Khanaqin's real boundary out on its own (the same resolution wall that forced Lebanon's confessional
mosaic to be dropped rather than faked, §4.3). Rather than leave this disclosed-but-invisible the way the
Lebanon case was, the author chose to mark **all of Diyala** as Disputed Territory — the same "clean shape over
perfect fidelity" tradeoff already used elsewhere in this project (the Taurus-Zagros redraw, Cascadia and the
CSA collapsing multiple Woodard regions). This visibly overstates the dispute's true geographic extent (most of
Diyala outside Khanaqin is uncontested Arab-Sunni territory) in exchange for actually showing it on the map at
all, rather than a geometrically precise but invisible flag.

### 3.10 Raqqa — the weakest demographic case for Kurdish Nation domain status, corrected 2026-09-28

⚠️ **Added after the author asked about the area between Ayn Issa and Lake Assad** — a strip of territory that
runs through the heart of Raqqa Governorate, including Raqqa city itself. This survey's earlier §3.7 treatment
had placed the entire Raqqa Governorate in Kurdish Nation's domain, alongside Al-Hasakah — this section revisits
that call with real demographic sourcing.

**Raqqa Governorate is roughly 90% Sunni Arab.** Kurds, Turkmens, Circassians, and Armenians together make up
only about 10% of the population (the Armenian community alone is roughly 2,000 people). Even Tell Abyad
District — the governorate's most-Kurdish district, and home to Ayn Issa — is still only about 70% Arab, 25%
Kurdish, and 5% Turkmen, with the Kurdish population itself concentrated specifically in the district's western
part rather than spread evenly. Ayn Issa town itself is predominantly Arab (~85%), with Kurds at roughly 15%,
despite having served as a major military and political headquarters for the SDF and the Democratic Autonomous
Administration of North and East Syria (DAANES) until 2026. **Confidence: high** — EUAA's Syria country guidance,
the Washington Institute's reporting on Tell Abyad, MEI's tribal mapping of Raqqa, and Wikipedia's Ayn Issa and
Tell Abyad District articles converge.

**This is a different kind of case from every other Kurdish-domain redraw this survey has made.** The
Tunceli/Bingöl/Muş cut (§3.7 addendum) and the Ilam discussion (open, not yet settled) both involve provinces
that are genuinely Kurdish-majority or -plurality, where the tension is between real demographics and some other
factor (geology, in Tunceli's case). Raqqa has no such tension to disclose — the demographic case for Kurdish
Nation status here was never strong to begin with. Its original inclusion appears to have been tracking **SDF/
AANES political and military control** of the area (Raqqa was SDF-held from 2017 and functioned as a DAANES
administrative center) rather than who actually lives there — a conflation of governance with culture/ethnicity
that runs against this survey package's own stated method of drawing the latter as the primary map layer.

**✅ Settled 2026-09-28: Raqqa moved out of Kurdish Nation's domain entirely, into Levant's Arab core.**
Al-Hasakah — Raqqa's former domain-mate — is unaffected by this and remains in Kurdish Nation's domain; it is
Syria's actual historic Kurdish heartland (the Jazira region), with genuine Kurdish plurality/near-majority in
parts, and does not share Raqqa's weak demographic profile.

### 3.11 Nineveh (Mosul) — real disputed periphery, flagged but NOT rendered, settled 2026-09-28

**Nineveh's disputed periphery, same Article 140 list Khanaqin sits on:**

otherwise uncontroversial governorate, so marking the whole governorate contested was a bounded, disclosed
overstatement. Nineveh's capital is **Mosul itself** — a massive, unambiguously Sunni Arab city that was never
seriously part of the KRG's Article 140 claims, and whose recapture from ISIS by federal Iraqi forces in 2017
only reinforced Baghdad's control there. Marking the entire governorate contested would paint Mosul proper as
disputed alongside Sinjar and the Nineveh Plains — a considerably bigger overstatement than Diyala's, given how
large and how clearly-not-disputed the governorate's own capital is.

governorate-level contested status would misrepresent Mosul specifically.

## Section 4 — The Sunni-Shia split

### 4.1 Origin

The split traces to a specific, datable event: the **Battle of Karbala, 680 CE**, in what is now southern
Iraq, where forces of the Sunni Umayyad Caliphate killed Husayn ibn Ali — grandson of the Prophet Muhammad and
son of Ali, the fourth caliph whose disputed succession had already split the early Muslim community. Husayn's
death at Karbala became the founding martyrdom narrative of Shia Islam, commemorated annually on Ashoura, the
most solemn date on the Shia calendar. Sunni Islam holds that leadership of the community was never limited to
Ali's bloodline; Shia Islam holds that it was. **This is a real, 1,300-year-old religious-historical fact, the
same category of load-bearing history as the mainland Southeast Asia survey's Indianization or the East Asia
survey's Mandarin dialect splits** — not a modern political invention retroactively dressed in religious
language.

### 4.2 Modern distribution, by this survey's country list

| Country | Shia share | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Iraq** | 65–70% | Shia majority governs a country with a real, recent Sunni-insurgency history (the 2003–2011 war, ISIS's 2014–2017 rise) |
| **Bahrain** | ~50% ("nearly half of Muslims") | Shia-majority population under a Sunni monarchy — a real, documented, structural tension |

sectarian geopolitics generally.

### 4.3 Lebanon's confessional system — the one place sect is the constitution, not just demography

Lebanon's political system formalizes sectarian identity into the state structure itself, via the unwritten
**National Pact of 1943** (Maronite Christian president Bechara El Khoury and Sunni Muslim prime minister Riad
El Solh, at independence from France): the president must be a **Maronite Christian**, the prime minister a
**Sunni Muslim**, and the speaker of parliament a **Shia Muslim**, with parliamentary seats similarly allotted
by sect. Critics (cited across France 24, Atalayar, and CJPME) describe the system as having entrenched the
power of sectarian family/party networks and paralyzed state functioning — a real, current, documented
critique, not merely a historical curiosity. **This is a genuinely distinctive governance structure within
this survey's scope**, with no equivalent elsewhere in the fifteen countries: sect is not just demography here,
it is written into the office-holding rules themselves.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29: the map's own confessional hatch, dropped entirely during Stage 4, has been partially
restored.** The original note ("Natural Earth's 6-polygon Lebanon geometry... hatching by sub-tier produced
overlapping, misleading texture") turned out to overstate the problem — closer inspection found the conflict
is real for only one of the three groups. South and Nabatieh governorates each have their own clean,
unshared NE polygon (no conflict at all); Mount Lebanon and Keserwan-Jbeil share one polygon, but both are
genuinely Maronite-Christian-heavy, so flagging the shared shape is accurate rather than a stretch. The one
real, unresolvable conflict is Baalbek-Hermel, which shares its sole NE polygon with Beqaa proper (the Zahle
area) — Beqaa proper is not Shia-majority, so painting that whole shared shape Shia would misrepresent it.
Baalbek-Hermel's real Shia population (part of this section's own 28% national figure) is disclosed in
`subdivisions.csv`'s own note rather than rendered. Applied to `middle_east_classify.py` (new `lebanon`/shia
and `lebanon`/maronite tiers) and `regions.csv`. Re-rendered and verified via the render pipeline's own live
coordinate data (no tier conflicts on any shared geometry) and a zoomed crop of the actual output, which shows
the north (Akkar/North) correctly plain, the coastal Mount Lebanon/Keserwan-Jbeil belt correctly cross-hatched,
and the south (South/Nabatieh) correctly diagonal-hatched.

### 4.4 Iraq's internal geography — confirmed as-is, 2026-09-29

**✅ SETTLED**: after a walkthrough of Iraq's current map treatment, the author confirmed it stands as
officially depicted — no border changes, just documented here for future reference (prose, further worldbuilding,
or a later map-polish pass).

**The shape, geographically, north to south**: Iraq's territory splits into four pieces once Kurdistan's own
core (Erbil, Duhok, Sulaymaniyah, Halabja — the settled KRI) is set aside.

- **The contested pair, both under Article 140's disputed-territories process** (§3.9's own Khanaqin writeup
  has the full reasoning): **Kirkuk**, directly adjacent to Kurdistan's core, and **Diyala**, more central-east
  — bordering Baghdad to the southwest and Iran to the east, sitting between Baghdad and Kirkuk/Kurdistan
  rather than further south. Both render with the same grid cross-hatch texture, correctly identical to each
  other despite sitting at different latitudes.
- **The Sunni-mixed north/center, unflagged**: Al-Anbar (the vast western desert), Salah Al-Din (Tikrit,
  Saddam's home region), Nineveh (Mosul — its own real disputed periphery, Sinjar and the Nineveh Plains,
  researched and deliberately left unrendered, §3.11), and **Baghdad itself — the weakest fit in this whole
  split**. Baghdad is genuinely a mixed Sunni/Shia city (with real sectarian residential sorting since the
  2006-08 civil war, but no clean majority the way the other 12 non-Kurdish, non-contested governorates have);
  it sits in this bucket by default rather than for a researched demographic reason, and is flagged here as
  such rather than silently presented as a settled call.
- **The Shia south, 9 governorates**: Basra, Dhi Qar, Maysan, Muthanna, Najaf, Karbala, Babil, Al-Qadisiyyah,
  and **Wasit** — the historic Shia Arab heartland, including the two holiest cities in Shia Islam (Najaf and
  Karbala). Wasit itself (capital Al-Kut, ~1.64 million people per its 2024 census, bordering Iran directly)
  is a representative case: genuinely Shia-majority with two small, currently-unflagged minority pockets (Marsh
  Arabs in its wetlands, Feyli Kurds around the eastern town of Badra), a real historical name (from Arabic
  "middle" — an early Islamic-era city, founded ~702 CE, that once sat midway between Baghdad and Basra along
  the Tigris before the river's course shifted and the original settlement was abandoned), and real conflict
  history of its own (heavy Iran-Iraq War fighting given its border position; a brief 2004 Mahdi Army seizure
  of Kut before U.S. forces retook it).

**Confidence: high** on Wasit's specific facts (Wikipedia's Wasit Governorate article, 2024 census figure);
**high** on the general Sunni/Shia geographic pattern (converges with §4.2's national figures); **low/flagged**
specifically on Baghdad's own default placement, which is disclosed as a convenience bucket, not a researched
sectarian-majority claim.

### 4.5 Not resolved here

This survey takes no position on whether or how sectarian geography should inform any future map-data
decision. It records the demographic facts because they are real, sourced, and load-bearing to how a
post-collapse (or any other future-history) version of this region would plausibly fracture or hold together —
the same posture the East Asia survey took toward the Hakka or the Maritime Southeast Asia survey took toward
Maluku's Christian/Muslim line.

## Section 5 — The Arabian Peninsula: tribal confederation and oil monarchy

### 5.1 Saudi Arabia's founding — a real theocratic-dynastic alliance, not a colonial handover

Unlike the Fertile Crescent states in §1, Saudi Arabia's modern shape was never mandated territory — it is the
product of a specific 20th-century conquest by a specific dynasty allied to a specific religious movement.
**The Al Saud–Wahhabi alliance** traces to the **1744 Diriyah agreement** between Muhammad ibn Saud and the
religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, founding the First Saudi State on a program of strict Islamic
observance. After the House of Saud's exile in British-protected Kuwait (from 1893, following defeat by the
rival Rashidi dynasty), **Ibn Saud recaptured Riyadh in 1902** and spent three decades — aided by the
**Ikhwan**, a Wahhabi-inspired military force of settled Bedouin, organized from 1912 outside normal tribal
loyalty structures — conquering the peninsula. He took Mecca and Medina and was proclaimed King of Hejaz in
1926, ruling Hejaz and Najd as separate units until **unifying them as the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932**.
**Confidence: high** (Britannica, Wikipedia's Unification of Saudi Arabia and Founding of Saudi Arabia articles
converge).

### 5.2 The Gulf Cooperation Council — six monarchies, one real internal wealth tier

The **GCC** (Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE) controls roughly **30% of the world's proven oil
reserves** and had a combined 2023 population of roughly 59.6 million. **2026 GDP per capita, ranked**: Qatar
$87,661; UAE $53,916; Saudi Arabia $28,457; Kuwait $27,345; Bahrain $26,562; Oman $21,897 — every member wealthy
by world standards, but with real internal variation (Qatar is roughly **four times** Oman's per-capita
figure). Diversification away from hydrocarbons also varies sharply: UAE's non-oil GDP share is ~73%; Saudi
Arabia's ~52% (rising under its Vision 2030 program); Kuwait's is the GCC's lowest, hydrocarbons still ~55% of
its economy. The UAE's combined sovereign-wealth-fund holdings (ADIA, Mubadala, ADQ, and others) exceed
**$1.56 trillion**. **Confidence: high**, though note these are all point-in-time 2026 figures for a
resource-price-sensitive economic bloc — treat as a snapshot, not a fixed ranking.

### 5.2.1 Settled 2026-09-29: "Arabia" splits into named countries, weighed one by one

⚠️ **"Arabia" was the last generic multi-country cultural-bloc label left on this map** — all six GCC monarchies
sharing one color and name, the same shape of inconsistency "The Levant" and "Anatolia/Mesopotamia/Persia" were
before being split into their own real country names elsewhere in this survey. The author weighed each
country's real case for continued independence directly, rather than splitting them all by default.

**Qatar — settled staying independent.** The highest GDP per capita in the *world* (driven by being the
largest LNG exporter), and a demonstrated willingness and ability to resist Saudi coercion specifically: the
**2017–2021 Saudi-led blockade of Qatar** (joined by the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt) was a real, serious attempt
to force Qatar into line, and Qatar weathered it without capitulating. Also hosts the US Al Udeid Air Base, an
external security guarantee independent of Saudi Arabia. Real vulnerability disclosed: its own citizenry is a
small minority of the country's population, and geographically it is a peninsula whose only land border is
with Saudi Arabia itself.

**Oman — settled staying independent.** The most genuinely distinct identity case of the whole bloc: roughly
**45% of Oman's Muslim population is Ibadi** — a real third branch of Islam, neither Sunni nor Shia,
historically found almost nowhere else at this scale, alongside a roughly equal 45% Sunni share and ~5% Shia.
A former maritime empire in its own right (once ruled Zanzibar and the East African coast), with a real,
maintained independent/neutral foreign policy (mediating Iran-US backchannels, for instance) and control of the
Musandam Peninsula overlooking the Strait of Hormuz — genuine strategic leverage. Real vulnerability disclosed:
the poorest of the six by 2026 GDP per capita ($21,897).

**Kuwait and Bahrain — settled absorbed into a Saudi successor state.** Kuwait's strongest evidence is
*negative*: Iraq's 1990 invasion fully occupied it, restored only by an international coalition, not its own
strength; it also shares long land borders with both Saudi Arabia and Iraq, and the two once shared a literal
"Neutral Zone" with Saudi before it was formally divided. Bahrain is close to a de facto Saudi dependency
already today: physically connected by the **King Fahd Causeway** (built for exactly this kind of
integration), and in **2011 Saudi Arabia and the UAE sent troops directly into Bahrain** (the Peninsula Shield
Force) to help its Sunni monarchy suppress a Shia-majority uprising — opposition figures called it "an
occupation" outright.

**A real historical reason the successor state needed a new name, not "Saudi Arabia."** The current kingdom
is actually the **Third Saudi State** — the Al Saud dynasty was destroyed once by Ottoman-Egyptian forces
(1818, ending the **First Saudi State**/Emirate of Diriyah) and defeated a second time by the rival Rashidi
dynasty (1891, forcing exile in Kuwait, ending the **Second Saudi State**/Emirate of Nejd) before Abdul-Aziz Al
Saud fought back from that same Kuwaiti exile starting in 1902. See §5.2.2 for the settled name.

**UAE — settled staying independent, see §5.2.3.**

**Applied to the map**: `middle_east_classify.py`'s `REGIONS` dict and `classify()` function, and
`middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s new `qatar`, `oman`, and `uae` regions (the Kuwait/Bahrain/Saudi successor
region is `nejd_hejaz`, named in §5.2.2). Both standalone and composite maps re-rendered and verified via the
render pipeline's own live coordinate data (Qatar, Bahrain, UAE, and Oman all confirmed classifying and
rendering correctly, including Bahrain's shia_majority hatch carrying over intact into the new Saudi successor

### 5.2.2 Settled 2026-09-29: the Saudi successor state is named the Kingdom of Nejd and Hejaz

Two real historical/geographic names, older than "Saudi Arabia" and not tied to the ruling family, were
researched as candidates: **Nejd** (the actual historical core region/heartland across all three Saudi states)
and **Hejaz** (the western coastal region with Mecca and Medina — a genuinely *separate* Hashemite-ruled
kingdom until conquered in 1925/26). Rather than picking one and dropping the other, the author settled on the
combined form: **the Kingdom of Nejd and Hejaz** — not an invented name but the dynasty's own **real historical
name from 1926–1932**, the interim state Abdul-Aziz Al Saud ruled after uniting the two regions and before
rebranding it "Saudi Arabia" in 1932. This keeps the project's standing convention (real historical names over
invented ones — see "Persia," "Arabistan") intact for its final Gulf naming decision.

Two follow-up questions the author raised and settled directly:

**Does the name need to cover 100% of the successor state's actual territory?** No — the same real 1926–1932
kingdom didn't cover Kuwait, Bahrain, or the Eastern Province either, and neither this project's own "Persia"
nor the real "Saudi Arabia" are literal, complete descriptions of every province each governs. A kingdom's name
naming its historical core, not an exhaustive gazetteer of its current borders, is the norm, not an exception.

**Is "Kingdom" — a monarchical government — characteristically consistent for this successor state, if it's
not necessarily the same Al Saud bloodline in charge?** Yes, and this is a genuinely strong regional pattern,
not a weak analogy: every real Gulf state today (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman) is a
monarchy — zero republics among them, even as the wider Arab world's mid-20th-century republican wave toppled
the monarchies of Iraq, Egypt, Libya, and North Yemen, and even through the 2011 Arab Spring. More specifically,
*both* prior Saudi-state collapses were followed by continued dynastic/tribal rule rather than any republican
turn: the 1891 collapse handed the interior to a *different* ruling house entirely (the rival Rashidi dynasty,
based in Ha'il) — still a monarchy, just not Al Saud — until Abdul-Aziz fought his way back from Kuwaiti exile.
The historical pattern here is "tribal-confederation political culture reverts to some dynastic/monarchical
form after a collapse," not "the Al Saud family specifically always survives" — so the author's own framing
(governmental form settled as Kingdom; which specific bloodline holds it, deliberately left open) fits the real
precedent cleanly. Contrast with Persia (§5-adjacent, this project's own deliberate move *away* from monarchy
toward secular-republican identity post-collapse) — the two regions are making opposite, equally-grounded
choices, not the same choice applied inconsistently.

**Applied to the map**: `middle_east_classify.py`'s `REGIONS` dict region_id renamed `saudi_arabia` →
`nejd_hejaz` (display name "Kingdom of Nejd and Hejaz," color unchanged, `#A88148`); `classify()`'s Gulf-states
block updated to return `nejd_hejaz` for Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Bahrain. `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s
three former `saudi_arabia` rows (core, shia_flag, shia_majority) renamed to `nejd_hejaz` with updated notes.
Both `middle-east-render.py` and `west-asia-composite-render.py` updated (legend confidence dicts, label
positions, legend-order lists) and both maps re-rendered.

### 5.2.3 Settled 2026-09-29: the UAE stays fully independent, and Abu Dhabi gets a neutral diplomatic district

Unlike Kuwait (whose independence case rested on negative evidence) or Bahrain (already close to a de facto
Saudi dependency), the UAE's case turned out to be the *strongest* of the whole former bloc — stronger even
than Qatar's — because the friction with the Nejd/Hejaz successor state is live, current, and severe rather
than hypothetical.

**A real, current (2025–2026) rupture, not old history.** The Yemen war already split the two once: the UAE
withdrew its own forces in July 2019 over strategy disagreements, and by December 2025 UAE-backed southern
separatists had seized territory in southern Yemen outright, prompting a direct Saudi demand for full Emirati
withdrawal. Real 2021 and 2023 OPEC+ production-quota disputes were bitter enough that the Saudi crown prince
reportedly accused the UAE of "stabbing us in the back"; the UAE has its own planned 2026 departure from OPEC
altogether. The two currently back *opposite* sides of Sudan's civil war (Saudi Arabia backs the Sudanese Armed
Forces; the UAE backs the rival Rapid Support Forces). Observers already describe this as a "rupture," with
Saudi state media running what one outlet called a "smear campaign" against the UAE's regional role.

**Independent military and foreign policy, already exercised, not hypothetical.** The UAE runs its own forward
military bases abroad (Al-Khadim airport in Libya, Assab in Eritrea), has held its own Defense Cooperation
Agreement with the US since 1994, and made its own unilateral choice to normalize relations with Israel via the
2020 Abraham Accords — well ahead of Saudi Arabia, which still hasn't formally followed.

**A real 50-plus-year track record as its own successful federation.** When Britain withdrew in 1971, Bahrain
and Qatar each walked away from the same union talks to become their own separate states; the UAE is what's
left of that negotiation — six (later seven) emirates who chose federation deliberately and have functioned as
one recognized sovereign country ever since, longer than most of the region's current borders have existed.

**Real vulnerabilities disclosed, not hidden.** Only ~12% of the population are native Emirati citizens — the
same small-citizen-base issue already disclosed, not disqualifying, for Qatar. Abu Dhabi dominates the internal
federation (~95% of UAE oil, ~92% of its gas, ~2/3 of the whole economy, and the permanent federal presidency
through the Al Nahyan family) while Dubai's own 2009 Dubai World debt crisis ($59 billion in debt, a
market-shaking "standstill" announcement, a $10 billion Abu Dhabi bailout that Abu Dhabi explicitly kept
"case-by-case" rather than open-ended) showed real fragility in Dubai's own growth model — an internal
Abu Dhabi/Dubai imbalance, not evidence the federation itself can't stand on its own externally. And Saudi
Arabia has a real, specific historical territorial claim on Abu Dhabi itself: on the eve of the UAE's 1971
independence, Saudi Arabia "laid claim to swathes of Abu Dhabi" territory, resolved only by a 1974 agreement —
left resolved by that same real agreement here, not reopened.

**Abu Dhabi's neutral diplomatic district.** The author's own addition: within the capital city of Abu Dhabi,
one small district is fully and completely neutral ground — a place where any party, regardless of who else
they're at war with, can openly negotiate and argue diplomacy and peace deals. This is below the map's own
whole-first-order-subdivision rendering rule (a sub-city district, not a full emirate), so it isn't drawn as
its own map region — it's documented as lore, in `subdivisions.csv`'s own "Abu Dhabi" note, the same way
Jerusalem's own neutral-city status is documented at the (larger) region scale. It fits, rather than invents,
the UAE's own real-world reputation as a diplomatic backchannel hub (Abu Dhabi and Dubai already host real
Iran-US and other back-channel diplomacy today).

**Applied to the map**: `middle_east_classify.py`'s `REGIONS` dict region_id renamed `arabia` → `uae` (display
name "United Arab Emirates," color unchanged, `#B9975B`); `classify()`'s United Arab Emirates case now returns
`uae`. `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s former `arabia` row renamed to `uae` with the full case above;
`subdivisions.csv`'s "Abu Dhabi" row carries the new neutral-district note. Both `middle-east-render.py` and
`west-asia-composite-render.py` updated (legend confidence dicts — raised from low to high confidence — label
positions, legend-order lists) for the `arabia` → `uae` region_id rename, and both maps re-rendered.

### 5.3 Jordan — the Hashemite Kingdom

**Note on this section's old title**: an earlier pass of this survey grouped Jordan under "the republics
outside the GCC" — factually wrong, since Jordan is a monarchy, not a republic. Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon (the
actual non-GCC republics) are already covered in §1 (Sykes-Picot/Mandate origins), §2 and §3 (Kurdish
question), and §4 (Sunni-Shia split/Lebanon's confessional system) — not repeated here. Jordan gets its own
subsection because, unlike those three, it had no dedicated treatment anywhere else in this survey until now.

**Founding**: Jordan is a direct product of the same post-Ottoman settlement §1 describes for its neighbors.
**Sharif Hussein bin Ali** of Mecca led the 1916 Arab Revolt against the Ottomans in exchange for British
promises of postwar Arab independence — promises Sykes-Picot itself already broke (§1). His son **Abdullah I**
arrived in the territory in November 1920 and, on **11 April 1921**, was installed as Emir of the newly
created **Emirate of Transjordan**, administratively split off from the British Mandate for Palestine. Jordan
became fully independent as the **Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan** on 25 May 1946, with Abdullah I proclaimed
king. The current monarch, King Abdullah II, is a direct descendant. **Confidence: high** (Wikipedia:
Establishment of the Emirate of Transjordan; Abdullah I of Jordan; History of Jordan).

**The Palestinian-majority population — Jordan's own load-bearing internal fact.** By the late 1960s,
Palestinians made up roughly **60% of Jordan's population** — about 1 million people — the result of two
refugee waves: roughly 700,000 displaced by the 1948 war (the Nakba, §2.2) and a second wave after the 1967
war. This is not a minor minority-politics footnote; it is a demographic majority-or-near-majority governed
by a non-Palestinian royal family, structurally comparable in shape (though not in every particular) to
Bahrain's Sunni-monarchy-over-Shia-majority case (§4) or Iraq's Sunni-minority rule under Saddam Hussein
before 2003. **Black September (September 1970 – June 1971)** was the resulting civil war: the Palestine
Liberation Organization had built up an armed, quasi-autonomous presence inside Jordan (sometimes called
"Fatahland") that increasingly challenged the monarchy's own authority; King Hussein's army fought the PLO's
fedayeen directly, expelling them into Lebanon by mid-1971 — where PLO forces then destabilized *that*
country's own confessional balance (§4.3), a direct causal chain between two of this survey's country
sections. **Confidence: high** (Wikipedia: Black September; Jordan–Palestine relations; multiple corroborating
histories).

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29, following directly from the Israel/Palestine post-collapse scenario (§2.7)**: this
survey had been developing a possible internal flag for Jordan's own Palestinian-population geography —
concentrated, per real sourcing, in Amman, Zarqa, and Irbid governorates specifically, with Jerash and Balqa as
secondary concentrations (Jordan's two largest Palestinian refugee camps, Baqa'a and Al-Wehdat, sit in exactly
this belt) — mirroring the internal-minority-flag treatment used elsewhere on this map. **That flag is now
declined, not applied, for a specific reason rather than left simply unresolved**: with Palestine now holding
vastly more territory and (implicitly) the capacity to receive people back, following the collapse of the
Israeli state, the author's own call is that **Jordan's Palestinian refugee population returns to Palestine**
— reframing this population's decades in Jordan as displacement pending return rather than permanent
settlement. Author's own words: "Jordan's Palestinian refugees are able to return to Palestine, thus making
Jordan unquestionably Jordanian-majority." This resolves the Azraq/Ruwaished repartition question from the same
research pass in the same direction it was already trending (both towns showed real Jordanian-citizen
majorities) — with the broader return migration, Jordan's whole demographic character reverts to the East Bank
Bedouin-tribal majority the monarchy's own base has always drawn from, and no internal Palestinian-concentration
flag is needed. Not further specified: the scale, timeline, or mechanics of the return migration itself
(voluntary/negotiated/rapid or gradual) — flagged as a real premise for later development, not built out here.

**Water scarcity**: Jordan is one of the most water-scarce countries on Earth by per-capita renewable supply —
sourced estimates put it at roughly **60–61 cubic meters per person per year**, and as of 2026 the kingdom
consumes an estimated 1.2 billion cubic meters annually against supplies below 900 million cubic meters, a
structural deficit. This is a **separate water system from §8's Tigris-Euphrates/GAP case** — Jordan's crisis
centers on the Jordan River and Yarmouk River basins (shared with Israel/Palestine and Syria, not researched
further this pass) and on groundwater depletion, plus population pressure from hosting large refugee
populations (below). The kingdom's flagship response is the **National Conveyance Project**, a Red Sea/Gulf of
Aqaba desalination-and-pipeline scheme intended to supply roughly 40% of drinking water once complete.
**Confidence: medium-high** (multiple 2025-2026 sources converge on the scarcity figures; the exact
supply/demand numbers are current-year estimates, not multi-year-averaged data).

**Material position**: Jordan has essentially no hydrocarbon wealth of its own, unlike most of its neighbors in
§5, and runs a service-dominated economy (services >70% of output). It hosts roughly 400,000+ UNHCR-registered
refugees, overwhelmingly Syrian — a real, current fiscal burden the World Bank estimates at **over $2.5
billion a year, roughly 6% of GDP and 25% of annual government revenue**, though the same refugee population
also brings substantial international aid and some labor-market benefit (manufacturing, agriculture). Public
debt exceeds 90% of GDP; youth unemployment runs around 40%. Jordan is structurally aid-dependent — a 2025
suspension of USAID funding alone was estimated to cost roughly 35,000 Jordanian and American jobs.
**Confidence: high** on the figures cited (World Bank, UNHCR, and multiple 2025-2026 economic-reporting
sources converge); note all dollar/percentage figures are current-year snapshots for a fast-moving aid and
refugee situation, not stable multi-year averages.

**Relative stability**: despite bordering three of this survey's most volatile cases (Syria, Iraq, and
Israel/Palestine, §2) and hosting one of the region's largest per-capita refugee burdens, Jordan has not had a
civil war or lost central government control since Black September, and the monarchy weathered the 2011 Arab
Spring protests without regime change (unlike Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria, or Yemen). **This survey does not
have a researched explanation for why** — flagged as a real open question, not asserted as a settled finding,
since no source specifically addressing Hashemite regime resilience was reviewed this pass.

### 5.4 Yemen — the peninsula's own internal fracture, currently active

Yemen sits on the same peninsula as the wealthy GCC states but is, by most world rankings, among the poorest
countries on Earth — and, as of this survey's date, is fighting an active three-way civil war. The
**Presidential Leadership Council (PLC)**, Saudi-backed, is the internationally recognized government
(replacing the earlier Hadi government in 2022). The **Houthi movement** (Ansar Allah), rooted in Zaidi Shia
identity and controlling the northern highlands and, as of September 2026, **the entire Red Sea coast**
(having retaken Perim Island and completed its hold on the Hanish Islands, with only the Midi district on the
west coast still outside its control), launched a major offensive against the PLC on 3 September 2026 — the
deadliest Yemeni fighting since a 2022 ceasefire collapsed, and one that has become entangled with the wider
2026 Iran war (§6). The **Southern Transitional Council (STC)**, a secessionist movement founded 2017 seeking
an independent South Yemen, briefly captured most non-Houthi territory in early 2026 before losing it and
being removed from the PLC; Saudi Arabia accused the UAE of arming the STC in a bid for Yemen's eastern
governorates and Aden (the PLC's own seat), leading to a Saudi military move against the STC and a UAE
withdrawal — though STC activists continue resisting both the PLC and Saudi Arabia. **This is a live, three-way,
currently-fighting civil war, reported here as a snapshot, not a resolution** — the same posture this survey
takes toward every other live conflict in scope.

**⚠️ Chronology clarified 2026-09-28**: the STC actually held Aden continuously since 2018 before losing it —
PLC (Saudi-backed) forces retook the city on **7 January 2026**, and the STC announced its own dissolution two
days later (9 January), disputed by some of its own members; its leader fled to the UAE. The STC-resistance
activity described above (briefly recapturing non-Houthi territory before being fully removed) reads as a
continuation of that same unresolved dissolution, not a separate episode.

### 5.5 Settled 2026-09-28: Yemen splits into two states, not one — the historical norm, not the exception

⚠️ **Added after the author asked directly what Yemen's history has "generally been"** — the answer reframes
the whole question: **a unified Republic of Yemen is the young, unstable anomaly; division along almost
exactly this same line is the deep historical default.**

**North Yemen's predecessor, a Zaydi Shia Imamate, ruled the highlands for the better part of a millennium** —
continuously, in various dynastic forms, from roughly the breakup of the early Islamic caliphate until **1962**
— interrupted only by loose Ottoman suzerainty that ended in 1918. North Yemen was never Western-colonized.
**South Yemen had a completely different, entirely separate colonial trajectory**: Britain captured Aden in
**1839** and held it — first as part of British India, then as a Crown Colony from 1937 — for **128 straight
years**, until 1967 independence produced the **People's Democratic Republic of Yemen**, the only Marxist
state that ever existed in the Arab world.

**Unification happened only on 22 May 1990**, and has never actually been stable: a civil war just four years
later (1994) when the south tried to leave again; the Houthi takeover of the north in 2014; the STC's rival
southern administration from 2018 to its contested January 2026 dissolution (§5.4 above); and the current live
Houthi offensive since September 2026. **Every decade since unification has produced a real attempt to
re-divide it.** Confidence: high, multiple corroborating sources on both the Zaydi Imamate's duration and the
Aden colonial timeline.

**✅ Author's settled decision**: Yemen is drawn as two states — **North Yemen** and **South Yemen** — following
the real pre-1990 YAR/PDRY political border, not the Zaydi-highland/Shafi'i-lowland religious split this
survey's own material previously used as a proxy (the two overlap substantially but are not identical — Ibb
and Ta'izz, for example, are Shafi'i-majority religiously but were politically North Yemen). **One governorate
does not divide cleanly**: **Dhale (Al Dali')** did not exist before 1990/1998 — it was created by stitching
together pieces of both former states (5 of its original 9 districts from South Yemen/Lahij, 4 from North
Yemen/Ibb and Ta'izz), sitting exactly on the old frontier near the historic Aden Protectorate boundary, and
was, before either state existed, the seat of its own local polity, the Emirate of Dhala. **Settled as
officially recognized Disputed Territory between the two new states** — the same Kirkuk-style treatment used
elsewhere in this survey, and the same structural case as Kaliningrad (Russia survey) or the Fergana enclaves
(Central Asia survey): a real subdivision that cannot be cleanly assigned without erasing genuine mixed
history. Applied to `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`.

**Sources**: [Yemeni unification — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemeni_unification); [South Yemen — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Yemen); [Yemen Arab Republic — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen_Arab_Republic); [Dhale Governorate — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhale_Governorate); [Fall of Aden (2026) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Aden_(2026)); [Yemen Governorates — statoids.com](https://statoids.com/uye.html).

### 5.6 Socotra — researched properly for the first time, confirmed staying part of South Yemen

⚠️ **Added 2026-09-29, closing out a flag this survey had carried since Stage 4 without researching it to the
depth Dhale or the North/South split got.**

**Scale**: genuinely small — the main island is ~3,665 km² (the whole archipelago ~3,796 km²), roughly the size
of Long Island, NY. Population is only about 60,000, almost all on the main island.

**Two real ties, pointing in different directions.** Ethnically and linguistically, Socotrans are indigenous
**Mahra** people speaking **Soqotri**, a Modern South Arabian language closely related to **Mehri** — the
language spoken in **Al Mahrah**, an actual mainland South Yemen governorate. This is a specific, real cultural
tie to Yemen, not a vague geographic one. Politically, however, current real-world control is the **UAE's**:
UAE troops seized Socotra's airport and seaport in April 2018, and in **June 2020 the UAE-backed Southern
Transitional Council staged an actual coup**, ousting local authorities and installing its own government —
with documented repression since (peaceful protests against the coup violently dispersed, journalists
arrested).

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29**: stays part of South Yemen, resolved on the same principle that already corrected
Raqqa's placement earlier in this survey (§3.10) — this map draws culture and identity, not who currently
controls the ground. Author's own words: "if the people consider themselves Yemenis, then it makes sense that
it would be part of Yemen." The population's own documented resistance to the UAE-backed coup (not welcoming
it) supports that read, rather than contradicting it. UAE's real political/military control is disclosed here
as a live fact, not treated as the basis for classification. Applied to `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s

## Section 7 — Turkey, Afghanistan, and the two bridges to Central Asia

### 7.1 Turkey's founding — a total break, not a continuation

Modern Turkey was founded in 1923 by **Mustafa Kemal Atatürk** from the remnants of the defeated Ottoman
Empire, as a deliberate, total civilizational reorientation: the Ottoman Caliphate was formally abolished on
3 March 1924, the last Caliph exiled, secularism imposed by law, and the new republic's orientation turned
toward Europe rather than continuing the Ottoman Empire's own Middle Eastern/Islamic imperial identity. This
makes Turkey this survey's cleanest example of a genuine "founding break" story — structurally closer to a
Woodard-style deliberate-founding-culture case than almost anything else in this survey's scope, Saudi Arabia's
own very different theocratic-dynastic founding (§5.1) being the other clean case.

**⚠️ Flagged future border seam, added 2026-09-28, explicitly not resolved here**: the author is considering
the real **Tekirdağ/Istanbul provincial border** in East Thrace — running between Tekirdağ's Çorlu/Çerkezköy
and Istanbul Province's Silivri (Büyükkılıçlı/Küçükkılıçlı) — as Anatolia's own eventual western edge, once
this project's Europe region exists to receive the rest of Turkey's European territory (Tekirdağ, Kırklareli,
and Edirne provinces, everything outside Istanbul Province itself). **Explicitly not decided or applied now**
— Bulgaria and Greece are not yet in scope for this project, and the author's own words were "it's not
necessary to start thinking about Greece and/or Bulgaria... we'll get to that once we start doing Europe."
Anatolia keeps all of East Thrace for the time being. This is the same kind of deferred-seam flag Russia's own
map once carried for Outer Manchuria, Sakhalin, and Buryatia/Tuva before East Asia existed to resolve them —
recorded now so it isn't lost, not because it needs deciding yet. Real historical grounding exists here for
whenever this is picked up again: the 1920 Treaty of Sèvres briefly awarded most of East Thrace to Greece
before the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne restored it to Turkey, alongside the large-scale Greek-Turkish population
exchange of that same era — neither fact was independently re-verified this pass, flagged for whenever Europe
work actually begins.

### 7.2 Turkey's own Kurdish history

Covered in full at §3.3 — the PKK's 1978–2025 insurgency and its 2025 ceasefire/disarmament — not repeated
here.

### 7.3 Turkey's bridge position, and the pan-Turkic sphere candidate

Turkey sits geographically and culturally between Europe/the Caucasus and the Middle East/Central Asia, and
this is not merely geographic: Turkish is a **Turkic language**, part of the same language family as
Kazakh, Uzbek, Turkmen, and Kyrgyz (the companion Central Asia survey's own subject countries) and Azerbaijani
(outside this survey's scope but geographically adjacent). **Pan-Turkism** — the real, documented 19th–20th
century political idea of closer cultural or political union among Turkic peoples — has a real modern
institutional expression in the **Organization of Turkic States** (not independently researched this pass,
flagged as a natural next step). **This is a genuine cross-region sphere-overlay candidate**, structurally
analogous to how this project used the Tai Continuum, the Mongolic World, or Linguistic Wallacea elsewhere —
a cultural-linguistic layer that crosses a hard region boundary (here, the West Asia region's own internal
Middle-East/Central-Asia seam) without redrawing any border. **Not proposed as settled geometry here** — only
flagged as available and well-grounded, the same posture the Maritime Southeast Asia survey took toward its
own sphere candidates.

### 7.4 Afghanistan — ethnic composition and its own bridge to Central Asia

Afghanistan's ethnic composition (estimates vary by source, ranges given): **Pashtun 40–50%** (the plurality,
concentrated south and southeast but present nationwide), **Tajik 25–27%** (concentrated north, northeast, and
the Kabul region), **Hazara 9–18%** (central highlands/Hazarajat and Kabul), **Uzbek 6–15%** (north),
**Turkmen 1.5–3%**, **Baluchi ~2%**, with the remainder split among smaller groups. ⚠️ **This survey did not
independently research the current Taliban government's structure or its relationship to this ethnic
breakdown** — a real, flagged gap, given how load-bearing ethnic-composition-versus-governing-group tension has
been to nearly every other stateless/sub-state-nation case in this survey and its companion.

**The direct cross-region tie**: Afghanistan's Tajik, Uzbek, and Turkmen populations are, ethnically and
linguistically, **the same peoples as the titular nationalities of three of the companion Central Asia
survey's five countries** (Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan) — not a distant cultural echo but a
direct population continuity split by a modern border, the same shape of fact as the Kurdish case (§3) or the
Fergana Valley tangle the companion survey names as its own single most load-bearing finding. Afghanistan's
official Dari language is, further, a Persian-family language — giving Afghanistan a **second** real tie, to
Iran's own Persian/Iranic civilizational core, distinct from and additional to its Central Asian ethnic ties.
**Flagged explicitly for whoever does Stage 3 map-data work across both halves of West Asia**: Afghanistan may
be this region's single strongest argument for treating "West Asia" as one connected map-data space rather than
two entirely separate ones, precisely because its own population ties run in two different directions
(Persian/Iranic west, Turkic/Tajik-Persianate north) that this survey's own Central Asia/Middle East split
otherwise keeps apart.

### 7.5 Could Afghanistan's ethnic zones actually survive as separate states? Mixed evidence, "stays whole" confirmed

⚠️ **Added 2026-09-29, testing the author's own hypothesis** that if Afghanistan's ethnic Tier-2 zones (§7.4)
were to actually separate rather than merely contest for control of the whole country (the Northern-Alliance-
style internal-fragmentation premise already flagged in `regions.csv`'s afghanistan/pashtun note), none of the
individual pieces could plausibly survive on their own — meaning the land stays whole either way. Researched
rather than assumed; the evidence is real but mixed, not uniform.

**Hazara (Bamyan, Daykundi) — the hypothesis holds up strongly here.** Both provinces are **entirely
landlocked within Afghanistan's own other provinces** — neither touches any of Afghanistan's six international
neighbors at all. An independent Hazara state would need transit rights *through* whichever other successor
controls the surrounding land just to reach the outside world. Combined with this being historically the
poorest, most marginalized region with no significant documented resource base, this is close to the weakest
possible case for independent viability anywhere on this map.

**Tajik north/northeast (Badakhshan, Takhar, Parwan, Panjshir) — the opposite case.** Badakhshan alone touches
**three international borders** — Tajikistan, China (via the Wakhan Corridor, Afghanistan's only land link to
China), and Pakistan — better international access than several whole countries already on this map.

**Uzbek/Turkmen north (Faryab, Jowzjan, Sar-e Pol) — also stronger than the hypothesis assumes.** Real,
documented natural gas and oil fields sit in this belt, plus direct borders with Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan
for trade access.

**Pashtun south/east — resource-rich on paper, unproven in practice.** Helmand holds rare-earth deposits (the
Khanashin carbonatites) and Logar holds the Mes Aynak copper deposit (~$33 billion estimated reserves) — but
neither has functioning extraction infrastructure; China has held Mes Aynak's rights for years without actually
mining it, given the security conditions. Real geology, not a proven near-term economy — the same caveat
already applied to Afghanistan's mineral claims generally (§8).

**What's true across every piece, and is the real load-bearing reason "stays whole" holds regardless of the
above**: Afghanistan's entire trade economy depends overwhelmingly on just two neighbors (Pakistan 42% of
exports, India 40%). Any fragment inherits that same acute single/dual-neighbor dependency with even less
leverage individually than the whole country already has collectively — economic non-viability alone doesn't
prevent fragmentation in the real world (many poor, landlocked states persist as sovereign entities, propped up
by aid or a patron, the same pattern this project's own Balochistan and Karakalpakstan cases already use), but
it does mean any breakaway piece — Hazara most acutely, but all of them to some degree — would need external
backing to actually function, not just declare independence.

**✅ CONFIRMED 2026-09-29: Afghanistan's territorial extent stays whole**, the author's own prior call (§7.4's
own regions.csv note) reaffirmed rather than reversed by this research. The mixed evidence doesn't overturn the
conclusion — Hazara's genuine landlocked isolation and every piece's shared dependence on the same one or two
neighbors both support it — but it does mean the reasoning is now grounded in real economic/geographic
viability, not only Afghanistan's well-documented historical immunity to outside conquest (§7.4's own note).
The Northern-Alliance-style premise stays exactly as previously flagged: a possible internal power struggle for
control of the whole country, not a clean four- or five-way partition into new sovereign states.

## Section 9 — Balochistan: a new cross-border scenario, settled 2026-09-29

**Author's own framing, following directly from a demographic/social/geological research pass on Persia's own
Sistan and Baluchestan province**: "The Balochi areas of Persia, Afghanistan, and Pakistan successfully unite
and gain control over a seaport." Unlike this survey's other border decisions, this one reaches outside the
project's normal West Asia scope (Pakistan/South Asia was explicitly excluded from the start) specifically for
one province, because the scenario doesn't hold together without it.

### 9.1 The three pieces

- **Iran/Persia's Sistan and Baluchestan** — genuinely Baloch-majority, and Iran's poorest province by most
  rankings. Unusually among this map's minority regions, it is a real "double minority" — ethnically Baloch
  *and* overwhelmingly Sunni in a Shia-majority state — which this survey's own comparative research (§3.8,
  the Iranian Azerbaijan case) flags as structurally the more severe kind of separatist grievance than a purely
  ethnic or purely sectarian minority faces alone. September 30, 2022's "Bloody Friday" in the provincial
  capital Zahedan (state forces killed 100+ protesters) and the active insurgent groups Jundallah and Jaish
  al-Adl (explicitly citing anti-Sunni-Baloch discrimination, describing their attacks as "defensive jihad")
  are real, live, ongoing facts, not historical color. Geologically the province is genuinely two zones: Sistan
  in the north, a flat desert basin fed by the Hirmand/Helmand River; Baluchestan in the south, mountainous
  (the volcanic Taftan, ~4,000m) and warmer, running down to the Makran coast. **Chabahar**, Iran's only
  oceanic port — built as the maritime anchor of India's INSTC trade corridor and an explicit counter to
  Chinese-backed Gwadar just 72 km away in Pakistan — sits inside this province, meaning the "control of a
  seaport" half of the scenario is met without reaching any further than Persia's own existing territory.
- **Pakistan's Balochistan** — 14,894,402 people per Pakistan's 7th Population & Housing Census (2023), the
  country's largest but least densely populated province. This is the piece that makes the scenario more than
  invention: Baloch separatist leaders **formally declared a "Republic of Balochistan" in May 2025** — a real,
  live secession attempt, though **not recognized or supported by the Pakistani government**, which continues
  to fight the insurgency militarily and (jointly with Britain) designates the Balochistan Liberation Army a
  terrorist organization. The BLA's own stated goal has long been a "greater" Balochistan explicitly including
  Iranian and Afghan Baloch — the same unification this scenario builds. Iran and Pakistan currently cooperate
  against Baloch militants on both sides of their shared border (a 2014 pact splitting responsibility); a
  seceding Iranian Baluchestan would break that arrangement, one of several open questions about how such a
  union would actually survive its first years (a friendly external patron drawn to Chabahar's strategic value,
  versus a hostile joint Persia/Pakistan reaction, versus internal fragmentation into competing tribal/militant
  factions given the region's chronic underdevelopment and lack of unified civil administration, are all
  live-and-undecided follow-on questions, not resolved here).
- **Afghanistan's Nimruz** — the necessary geographic link, bordering both Iran's Sistan and Baluchestan (west)
  and Pakistan's Balochistan (south) directly, in addition to Afghanistan's own Farah and Helmand. Real,
  sourced demographics: ~60% Baloch, ~25% Pashtun, ~5% Brahui; the province's own governors are ethnically
  Baloch. **Checked and ruled out**: neither neighboring Afghan province qualifies for the same treatment —
  Helmand is 92% Pashtun and Farah is 80% Pashtun, both with only a minor Baloch presence, so Nimruz alone is
  the correct and sufficient Afghan piece.

### 9.2 Applied to the map

Drawn as a new cross-border region, **Balochistan**, the same "Amazonia-style" continuous treatment already
used for Kurdistan (§3): Iran's Sistan and Baluchestan and Pakistan's Baluchistan as **core** tier (both
independently Baloch-majority, both anchoring the union from a different real-world direction), Afghanistan's
Nimruz as **domain** tier (Baloch-majority but with a real Pashtun minority, smaller and more mixed than the
other two pieces — the same core/domain logic Kurdistan already uses for Iraq's KRI versus its Turkish/
Iranian/Syrian pieces). Geometry for Pakistan's Baluchistan comes from Natural Earth's own admin-1 "Baluchistan"
feature; the rest of Pakistan remains an ordinary out-of-scope gray neighbor, unaffected by this one province's
inclusion. Full sourcing and settled-decision notes: `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s `balochistan` rows and
`subdivisions.csv`'s Iran/Sistan and Baluchestan, Afghanistan/Nimruz, and Pakistan/Baluchistan rows.

## Section 10 — Khuzestan: marked unstable territory, settled 2026-09-29

**Author's framing, following a demographic/social/geological research pass**: rather than assign Khuzestan a
final outcome, mark it "unstable territory" — genuinely unresolved between two real, sourced paths, neither
chosen.

### 10.1 The historical case, beyond the internal-minority flag this survey previously carried

Khuzestan carries a real name and a real precedent of its own, distinct from Iran generally:

- **"Arabistan"** was the semi-official name for the region from the Safavid era onward, following major Arab
  tribal settlement (the Banu Ka'b and Bani Lam tribes, migrating from Kuwait and Iraq).
- Until **1925**, western Khuzestan was a genuinely autonomous polity — the **Emirate of Muhammara**, ruled by
  **Sheikh Khaz'al Ibn Jabir** — suppressed by Reza Shah's centralizing Pahlavi government in 1924-25, which
  then formally erased the name "Arabistan" from official government use by 1936.
- Real separatist activity has continued since: the **1979 APCO uprising** (Arab Political and Cultural
  Organisation, seeking autonomy), the **1980 Iranian Embassy siege in London** (the famous SAS hostage-rescue
  operation) carried out by the self-named "Arab Popular Movement in Arabistan," and a **2018 attack on a
  military parade in Ahvaz** (24+ killed) followed by a documented Amnesty International-tracked crackdown on
  Arab activists. The more recent Ahwaz Falcons continue this pattern.
- **The load-bearing nuance, and the reason this isn't simply "Arabs want to join Iraq"**: when Saddam Hussein
  invaded in 1980, he explicitly expected Khuzestan's Shia Arab population to rise up and welcome union with
  his (Sunni-ruled, Ba'athist) Iraq. **That did not happen** — the population stayed loyal to Iran throughout
  the Iran-Iraq War. Shia religious solidarity with Tehran's own revolutionary Islamic government bound them to
  Iran more than Arab ethnicity bound them to Baghdad.

### 10.2 Why that 1980 outcome may not hold in this project's own post-war setting

This survey's own settled decision (§9's sibling naming decisions; see `regions.csv`'s persia/core note) is
that **post-war Persia turns secular** — the citizenry deliberately readopts the historical "Persia" name over
"Iran" specifically to mark a break from the Islamic Republic era. That is exactly the condition that made
1980's rejection make sense: a Shia population's loyalty to a Shia Islamic Republic, against a hostile secular/
Sunni invader. Remove the Islamic Republic, and the specific religious tie that bound Khuzestan to Tehran no
longer straightforwardly applies. On this same map, southern Iraq (Basra, Dhi Qar, Maysan, and the rest of
Mesopotamia's own "shia" tier) is itself Shia-Arab — sharing both ethnicity and sect with Khuzestan, unlike
Saddam's Ba'athist regime.

### 10.3 Two real paths, deliberately not chosen between

- **Full independence as "Arabistan"** — the better-precedented default: every actual historical separatist
  movement (Muhammara, APCO, the 1980 embassy siege, the Ahwaz Falcons) sought autonomy for Khuzestan itself,
  never annexation by a neighbor. Economically self-sustaining given its oil wealth (Ahvaz Field, Yadavaran),
  the same resource-rich-microstate pattern that already makes Kuwait and Qatar work as sovereign states on
  this map.
- **Reunification with Iraq's Shia south specifically** (not Iraq as a whole) — newly plausible precisely
  because post-war Persia's turn to secularism removes 1980's reason for rejection, and because the ethnic/
  sectarian match with southern Iraq specifically (not Saddam's old Sunni Ba'athist state) is exact.
