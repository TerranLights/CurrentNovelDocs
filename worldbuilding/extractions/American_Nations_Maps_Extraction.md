# American Nations — Map Images, Described

Source: *American Nations: A History of the Eleven Rival Regional Cultures of North America*, Colin Woodard
(2011), PDF at `worldbuilding/source-books/`. This file describes, in original wording, the five map
images embedded in that PDF. **It is a description, not a reproduction** — no map image, no on-map prose, and
no substantial verbatim text from the book appears here; place names, founding dates, and city labels are
stated directly since they are facts, not the book's protected expression. Purpose: reference material for
designing this project's own post-apocalyptic "New North America" nation boundaries (see
`worldbuilding/nations/World-Nations-Roster.md`), by
comparison against Woodard's county-level regional-culture boundaries.

**Page-numbering note:** rendering the PDF directly at high resolution turned up a discrepancy against an
earlier low-resolution identification pass — the two historical maps described below as "at rendered page 35"
and "at rendered page 170" are the reverse of what a prior quick check reported. This file describes each map
by its **verified actual content**, labeled with the page number it was actually rendered from in this pass;
treat this file's page numbers as the authoritative ones. Every page number below was directly re-verified
(rendered and visually confirmed) on 2026-09-19, not carried over from the earlier discrepant pass.

## The Modern Map (pp. 13–14): A Two-Page Spread, West and East

The book's signature map, split across two facing pages — page 13 covers the Pacific coast through the
Mississippi valley, page 14 covers the Mississippi valley through the Atlantic. Together they shade the
continental US and Canada into eleven named cultural regions at **county-level** granularity — the fill
boundaries frequently cut across individual counties rather than following state or provincial lines, which is
the map's central argument made visible: cultural regions and political boundaries are different things.

Two of the eleven regions (The Far West, Greater Appalachia) render as unshaded white in this particular
printing, distinguishable only by their county-line texture and text label rather than a gray fill — worth
noting since it means "no color" on this map is not the same as "not a claimed region."

### Overview of the eleven regions, west to east

**The Left Coast** — a narrow coastal ribbon, never more than one or two counties deep. Runs from coastal
British Columbia (including Vancouver Island and the adjoining coastal islands) down through coastal
Washington and Oregon, through the San Francisco Bay Area, and continues as a thinner strip down the California
coast as far as the Los Angeles/San Diego corridor. Does not claim the interior of any state it touches —
Washington, Oregon, and California are each split between this coastal strip and inland neighbors, with the
dividing line tracking the Cascade/coastal mountain divide rather than any county or state boundary.

**The Far West** — the large interior block: most of eastern Washington and Oregon, Idaho, Montana, Wyoming,
most of Nevada, Utah, western Colorado, and a further eastward reach into the western portions of the Dakotas,
Nebraska, Kansas, and the Texas panhandle. Shares no state cleanly with any neighbor — every western state it
touches is divided between it and either the Left Coast, El Norte, or the Midlands/First Nation block to the
northeast.

**El Norte** — a large, contiguous borderlands zone that straddles the US–Mexico line without regard to it:
Baja California, the northern Mexican border states, southern Arizona, southern New Mexico, and a substantial
southern/western portion of Texas following the Rio Grande corridor, plus a coastal reach up the California
coast to the San Diego area. Within Texas, it claims only the southern/western borderlands, not the whole
state. The deep interior of Mexico south of this zone is left in a lighter, unlabeled tone — shown for
geographic context, not claimed as one of the eleven regions.

**First Nation** — the broad northern band across the top of the map, covering a wide swath of northern Canada.
Its southern edge is the one boundary on the map that doesn't track any nearby political line at all — drawn
well north of the Canada–US border, following what reads as a rough settlement/climatic line rather than any
jurisdiction.

**The Midlands** — appears as two connected lobes rather than one block. A northern lobe runs along the north
and west shores of Lake Superior and Lake Huron (northern Ontario, the tip of Michigan's Upper Peninsula); a
larger southern lobe runs west of Lake Michigan through Wisconsin and across the northern tier of Illinois and
Iowa, reaching into the eastern Dakotas, Nebraska, and Kansas. The two lobes meet around the western end of the
Great Lakes. A separate, narrow arm of Midlands territory also hugs the *south* shore of Lake Erie — a thin
coastal band cutting across northern Ohio, northern Pennsylvania, and the far western tip of New York, distinct
from the interior of those same states.

**Yankeedom** — also two lobes, separated by the Midlands' Great Lakes wedge and not geographically contiguous
with each other on this map. A western lobe covers Minnesota and northern Wisconsin; a much larger eastern lobe
covers all of New England, upstate/western New York (excluding both the New York City area and the narrow Lake
Erie shore band claimed by the Midlands), and a strip of southern Ontario along the Erie/Ontario shore.

**New Netherland** — a small, compact pocket centered on New York City, the lower Hudson Valley, and adjoining
slivers of northern New Jersey and Connecticut. Not the state of New York or New Jersey — just this dense
metropolitan core, with an irregular boundary that clearly follows old settlement patterns rather than any
modern administrative line.

**Tidewater** — a narrow coastal strip around Chesapeake Bay: the Eastern Shore/Delmarva Peninsula and the
tidewater lowlands of Virginia and Maryland, reaching into the northeastern corner of North Carolina. Notably
thin on this modern map (see the historical maps below for how much larger it once read) — it excludes the
interior/piedmont of Virginia and Maryland entirely, and looks like the smallest of the eleven regions here.

**Greater Appalachia** — a long mountain-and-upland corridor from western Pennsylvania and West Virginia
through Kentucky and Tennessee, western Virginia and the Carolinas, into northern Georgia and Alabama, then
west again into southern Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois, the Ozarks of Missouri and Arkansas, and into eastern
Oklahoma and north Texas. Splits Virginia roughly down the middle (Appalachia west, Tidewater east) and claims
only the southern portions of Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, and Missouri, leaving those states' northern portions to
the Midlands or Yankeedom.

**Deep South** — covers eastern Texas, Louisiana (minus one enclave, below), Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia,
South Carolina, and Florida's panhandle and peninsula down to roughly the Orlando/Tampa latitude. Notably does
**not** claim the southern tip of Florida — the Miami/Everglades area is left unshaded, outside all eleven
named regions entirely.

**New France** — two small, disconnected pieces, both notably smaller than most of the other regions. A
northern piece centered on the St. Lawrence valley around Quebec City, shaded slightly differently between its
core (around Quebec) and its Acadian extension into the Maritime provinces and Gaspé Peninsula. A separate
southern piece is a small dark enclave around New Orleans and the immediately surrounding Louisiana parishes,
entirely surrounded by Deep South territory — the single clearest "island" on the whole map, one nation's
territory sitting completely inside another's with no shared border to the outside.

### The state/provincial splits worth flagging for cross-referencing purposes

These are the boundary facts a state-line map would structurally be unable to capture, and are the actual
point of a county-level source:

- **New York** is divided at least three ways: Yankeedom (upstate/western), New Netherland (NYC/lower Hudson),
  and a thin Midlands sliver along the Lake Erie shore in the state's far west.
- **Michigan** is divided between the Midlands' two lobes and territory bordering First Nation near the Upper
  Peninsula — no part of Michigan reads as a single, unified regional-culture block.
- **Texas** is divided three ways: El Norte (south/west borderlands), Deep South (east), and The Far West (the
  panhandle).
- **Virginia** splits roughly in half: Tidewater (east) and Greater Appalachia (west).
- **Ohio** splits three ways: a Midlands sliver on the Lake Erie shore, Yankeedom in the northeast, and Greater
  Appalachia in the south.
- **California, Washington, and Oregon** each split into a coastal Left Coast strip versus an interior Far West
  (or, for southern California, El Norte) block.
- States that read as effectively whole on this map: **Kentucky and Tennessee** (Greater Appalachia), **the
  core New England states** (Yankeedom).

## Historical Settlement Maps (Three Inset Maps)

These complement the static present-day picture above by showing *how* the regions formed and spread — origin
points, founding dates, and the direction of inland migration.

### East Coast, Founding Era — "Settled Area, 1775" (rendered page 47)

Shows the original eight East Coast-founding regions with a founding date attached to each: New France/Quebec
(1608), New France/Acadia (1604), Yankeedom (1621), New Netherland (1624), the Midlands (1682), Tidewater
(1607), Greater Appalachia (1717), and Deep South (1670). Key founding cities are marked: Quebec, Boston, New
Amsterdam, Philadelphia, Jamestown, Donegal, and Charleston. Arrows radiate inland from each region's coastal
core, already reaching toward the Appalachian mountains by this date — Greater Appalachia's arrows in
particular fan out widely along the mountain spine, consistent with its later, more rapid inland spread despite
its 1717 founding date being the most recent of the eight.

### East Coast, Expansion — "Settled Area, 1776" / "Political Boundaries, 1850" (rendered page 170)

The same eight East Coast regions, now shown without founding-date labels but with a much longer time horizon:
the shaded settled area corresponds to 1776, while a dashed overlay shows political (state/provincial)
boundaries as they stood in 1850 — a deliberate juxtaposition of cultural settlement against political
lines that had by then diverged from it. The migration arrows are far more extensive here than on the 1775 map,
fanning deep into the Great Lakes basin and the Mississippi valley — Greater Appalachia's and the Midlands'
arrows in particular reach past the Mississippi, while New France/Quebec's arrows push into the upper Midwest.
New Orleans is marked as a labeled city under "New France (Louisiana)," confirming this Gulf Coast enclave was
already established by this period rather than a later development. Richmond, Knoxville, and Charleston appear
as additional marked cities for Tidewater, Greater Appalachia, and Deep South respectively.

### El Norte / Southwest — "Spanish Settled Area, 1830" (rendered page 35)

A separate, geographically distinct map covering California through the Southwest and into northern Mexico —
the origin story for El Norte specifically, on its own timeline (predating and separate from the Anglo East
Coast founding dates above). Marks a chain of California mission/settlement towns along the coast with
individual founding dates: San Diego (1769), San Gabriel/Los Angeles (1771), San Juan Capistrano (1776), San
Luis Obispo (1772), Santa Barbara (1782), San Buenaventura (1782), Santa Clara (1777), San José (1770), San
Francisco (1776), San Rafael (1817), and San Francisco Solano (1823) — a steady chain moving north along the
coast over roughly fifty years. Inland, it marks Santa Fe (1607) and Taos (1615) as considerably older than the
California missions, plus Albuquerque (1706), El Paso (1659), Tucson (1775), and Tubac (1752). South of the
modern border it marks Chihuahua (1709), Saltillo (1577), Monterrey (1560), Guaymas (1769), Matamoros (1824),
Reynosa (1749), Laredo (1755), Nacogdoches (1779), Matagorda (1823), and Refugio (1795) — establishing that
several of these Mexican settlements substantially predate the California missions, with Monterrey and Saltillo
in particular founded over two centuries earlier. Arrows show the settlement pattern radiating outward from
these hubs — one clear route running up from central Mexico through Chihuahua toward Santa Fe/Taos and a
separate branch toward El Paso, and another route up the Pacific coast connecting the Sonoran towns to the
California mission chain, plus an eastward push from the Rio Grande hubs toward Nacogdoches in what is now East
Texas.

## Synthesis Notes for Cross-Referencing

- **The book's regional culture boundaries almost never coincide with US state lines**, and where an inset map
  shows the *founding* period, the divergence is even starker — the eight East Coast nations' original 1775
  extent is a set of overlapping coastal fans radiating from single cities, not anything resembling a modern
  state shape.
- **Two regions (New France's Louisiana piece, and the unclaimed southern tip of Florida) are genuine
  enclaves/exclusions** that a simple state-assignment approach would miss entirely — a single nation's
  territory sitting inside another's with no shared outside border, and a populated area left outside all
  eleven regions altogether.
- **The El Norte/Southwest settlement history runs on a completely separate timeline and origin logic** from
  the eight East Coast nations — centuries older in its Mexican interior origin points, and driven by a
  different colonizing power (Spain, not Britain/France), which is likely relevant if the same founding-wave
  method gets applied to other continents later, since not every region on a future map will share one
  originating colonial process.
