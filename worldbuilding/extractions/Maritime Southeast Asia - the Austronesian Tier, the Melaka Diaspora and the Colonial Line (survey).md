# Maritime Southeast Asia — the Austronesian Tier, the Melaka Diaspora and the Colonial Line

2026-09-26. **Reconnaissance survey. No nations named, no borders drawn.** The analytical layer for
**Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore and Timor-Leste** — the six polities the Mainland
Southeast Asia survey named and set aside as "a separate later pass," on the grounds that the object is
genuinely different: Austronesian rather than Tai/Mon-Khmer/Tibeto-Burman, more Islamic and Christian than
Theravada, and archipelagic rather than riverine.

**Companion files:** `Mainland Southeast Asia - Mandalas, the Massif and the Tai Continuum (survey).md` (the
sibling region, same broader setting — cited throughout rather than re-derived) and
`METHOD - How Nations Get Drawn.md` (the cross-region methodology this survey applies). The material/economic
survey and the map-data CSVs are separate, later stages and are not attempted here.

⚠️ **This is a first pass, built in one research session under a shared web-search budget, and it is
narrower than the East Asia and Latin America surveys.** Treat it as a frame to argue with, not a finished
analysis. Gaps are flagged rather than filled, per this project's own convention.

---

## Headline findings

1. **The mandala was maritime before it was riverine, and the sibling survey already named the two clearest
   cases without dwelling on them.** Of the mandala's "notable" 5th–15th century examples the mainland survey
   lists — the Khmer Empire, Srivijaya, Majapahit, Ayutthaya, Champa, Lan Xang, Lanna — **two of the seven,
   Srivijaya and Majapahit, are maritime thalassocracies, not river-valley states.** Their extent was
   control of a strait and its trade, not a drainage basin. **The core/domain/sphere vocabulary transfers to
   this region even more directly than it transferred to the mainland**, because the mandala's founding
   examples already include maritime ones.
2. **Tier-1/tier-2 ethnic identity works almost everywhere here, and the one place it doesn't is the sharpest
   line in the whole survey.** The overwhelming majority of every country in this set is **Austronesian** at
   tier 1 — Malay, Javanese, Sundanese, Tagalog, Cebuano, Bugis, the lot. **West Papua is the exception, and it
   is total**: Papuans are ethnically and linguistically **Melanesian**, part of the Papuan-language family
   that has no Austronesian affiliation at all, and Indonesia's own 1969 absorption of the territory is
   contested on exactly that basis. This is this survey's version of Vietnam in the mainland file — a
   population that belongs to a different civilizational world than the region it sits inside on a modern map.
3. **The line between the two tier-1 worlds is close to a real, mapped biogeographic boundary, and that
   deserves a flag rather than a claim.** The **Wallace Line** (1859) and the wider transitional zone
   **Wallacea** mark where Asian fauna gives way to a mixed Asian/Australian fauna, running between Borneo and
   Sulawesi and through the Lombok Strait between Bali and Lombok. **⚠️ This survey did not verify a rigorous
   ethnolinguistic-line comparison in this session** — Wallacea is a zone, not a line, and Papuan-language
   populations are documented as far west as Halmahera and Alor, inside Wallacea rather than cleanly east of
   it. The honest claim is that the Austronesian/Melanesian tier-1 transition **broadly tracks the same
   biogeographic transition zone**, not that a single line does double duty as Hu's line did in East Asia.
4. **Java is this survey's Hu Line, and it is an internal fact rather than a border-crossing one.** Java holds
   roughly **56% of Indonesia's population on about 7% of its land area** — 158 million people on one island.
   Every other measured discontinuity in this atlas (Hu's line, Isan, the Qinling–Huaihe results) is about
   which side of a line a person falls on. **This one is about a single country whose demographic center of
   gravity is catastrophically off-center from its geographic one**, which is the load-bearing fact for how
   Indonesia's own internal division — already decided by the author — should actually be drawn.
5. **Indonesia already did, in reality, almost exactly what the author has decided to do to it.** In 2022 the
   Indonesian state split its restive, ethnically distinct Papua province into **four new provinces**
   (Highland Papua, Central Papua, South Papua, Southwest Papua, alongside the original Papua province) —
   driven by the same logic of ethnic distinctness and demand for more local control that a post-collapse
   internal-division scheme would use. **This is a live precedent to build the recommended internal-division
   scheme on, not a hypothetical.** See 3.1.5.
6. **The Philippines is this survey's Vietnam a second time — the one Christian, Latin-alphabet, formerly
   Spanish-then-American-administered outlier in a Muslim-and-Buddhist-majority region.** Everywhere else in
   this set, Islam is the dominant or historically dominant tradition. The Philippines took the same
   Melaka-diaspora Islamization wave (it reached Sulu and Mindanao) and then had it **arrested by Spanish
   conquest in the 16th century**, which imposed Catholicism everywhere colonial administration reached and
   left Islam intact only in the south. **The country the Islamization wave reached last is also the one where
   it was stopped hardest**, and the residue is the Bangsamoro.
7. **The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao is this survey's Myanmar — the one place the lines
   already exist.** A real, current, first-order-subdivision-respecting autonomous region (five provinces plus
   Cotabato City, over 89% Muslim), established by a 2019 organic law after a half-century insurgency. **No
   drawing is required here; the state already drew it.** ⚠️ **Population reconciled 2026-09-26**: this
   survey's original 5.69 million (2024) figure predates **Executive Order No. 91 (30 July 2025)**, which
   formally excluded Sulu from BARMM — final Philippine Supreme Court ruling, November 2024 — and transferred
   it to Region IX (Zamboanga Peninsula) in full, including all its municipalities and barangays. The
   post-exclusion BARMM population is **4,545,486**; the roughly 1.1 million difference between the two
   figures is accounted for by Sulu's population moving to the Zamboanga Peninsula region, not a data error.
8. **Sabah and Sarawak are this survey's Isan — a documented, current, quantified grievance about the terms of
   a union**, not a settled question of ethnic distinctness the way Bangsamoro or West Papua are. East Malaysia
   joined the 1963 federation on the Malaysia Agreement's promises of autonomy and (per some accounts) 40% of
   federal revenue; in practice **both Sabah and Sarawak receive a 5% oil royalty** — Sarawak's separate,
   genuine win was a **5% state sales tax on petroleum products**, not a 20% royalty (⚠️ **corrected
   2026-09-26**: an earlier draft of this survey stated Sarawak's royalty itself as 20%; that figure is what
   both states have been *demanding*, not what either receives). **Two federal partners admitted on the same
   terms have not received the same deal, and the fact is on the record and disputed today, not merely
   historical.**

## Part 1 — What replaces first effective settlement

### 1.1 Tier-1/tier-2 ethnic identity, and its one clean exception

The mainland survey's headline 2 already established that Woodard's Doctrine of First Effective Settlement
fails in Southeast Asia for a different reason than it failed in East Asia: not because there was no founding
moment, but because **the defining cultural layer — Indianization — was chosen, not brought by settlers.**
That finding transfers here whole. What this survey adds is the tier-1/tier-2 layer itself.

**Tier 1 across almost the whole region is Austronesian.** The Austronesian expansion began among the
Dapenkeng culture of southeastern-China-derived peoples on Taiwan (from roughly 4500 BCE), and by roughly
3000–2200 BCE seafarers from Taiwan had reached the Batanes Archipelago and northern Luzon, spreading through
the rest of the Philippines and maritime Southeast Asia over the following two millennia, and on to eastern
Indonesia and Melanesia by 1500–1000 BCE. **Every major ethnic group named below — Malay, Javanese, Sundanese,
Tagalog, Cebuano, Bugis, Iban, Bruneian Malay — is Austronesian at tier 1.** This is the single broadest
ethnic-identity fact in the survey, and it is why the region reads, culturally, as one object despite six
different modern sovereign states.

| Tier 1 | Tier 2 (a partial list; not exhaustive) |
|---|---|
| **Austronesian (western/Malayic branch)** | Malay, Minangkabau, Acehnese, Bruneian Malay, Bugis, Banjarese, Bantenese, Betawi |
| **Austronesian (Javanese/Sundanese)** | Javanese (~40% of Indonesia alone), Sundanese (~15%), Madurese |
| **Austronesian (Philippine branch)** | Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, and the other major Philippine language groups |
| **Austronesian (Bornean)** | Dayak (many sub-groups; ~1.4% of Indonesia), Iban, Kadazan-Dusun |
| **Austronesian (other)** | Balinese, Batak (~3.6%), Toraja |
| **Papuan / Melanesian — the exception** | Dozens of unrelated Papuan-language groups across New Guinea and its offshore islands; no Austronesian affiliation |
| **Sinitic (overseas Chinese)** | See 1.4 below — a cross-cutting minority present in every country in this set |

**The exception is total, not partial.** Papuans are ethnically and linguistically Melanesian — related to the
majority populations of Papua New Guinea, Fiji and Vanuatu, not to the Austronesian-speaking majority of the
rest of Indonesia. Indonesia's absorption of Netherlands New Guinea followed a 1969 "Act of Free Choice" in
which roughly a thousand hand-picked representatives, selected by the Indonesian military and officials, voted
on behalf of the whole territory — internationally recognized at the time, but rejected by many Papuans then
and since as neither free nor a genuine choice. The state has since run a sustained transmigration program
(over 300,000 non-Papuan settlers, largely from Java, by 2000) into the territory, and a low-grade separatist
insurgency (the Free Papua Movement, OPM) has run continuously since. **This is not a border-cutting-a-people
case like Isan or the Khmer Krom — it is a case of one state holding a population that belongs to an entirely
different tier-1 world**, closer in shape to Tibet's or Xinjiang's relationship to the Sinian Federation than
to anything in the mainland survey.

### 1.2 The maritime mandala

The mainland survey's Part 1 already states the mandala model plainly: authority radiating from a center
through personal allegiance rather than fixed territory; extent defined by who acknowledges whom; a border
polity able to acknowledge several overlords at once. **What that survey did not dwell on is that two of its
own seven named examples — Srivijaya (Sumatra-based, 7th–13th centuries, controlling the Strait of
Malacca) and Majapahit (Java-based, 13th–15th centuries, claiming influence across much of the archipelago and
into the Malay Peninsula) — were thalassocracies.** Their power was control of sea lanes and the tribute of
ports, not a drainage basin. Where a river-valley mandala's domain fades outward across land, a maritime
mandala's domain fades outward across a trade network: a port either sent tribute and received protection, or
it did not, and the same port could plausibly answer to more than one distant center depending on which fleet
last called.

**Consequence for the map, inherited directly from the mainland survey's own §1.2:** the `core_territory` /
`domain_territory` / `sphere_territory` columns are appropriate here too, and for the same reason —
**graduated, overlapping edges are the honest shape of precolonial authority in this region, not an error to
tidy away.** Whether this project's map-data stage actually needs the sphere layer for maritime Southeast Asia
the way it needed it for the mainland's Tai Continuum/Isan/Zomia/Khmer Krom spheres is a question for that
later stage — candidates would include the historical reach of Srivijaya/Majapahit-style trade-network
influence, or the modern overseas-Chinese commercial diaspora (1.4) — but this survey does not propose specific
sphere geometry, only flags that the vocabulary is available and well-grounded here.

### 1.3 Islamization: the second wave, and the Melaka diaspora mechanism

Indianization (Part 1.3 of the mainland survey) reached maritime Southeast Asia too — Srivijaya, Majapahit,
Tarumanagara, Singhasari and Kutai are the maritime-world examples the sibling survey already names — by the
same trade-borne, no-conquest mechanism as the mainland. **Islam arrived the same way, centuries later, and
the mechanism by which it spread has a specific, documented hub: Melaka.**

By the 15th century the Sultanate of Melaka was the dominant trading power on the Malay Peninsula and a center
of Islamic learning. **The Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511 did not stop the spread of Islam — it
accelerated it**: Muslim merchants and scholars fled the city and dispersed to other ports, carrying Melaka's
Islamic culture with them. Two of the resulting centers were **Ternate and Tidore**, the rival sultanates of
the Moluccas (the historical Spice Islands), which had already begun converting from the late 14th century and
completed the process in the 15th; they used control of the clove and nutmeg trade to project Islamic
political authority across the eastern archipelago, continuing the diaspora's work. Aceh, on Sumatra's
northern tip, is the other major node, and remains the one Indonesian province to retain a formal place for
Islamic law (see 3.1.2).

**This gives the region a second Indianization-shaped finding: another civilizational layer adopted through
trade and prestige rather than conquest**, arriving in waves that radiate outward from specific ports rather
than from a single frontier the way the Han core's expansion did. The Philippines is where this wave's reach
is most visible as an edge rather than a saturation — see 1.5 and 3.2.

### 1.4 The overseas Chinese layer — present everywhere, weighted very differently

Every country in this survey carries a Sinitic minority whose presence long predates modern nationhood, and
whose weight varies by an order of magnitude country to country:

| Country | Ethnic Chinese, approx. | Share of national population |
|---|---|---|
| **Singapore** | ~2.7 million (2020) | **~75.5%** — a majority, not a minority |
| **Malaysia** | ~7.5 million (2020) | **~23%** (⚠️ corrected 2026-09-26; the earlier ~34% figure is roughly Malaysia's Chinese share in the 1960s, not the current 2020 census share) |
| **Indonesia** | ~3.3 million (2020) | **~3%** — small share, largest absolute count in the set after Malaysia |
| **Philippines** | ~1.35 million | **~1–2%** |

**Singapore inverts the pattern entirely** — nowhere else in this survey, or in the mainland survey's Hui/Hakka
treatment, is what's elsewhere a minority the plain majority. That is one more reason Singapore reads as its
own category rather than a smaller version of Malaysia (3.5). ⚠️ **This survey did not research the
overseas-Chinese population's internal linguistic/regional composition** (Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese,
Hakka, Hainanese communities differ by country and city) — flagged as an open item if finer resolution is
wanted later, on the same model as the Hui/Hakka treatment in the East Asia and mainland files.

### 1.5 The colonial line — why this region's modern borders are not mandala-shaped at all

**State the contrast plainly, because it is the single biggest structural difference from the mainland
survey.** That file's headline 1 states that "the modern borders of mainland Southeast Asia are a European
artifact laid over a system that had no borders at all" — true, but the mainland's modern states (Thailand,
Myanmar, Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia) each have a real, continuous precolonial polity behind their modern shape,
however redrawn at the edges. **Maritime Southeast Asia does not have that.** With one partial exception
(Brunei, 3.4), **the six polities in this survey are, almost without remainder, the colonial administrative
units their borders were drawn as**, inherited at independence under the customary-international-law principle
of ***uti possidetis juris*** — new states take the boundaries of the administrative unit they were before
independence, rather than redrawing on ethnic or historical lines.

- **Indonesia** is the former Dutch East Indies, whole, at independence.
- **The Philippines** is the former Spanish, then American, colonial administrative territory, whole.
- **Malaysia and Singapore** are former British Malaya, the Straits Settlements, and British Borneo
  (Sarawak, North Borneo), reorganized once at the 1963 federation and once again at Singapore's 1965
  separation — but still colonial administrative units in shape, not precolonial sultanates redrawn.
- **Timor-Leste** is the former Portuguese colonial territory, including its odd exclave (see 3.6).
- **Brunei alone** is a rump of a much larger precolonial sultanate that once claimed most of Borneo's coast —
  the one place in this survey where the modern border is a *shrinkage* of a real precolonial polity rather
  than a colonial administrative unit adopted wholesale.

**This matters for how this survey's Part 3 should be read.** Where the mainland survey could ask "what did
this precolonial mandala actually control, and does the modern state's shape still reflect it," that question
mostly returns nothing useful here. The live fault lines in this region (Aceh, West Papua, Maluku, Bangsamoro,
Sabah/Sarawak) are not precolonial-mandala survivals — **they are colonial-era administrative lines that
turned out to bind together peoples who do not agree they belong together, or that split apart peoples
(Sulu's Sabah claim, 3.2) who once did.**

⚠️ **The Indonesia–Malaysia and Malaysia–Philippines borders are explicitly not fully settled even today**:
demarcation disputes persist on the Borneo land border (differing interpretations of colonial-era documents),
and no common tripoint exists between Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines in the Celebes Sea, because no
single colonial treaty involved all three colonial powers. **Uti possidetis gave the region its borders; it
did not give the region agreement on exactly where all of them run.**

## Part 2 — The measured discontinuities

### 2.1 Java — the internal Hu Line

Restated from headline 4, with the numbers in one place: **Java holds roughly 56% of Indonesia's population
(about 158 million people, mid-2025) on about 7% of the country's land area.** Indonesia has 38 provinces
spread across roughly 17,000 islands; more than half its people live on one of them.

**Why this is structurally different from every other measured discontinuity in this atlas.** The Hu Line,
Isan, and the Qinling–Huaihe results are all about a population split by a line — two groups, one on each
side, with the discontinuity *between* them. **Java's imbalance is not a split at all: it is one country whose
population and whose land area point in opposite directions with no comparably-sized rival anywhere else in
the archipelago.** That is a different design problem for a post-collapse map. A line-splitting discontinuity
asks "which side does this land end up on." A Java-shaped discontinuity asks "does the core survive the
collapse in a radically different position relative to its own periphery than it held before" — Java's
food-and-industry base, population, and colonial-then-national capital (Batavia/Jakarta) all sit in the same
overrepresented 7%, which is either a source of extraordinary post-collapse leverage (if Java's institutions
and infrastructure hold) or an extraordinary vulnerability (if they do not), and either reading is a real
option for later stages to develop, not something this survey resolves.

### 2.2 The Wallace Line / Wallacea — a real but approximate correlate, flagged rather than claimed

Restated from headline 3. **Alfred Russel Wallace** drew the line in 1859 (named for him by T.H. Huxley); it
runs through the Lombok Strait (Bali/Lombok) and between Borneo and Sulawesi, separating Asian fauna to the
west from a mixed Asian-Australian fauna to the east. **Wallacea** — the transitional zone itself, a 1924
coinage — comprises Sulawesi, Lombok, Sumbawa, Flores, Sumba, Timor, Halmahera, Buru, Seram and the smaller
islands between them, sitting at the collision point of several tectonic plates.

**The tempting move is to claim this as this region's Hu Line: a hard biogeographic fact that also marks the
Austronesian/Melanesian cultural transition.** Wallacea is explicitly a *zone*, and Papuan-language
(Melanesian-affiliated) populations are documented on islands inside it and arguably west of a strict
single-line reading (Halmahera, Alor). **Resolved 2026-09-26 (evidence pass):** the linguistic-boundary source
this survey said it hadn't located exists and has a name — **"Linguistic Wallacea," per Antoinette Schapper
(2015, 2017)**. It is a real, peer-reviewed academic concept, not a loose analogy: a documented transitional
zone where Austronesian languages (Central Malayo-Polynesian, South Halmahera–West New Guinea) sit alongside
Papuan language families (Greater West Bomberai, North Halmahera, East/West/South Bird's Head, Hatam–Mansim,
Yawa-Saweru, Mairasic) in long-standing contact, with borrowing running both directions. **But it does not
coincide with biological Wallacea, and that mismatch is the finding worth keeping:** Linguistic Wallacea is
shifted east of the biogeographic line — it **excludes Lombok and Sulawesi** (both biologically "Wallacean"
but linguistically on the Austronesian side of a cleaner boundary) and **extends further east into New
Guinea's Bird's Head, Neck, and Cenderawasih Bay** than the biogeographic zone reaches. **So the honestly
supportable claim is now stronger than "roughly the same stretch," but different from a clean Hu-Line
equivalence**: there is a real, named, citable ethnolinguistic transition zone here, but its own boundary is a
distinct object from Wallace's line, not a linguistic restatement of it. Any map wanting to show this should
draw Linguistic Wallacea's own footprint (Minor Sundic Islands east of Lombok, Timor-Leste, Maluku, the Bird's
Head/Neck and Cenderawasih Bay) rather than tracing the 1859 biogeographic line itself.

## Part 3 — The countries, as they stand

**This is a description of present ground, not a proposal**, per the mainland survey's own framing —
except for 3.1.5, which is explicitly a recommendation flagged as such, because "Indonesia gets its own
internal division" is the one place this survey is asked to propose rather than only describe.

### 3.1 Indonesia — the one that needs internal division

**~285 million people (2026 estimate), 38 provinces, roughly 17,000 islands.** The world's largest
archipelagic state and, after China, India and the US, the world's fourth most populous country.

**3.1.1 The ethnic breakdown (tier 2, national level).** Javanese ~40%, Sundanese ~15%, Malay ~3.7%, Batak
~3.6%, Madurese ~3%, Betawi ~2.9%, Minangkabau ~2.7%, Buginese ~2.7%, Bantenese ~2%, Banjarese ~1.7%, Dayak
~1.4%, and a very long tail — Statistics Indonesia counts more than 1,300 distinct ethnicities, grouped into
31 broader categories. **Indonesia's own nation-building answer to this diversity is itself a finding worth
recording**: in 1928 nationalist youth leaders, who were mostly Javanese and could have made Javanese —
already the most widely spoken native language — the national language without real opposition, **deliberately
chose Malay instead**, precisely because it was not tied to any one dominant ethnic hierarchy and already
functioned as a neutral cross-archipelago trade language. Bahasa Indonesia is therefore a language chosen for
what it was *not* — not Javanese — as much as for what it was. **This is a genuine case of engineered rather
than inherited national identity**, and it has no real parallel elsewhere in this atlas.

**3.1.2 Aceh — special autonomy, and the one place Islamic law has a formal state role.** Promised special
status by Sukarno for its contribution to independence, a promise "broken almost immediately"; a home to the
Darul Islam rebellion in the 1950s; granted "special region" status (including elevated recognition of Islamic
law and custom) in 1959. **The Free Aceh Movement (GAM)**, founded 1976, fought for outright independence
rather than mere autonomy, on the strength of Aceh's precolonial history as an independent sultanate. The 1998
fall of Suharto opened a reform window; the **2005 Helsinki peace agreement** ended the conflict, granting
Aceh full special autonomy within Indonesia and converting GAM into a political party. **Aceh is therefore a
live case of exactly the kind of internal-division logic Indonesia's 2022 Papua reorganization would later
repeat**: an ethnically and religiously distinct region, with its own precolonial-sultanate case for
separateness, resolved (for now) by autonomy rather than partition or force.

**3.1.3 West Papua — the tier-1 exception made concrete (see 1.1).** Papuans are Melanesian, not Austronesian;
Indonesia's hold on the territory dates to a contested 1969 vote; a transmigration program has moved hundreds
of thousands of non-Papuan settlers in; the OPM insurgency is decades old and ongoing (a 2025 Yahukimo
massacre is the most recent entry in a long list). Indonesia will not readily give this territory up — the
Grasberg mine alone was projected to produce **$1.94 billion in revenue in 2022** — which sets up a real
tension between the territory's cultural case for separateness and the core's material stake in keeping it,
structurally similar to the tension the Guri Dam creates for Venezuela and Amazonia in the Latin America
survey, or Bolívar's deliberate-exception treatment there.

**3.1.4 Maluku — a sectarian fault line with its own failed secession.** The historical Spice Islands, home to
the Ternate/Tidore sultanates that helped carry Islam eastward (1.3), also carry a distinct **Christian**
population descended from Dutch-era conversion efforts concentrated in Ambon and the Lease Islands — a colonial
policy that produced a loyal Christian-Ambonese administrative elite alongside the Muslim sultanates' retained
semi-autonomy. **Chris Soumokil declared an independent Republic of the South Moluccas (RMS) in April 1950**;
the rebellion failed quickly and Soumokil was executed in 1966, but RMS survives as a government-in-exile and
a live symbol. **1999–2002** saw a second, much bloodier round — the Maluku sectarian conflict, at least 5,000
dead and half a million displaced, driven by post-Suharto instability, migration pressure, and a perceived
"greening" (Islamicization) of the central government. **A second internal fault line, distinct from Aceh's and
Papua's, on a religious rather than ethnic axis.**

**3.1.5 Recommended approach to Indonesia's internal division — flagged as a recommendation, not a decision.**
The 2022 Papua precedent (headline 5) is the strongest available real-world grounding: the state itself just
subdivided a large, ethnically distinct, restive province into four smaller ones on almost exactly the logic a
post-collapse internal-division scheme would use. **This survey recommends grouping Indonesia's 38 provinces
into island-group blocs** — Sumatra, Java (kept whole, or split at most along its existing
Javanese/Sundanese/Madurese linguistic seams if a finer grain is wanted), Kalimantan (Indonesian Borneo),
Sulawesi, Maluku (the historical Spice Islands, plus North Maluku), Nusa Tenggara plus Bali, and Papua (already
four provinces post-2022, plus West Papua province) — **rather than the 31-ethnicity tier-2 map**, on the same
"tier 2 has more resolution than the nations layer can carry" caution the East Asia survey raised about the
Sinitic language map and Ribeiro's five Brasis. Aceh and West Papua's cases for special treatment within their
respective blocs (Sumatra and Papua) are real and documented above; whether that becomes a sphere-style
overlay, a hatched autonomous pocket (the Wa/Hakka treatment), or a full separate block is exactly the kind of
open decision this survey is supposed to surface, not settle.

**⚠️ Evidence added 2026-09-26, decision still open.** Two figures worth having on hand when that decision gets
made: **Aceh is demographically Austronesian, not tier-1 distinct** — Acehnese are 2020-census-confirmed at
roughly 70% of the province's ~5.4 million people, so its case rests entirely on tier-2/religious-political
grounds (precolonial sultanate, Islamic law, GAM), not on the kind of civilizational-boundary case West Papua
has. **West Papua's Melanesian population is now roughly at demographic parity with settlers, not simply a
majority with some migration**: current estimates put the territory (2.7 million) at about 50% indigenous
Melanesian and 50% Indonesian-migrant-descended, the latter concentrated in coastal cities and driven by
decades of state-sponsored transmigration, while the highlands remain overwhelmingly Papuan. That 50/50 split
is itself an argument for *some* kind of differentiated treatment (a full block would visually overstate how
settled the territory now is; folding it into a plain Papua bloc with no marking would understate the real,
ongoing tier-1 distinctness and conflict) — but which treatment is still the author's call, not resolved here.

### 3.2 The Philippines — the Catholic outlier, and the one place a Moro autonomous region already exists

**~82 provinces, 18 regions** (8 Luzon, 4 Visayas, 6 Mindanao — the Negros Island Region was
re-established in June 2024, splitting Negros Occidental, Negros Oriental and Siquijor out of two neighboring
Visayan regions, which is itself a small, recent, real precedent for regional boundary revision by the state).

**Precolonial Islam reached the far south and stalled there.** Islam arrived in Sulu in 1380 via the Sunni-Sufi
missionary Karim-ul Makhdum; the **Sultanate of Sulu** (established c. 1457) and the **Sultanate of
Maguindanao** (1515) extended Moro Islamic rule across the Sulu Archipelago, Basilan, Palawan, coastal
Zamboanga, and — significantly — **North Borneo, ceded to Sulu *by* the Sultan of Brunei in the 17th century
in return for Sulu's military help in a Brunei civil war** (⚠️ **direction corrected 2026-09-26**: an earlier
draft of this survey had the cession running the other way, Sulu to Brunei in the 15th century — it was
Brunei that ceded the territory to Sulu, not Sulu that ceded it to Brunei), which is the historical root of
the modern, unresolved **Philippine claim to Sabah** (see
3.3). Spanish conquest, beginning in the 16th century, imposed Catholicism everywhere it reached but never
fully subdued the Moro south — the "Moro Wars" ran intermittently from 1578 to 1898 — leaving the Philippines
as **the one country in this survey where the Melaka-diaspora Islamization wave was stopped rather than
completed**, with the stopping line still visible on the modern map as the Christian/Muslim divide between
most of the country and the Bangsamoro region.

**The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM)** — five provinces (Basilan, Lanao del Sur,
Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Tawi-Tawi) plus the independent city of Cotabato — was established
by a 2019 organic law, replacing the earlier, weaker Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao. **Population 5.69
million (2024 census), over 89% Muslim** — the country's most religiously distinct region by a wide margin.
Per headline 7, this is drawn on real first-order-subdivision lines already, the same status Myanmar's ethnic
states held for the mainland survey.

### 3.3 Malaysia — Peninsular, Sarawak and Sabah: one federation, two very different deals

**13 states, 3 federal territories.** Peninsular Malaysia: Perlis, Kedah, Penang, Perak, Selangor, Negeri
Sembilan, Malacca, Johor, Kelantan, Terengganu, Pahang (11 states). East Malaysia: **Sabah and Sarawak** (2
states, on Borneo, separated from the peninsula by the South China Sea). Federal territories: Kuala Lumpur
(national capital), Putrajaya (administrative capital), Labuan (offshore financial center).

**The structural fact, restated from headline 8:** Sabah and Sarawak joined the 1963 federation as separate
polities with their own colonial histories — Sabah a former chartered-company territory (British North
Borneo), Sarawak a former independent kingdom under the Brooke dynasty — under the **Malaysia Agreement 1963
(MA63)**, which promised broader autonomy and a larger federal-revenue share than either state has actually
received. **Both Sabah and Sarawak get a 5% oil royalty; Sarawak's separate win was a 5% state sales tax on
petroleum products, not a 20% royalty** (⚠️ **corrected 2026-09-26**, see headline 8) — the two "equal
partners" of 1963 do not hold equal terms today, and MA63's guarantees are widely described in both states'
own politics as eroded by federal centralization over time. This is a real, current, documented grievance, not
a historical footnote — unlike Aceh's or Bangsamoro's, it has not (yet) produced an armed insurgency or a
formal autonomous-region carve-out, which places it closer to Isan's "suppressed rather than absent" identity
than to West Papua's open conflict.

**⚠️ Brunei's 1962 near-miss belongs here as context, not as Malaysian territory** — see 3.4.

### 3.4 Brunei — the one place the precolonial line survives, shrunk

**~455,500 people (2024), 4 districts** (Brunei-Muara — home to the capital and over 72% of the population —
Belait, Temburong, Tutong), subdivided into 39 mukims. **An absolute monarchy**: Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah holds
full executive authority under the 1959 constitution (extended after a 1962 rebellion), with no elected
national-level representatives. A British protectorate from 1888, independent in 1984.

**Brunei is this survey's version of "the one place the lines already exist," but for a different reason than
Bangsamoro.** Where Bangsamoro's borders are a recent state creation, **Brunei's borders are what remained of
a much larger precolonial sultanate that once dominated Borneo's coast and, via Sulu, extended influence
across the water to Mindanao** (3.2) — the one polity in this entire survey whose modern shape is a
*shrinkage* of a real precolonial state rather than a colonial administrative unit adopted wholesale (1.5).
**Brunei chose not to join the 1963 Malaysia federation** despite being geographically and historically
entangled with Sarawak and Sabah (which sit on either side of it) — a genuine divergence point worth keeping
in mind for how the region's post-collapse politics might run: three adjacent Bornean polities took three
different 1963 paths (Sarawak and Sabah joined on the MA63 terms now disputed; Brunei stayed out entirely and
kept its oil wealth and its sultan).

### 3.5 Singapore — the exception that proves the Chinese-minority rule

**~4.2 million residents** (3.66 million citizens, 540,000 permanent residents). Ethnic composition under the
official CMIO framework: **Chinese 75.5%, Malay 15.1% (recognized as the indigenous community), Indian 7.6%,
Others 1.8%.** Separated from the Malaysia federation in 1965, two years after joining it. A city-state with
no internal first-order subdivisions to speak of in the way this survey's other five polities have — **it
simply stays itself** on any map this project would draw, the same status Woodard's own account gives no
equivalent case in North America (the closest analogue elsewhere in this project is Nay Pyi Taw Union
Territory or a federal-territory-scale unit, not a whole nation).

### 3.6 Timor-Leste — already fully sovereign, with an odd exclave worth flagging

**14 municipalities** (⚠️ **corrected 2026-09-26**; an earlier draft of this survey said 13): Aileu, Ainaro,
Atauro, Baucau, Bobonaro, Covalima, Dili, Ermera, Lautém, Liquiçá, Manatuto, Manufahi, **Oecusse-Ambeno**,
Viqueque — a direct continuation of the Portuguese colonial administrative structure (11 municipalities plus
the Oecussi-Ambeno circumscription under Portugal), relabeled as Indonesian *kabupaten* during the 1975–1999
occupation and then simply renamed districts/municipalities at independence. **Atauro was split out of Dili as
its own municipality in 2022** — the island had been administered as part of Dili municipality until then.
**Oecusse-Ambeno is formally a Special Administrative Region (RAEOA), not an ordinary municipality** — a
distinct administrative tier with its own governance arrangement, though it is still drawn as this project's
usual first-order-subdivision unit. **Indonesia's 1975 invasion began through Oecussi itself**, a week before
the main invasion.

**Timor-Leste became internationally recognized as independent in 2002**, following a UN-administered
transition after a 1999 independence referendum — the clearest, most recent, and most completely resolved
sovereignty case in this entire survey. Nothing about its status needs deciding. **The one geographic curiosity
worth flagging for a future map stage: Oecusse-Ambeno is an exclave**, sitting inside Indonesian West Timor
territory, separated from the rest of Timor-Leste by a strip of Indonesian land — the kind of seam a map
series in this project's style would want to mark clearly rather than let a reader assume is a cartography
error.

## Part 4 — Deliberately excluded, and named

- **Papua New Guinea** — a separate sovereign state sharing the island of New Guinea with Indonesian Papua.
  Ethnically and linguistically continuous with West Papua (3.1.3) but out of scope: it was never part of the
  Dutch East Indies and its modern border with Indonesia is a colonial-Dutch/German/British-era line, not a
  question this survey's six-polity scope needs to resolve.
- **The South China Sea disputes** (the Spratly and Paracel claims, the "nine-dash line," and the
  Philippines–China and Malaysia–China maritime boundary disputes) — live, real, and touch several of this
  survey's countries, but they are maritime-boundary/resource questions rather than first-order-subdivision
  land-border questions, and are out of scope for the same reason South America's offshore basins were noted
  but not resolved in the Latin America survey.
- **The Philippine claim to Sabah**, rooted in the Sulu Sultanate's historical cession, is named at 3.2 and
  3.3 because it explains *why* the claim exists, but whether or how it matters for a post-collapse map is not
  addressed here.
- **Wolters on the mandala, Tambiah on the galactic polity, and a rigorous linguistic-map check of the
  Wallace Line/ethnolinguistic correlation (2.2)** — all named but not read/checked in this session, on the
  same "flag rather than fill" convention the mainland survey used for Wolters, Scott, van Schendel and the
  Zomia critics.
