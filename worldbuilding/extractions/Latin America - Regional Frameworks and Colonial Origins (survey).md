# Latin America — Regional Frameworks and Colonial Origins (Reconnaissance Survey)

**Date:** 2026-09-23
**Type:** reconnaissance survey — what exists to work from, plus a first-pass synthesis. Not a
country-by-country buildout, and not a set of final nations.

**Purpose:** find what fills, for Latin America, the slot that Colin Woodard's *American Nations* fills for
North America — a framework of durable regional cultures defined by founding populations and founding
institutions, usable as the analytical basis for designing post-collapse successor nations. Companion to
`worldbuilding/extractions/American_Nations_Extraction.md`; that file's Part Two (nation vs. state, the
Doctrine of First Effective Settlement, core/domain/sphere) and its Part Four checklist are the rubric used
throughout here and are not restated.

**Scope:** everything south of Sonora — all of Mexico south of the northern-tier states, all of Central
America, **the Caribbean islands**, and all of South America **including the Guianas**.

**Provenance:** all wording original; no source passage reproduced. Numeric tables are restated from named
sources. Every substantive claim carries a URL in the source list. Anything not verified in this session is
flagged ⚠️.

## Headline findings

1. **No one has written a "Woodard for Latin America," and no one has drawn the map.** That gap is real,
   and it is the author's opportunity. What exists is a set of components, some of them excellent.
2. **The closest analogue is Darcy Ribeiro**, who made the same analytical move as Woodard — sorting the
   Americas by founding population rather than by state border — in *As Américas e a Civilização* (1970),
   **forty-one years before *American Nations*.** He then did it again, at the internal-region resolution,
   for Brazil alone in *O Povo Brasileiro* (1995). Ribeiro is the spine of this document.
3. **The rigorous analogue to the Doctrine of First Effective Settlement is the colonial-institutions
   economics literature** — Engerman & Sokoloff, Acemoglu/Johnson/Robinson, and Melissa Dell. Woodard
   asserts institutional persistence from narrative history; these authors measure it, and the measurements
   hold. Dell's mining-mita result is the single cleanest demonstration of Woodard's core premise that
   exists anywhere, for any continent.
4. **Brazil is not one nation and is not five administrative regions.** Ribeiro already answered this on his
   own authority, with five *Brasis* that cut across the state and regional boundaries. See Part 4.
5. **The single strongest predictor of everything downstream is Indigenous population density at contact.**
   Dense, sedentary, already-taxed populations got extraction — encomienda, mita, tribute, hacienda. Sparse
   populations got settlers, smallholdings and, eventually, broad-access institutions. This is the same
   mechanism Woodard describes, expressed as a continuous variable across a hemisphere.

## Part 1 — Does a "Woodard for Latin America" exist?

**Verdict: no single book does the job. Three partial frameworks do most of it, one of them very well, and
the map itself has never been drawn.** That last point is worth stating plainly rather than softening.
Woodard's distinctive contribution was not the idea that founding populations leave durable cultural
residue — cultural geographers had that from Zelinsky in 1973, and Ribeiro had the Latin American version in
1970. Woodard's contribution was assigning *bounded territory* to each culture, with cores, domains and
spheres, and arguing the borders. **Nobody has done that for Latin America.** The frameworks below supply
the populations and the institutions; none of them supplies the map.

### 1.1 Darcy Ribeiro — the closest thing that exists

Darcy Ribeiro (1922–1997) was a Brazilian anthropologist, ethnologist of Amazonian peoples, education
minister and later senator. His *As Américas e a Civilização* (1970) sorts the entire hemisphere into three
categories of people, defined by **what the colonial encounter did to the founding population** rather than
by language, religion, state or race:

- **Povos Testemunho** — "witness peoples." Populations that are the surviving remnant of a conquered high
  civilization, carrying its cultural substrate forward beneath a Hispanic surface. Mexico, Guatemala,
  Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia.
- **Povos Novos** — "new peoples." Populations that did not exist before the colonial encounter and were
  manufactured by it, out of European, African and Indigenous components fused under a plantation or
  mining regime. Brazil, Venezuela, Colombia, Chile, Paraguay, the Antilles.
- **Povos Transplantados** — "transplanted peoples." European populations moved wholesale into territory
  whose prior inhabitants were displaced rather than absorbed, reproducing a European society abroad.
  Argentina, Uruguay — and, in Ribeiro's own scheme, the United States and Canada.

A fourth category, **Povos Emergentes** ("emerging peoples"), covers populations reconstituting themselves
as modern nations out of a pre-colonial base, which Ribeiro applied mainly outside the Americas.

Three things make this the real analogue rather than a loose parallel:

1. **It overrides state borders by design.** Ribeiro's categories cut across republics and assign the United
   States and Argentina to the same type — an unfamiliar grouping that is the whole point, exactly as
   Woodard's placement of southern Ontario inside the Midlands is the point.
2. **It is founding-population analysis, not present-demography analysis.** A Povo Testemunho stays a Povo
   Testemunho regardless of how mestizo its current population is, because the category describes what
   happened at first contact and what institutional substrate that left. This is the Doctrine of First
   Effective Settlement in different vocabulary.
3. **The timing.** 1970. Woodard published in 2011, and Zelinsky's formulation of first effective settlement
   is 1973. Ribeiro was three years ahead of the doctrine Woodard builds on and four decades ahead of the
   book.

Ribeiro's intellectual lineage is worth knowing because it explains both his strengths and the standard
objection to him: he worked from the neo-evolutionist anthropology of Leslie White and Julian Steward and
from V. Gordon Childe's Marxist archaeology, and he organized human history around a sequence of
technological revolutions. The objection is that his typology has a developmental ladder buried in it. For
worldbuilding purposes that matters less than it does for anthropology, but it is why Ribeiro is cited
today more as a national-interpretive classic than as a working analytical tool.

His second contribution, *O Povo Brasileiro* (1995), is the internal-resolution version, and it is
effectively *American Nations* for Brazil. It gets its own treatment in Part 4.

### 1.2 Charles Wagley — the three culture spheres

Independently and thirteen years earlier, the American anthropologist Charles Wagley (1913–1991), a
Brazilianist and Amazonian fieldworker, presented "Plantation America: A Culture Sphere" at a Caribbean
studies symposium in **1957**, dividing the New World into three spheres on the basis of environment,
language, history and mode of production:

- **Euro-America**
- **Indo-America**
- **Plantation America**

The mapping onto Ribeiro is close to one-to-one: Euro-America ≈ Povos Transplantados, Indo-America ≈ Povos
Testemunho, Plantation America ≈ Povos Novos. Two scholars working in different countries, different
languages and different theoretical traditions arrived at the same three-way split, which is a reasonable
sign that the split tracks something real rather than one author's taste.

Wagley's framing has one advantage over Ribeiro's for this project: it is explicitly a **culture *sphere***
concept, i.e. already geographic. It names zones, not peoples. That makes it the natural coarse resolution
for Part 3, and it is used that way below.

Wagley's 1959 paper on "social race" — comparing how racial categories are constructed differently across
the Americas — is the other piece of his that matters here, because it is the reason the same African-
descended founding population produces a caste society in one place and a fluid-but-stratified one in
another. It is the mechanism behind Ribeiro's *barroco*/*gótico* contrast in Part 4.

### 1.3 The anthropological culture-area tradition

Older, narrower, and about pre-contact peoples rather than colonial societies — but it is the source of most
of the region names in general use, and it defines the *substrate* every colonial institution was laid on
top of.

- **Mesoamerica** — defined by Paul Kirchhoff in 1943 as a culture area of shared traits (maize agriculture,
  the 260-day ritual calendar, ballcourts, screenfold books, human sacrifice, specific market and tribute
  structures) running from central Mexico through Guatemala, Belize, El Salvador and western Honduras. This
  is the single most durable culture-area concept in the Americas and is still in active scholarly use.
- **Aridoamerica** and **Oasisamerica** — also Kirchhoff (1954), popularized in the "Aridoamerica" form by
  Gary Paul Nabhan (1985). Aridoamerica is the arid gatherer zone of northern Mexico and the US Southwest,
  receiving roughly 120–160 mm of rain a year; Oasisamerica is the farming zone within it. The
  Mesoamerica/Aridoamerica frontier matters enormously for this project: it is roughly where sedentary,
  taxable, conquerable society stopped and mobile Chichimeca society began, and therefore roughly where the
  Spanish colonial system of encomienda and tribute stopped working and had to be replaced by presidios,
  missions and ranching. **It is, in other words, the southern edge of Woodard's El Norte, seen from the
  other side.** The Mexican anthropologist Guillermo Bonfil Batalla argued the boundary should be read as a
  shifting climatic gradient with constant traffic across it rather than a wall — useful, because it means
  the frontier is a zone, which is exactly Woodard's core/domain/sphere model.
- **Julian Steward's *Handbook of South American Indians*** (Smithsonian Institution, 7 volumes, 1940–47,
  over 100 contributors) established the standard four-way division of South America that is still the
  default vocabulary: **Marginal Tribes** (vol. 1, 1946 — the Southern Cone, Gran Chaco, eastern Brazil),
  **Andean Civilizations** (vol. 2, 1946), **Tropical Forest Tribes** (vol. 3, 1948 — Amazonia and the
  Guianas), **Circum-Caribbean Tribes** (vol. 4, 1948 — Central America, northwestern South America, the
  West Indies). ⚠️ The standard modern criticism is that Steward's sequence is a complexity ladder dressed
  as geography — "Marginal" means "simple," and the scheme systematically underrates the societies it puts
  at the bottom. Amazonian archaeology since the 1970s has demolished the "Tropical Forest" tier's
  assumptions specifically (see 2.5 and Part 3). Use the region names; do not inherit the ranking. Paul
  Radin's contemporary review already flagged the Circum-Caribbean volume as the weakest.

### 1.4 Joel Garreau — why *Nine Nations* does not fill the slot

Joel Garreau's *The Nine Nations of North America* (Houghton Mifflin, 1981) is the obvious candidate and it
genuinely does not do the job, for a reason worth being precise about.

Garreau's nine, each with a capital: **New England** (Boston), **The Foundry** (Detroit), **Dixie**
(Atlanta), **The Breadbasket** (Kansas City), **The Islands** (Miami), **MexAmerica** (Los Angeles),
**Ecotopia** (San Francisco), **The Empty Quarter** (Denver), **Quebec** (Quebec City). He treats
Washington DC, Manhattan below Harlem, Hawaii and northern Alaska as aberrations outside the scheme. His
stated premise is the same as Woodard's — conventional national and state borders are artificial and
obscure more than they reveal.

Two of the nine reach into this survey's scope:

- **MexAmerica** — the borderlands plus northern Mexico, the rough analogue of Woodard's El Norte, though
  Garreau's version is a functional economic zone (maquiladora manufacturing, migration, irrigated
  agriculture, a shared labor market) rather than a founding-settlement culture.
- **The Islands** — Miami plus the Caribbean, treated as a single trade-and-diaspora sphere with Miami as
  its capital. This is a genuinely useful observation and it is the only place in either framework where
  the Caribbean is treated as a coherent region with a mainland node.

But **Garreau is present-tense and economic where Woodard is historical and institutional.** Garreau asks
where the money, the newspapers and the commuting patterns actually flow *now*; Woodard asks who founded the
place and what they built. Garreau's nations are therefore artifacts of a late-twentieth-century economy and
would not survive the collapse of that economy, which makes them close to useless for post-catastrophe
worldbuilding. Contemporary assessment of the book has been that it captures readily observable patterns in
accessible language rather than establishing a new analysis — fair, and it is the same reason it cannot
carry this weight. Garreau also stops at the Rio Grande's near side: everything south of MexAmerica is
simply absent.

### 1.5 Woodard's own stated exclusions

Worth recording, because it means the gap is acknowledged rather than accidental. Per the existing
extraction (Part Two, "Deliberate scope limits"), Woodard explicitly leaves out South Florida's Cuban
diaspora culture, Hawaii's Polynesian culture, **several distinct cultures within central Mexico and Central
America**, and **a proposed African-American-diaspora "Creole nation" spanning the Caribbean and Brazil** —
not because he denies they exist, but as a deliberate stopping point to avoid infinite subdivision.

Two of those four exclusions are precisely this survey's subject. Woodard knew the Latin American material
belonged in the framework and declined to do it. Notably, the "Creole nation" he names and sets aside is
almost exactly Wagley's Plantation America and Ribeiro's Povos Novos — which means all three authors
independently identified the same entity, and only two of them wrote it down.

### 1.6 The national interpretive canon

Each Latin American country has a tradition of book-length interpretations of its own national character.
These are not regional frameworks — they are usually arguments about a single national essence, which is the
opposite of what Woodard does — but they are where the cultural detail lives, and a worldbuilder will want
them for texture rather than for structure.

- **Gilberto Freyre, *Casa-Grande e Senzala*** (1933) — Brazil's plantation household as the formative
  institution of Brazilian society; patriarchal hierarchy radiating outward from the sugar estate, and
  racial mixing reframed as constitutive rather than degenerate. Enormously influential, and now heavily
  criticized for softening colonial brutality and romanticizing a hybridity produced by coercion. Read it
  for the mechanism of the *engenho* household, not for its conclusions.
- ⚠️ **Not verified in this session, listed from general knowledge and to be checked before use:** Sérgio
  Buarque de Holanda, *Raízes do Brasil* (1936, the "cordial man" and the Iberian inheritance); Caio Prado
  Júnior, *Formação do Brasil Contemporâneo* (1942, Brazil as an export colony first and a society second);
  Claudio Véliz, *The Centralist Tradition of Latin America* (1980, the argument that Latin American
  centralism is an Iberian institutional inheritance rather than a modern pathology); Guillermo Bonfil
  Batalla, *México Profundo* (1987, a surviving Mesoamerican civilization beneath the Mexican state).
  Véliz and Bonfil Batalla are the two most likely to be directly useful, because both make
  institutional-persistence arguments of the Woodard type.

### 1.7 What is genuinely missing

To state it once, plainly:

**There is no published map of Latin American culture-nations with bounded territories, cores, domains and
spheres.** Ribeiro assigns categories to countries. Wagley assigns spheres to broad zones without drawing
edges. Steward and Kirchhoff map pre-contact peoples, not colonial or modern societies. Garreau maps a
1981 economy and stops at the border. The economists in Part 2 map institutions to administrative units —
districts, provinces, countries — because that is where their data live, not because those are the real
boundaries.

The boundary-drawing has not been done. Part 3 is a first attempt, offered for argument, not as an answer.

## Part 2 — The colonial-institutions literature

**This is Latin America's Doctrine of First Effective Settlement, with regressions.** Woodard asserts that
founding institutions persist and demonstrates it through narrative history. A large economics literature
makes the same claim about the same hemisphere and tests it quantitatively. It is the strongest material
available for this project, and it is more rigorous than Woodard's own evidentiary base.

The three pillars, in order of how closely they track Woodard's method:

### 2.1 Engerman & Sokoloff — factor endowments and paths of development

Stanley Engerman (Rochester) and Kenneth Sokoloff (UCLA) developed this thesis across a series of papers
from 1997 onward. The fullest statement retrieved for this survey is **"Factor Endowments, Inequality, and
Paths of Development Among New World Economies," NBER Working Paper 9259 (October 2002)**, with a later
restatement in **"Colonialism, Inequality, and Long-Run Paths of Development," NBER Working Paper 11057
(January 2005)**.

**The thesis.** Their starting puzzle is that the colonies that were *richest first* are the ones that
ended up *poorest*. The areas Europeans reached first and valued most — the Caribbean, Brazil, Mexico,
Peru — fell behind; the areas latecomers had to settle for, because nobody else wanted them, pulled ahead.
For the first two and a half centuries after contact, income differences across the Americas were small,
and parts of the Caribbean and South America were richer per head than the colonies that became the United
States and Canada.

Their explanation rejects the two standard ones. It is **not** British institutional heritage and it is
**not** Protestantism, and their disproof is elegant: the British colonies themselves diverged wildly from
one another despite sharing a legal system, a language, a religion and a migrant pool. Barbados and
Massachusetts were both English and both Protestant and developed into opposite kinds of society. Meanwhile
each British colony strongly resembled its *non-British neighbors* — British Jamaica looks like French
Saint-Domingue and Portuguese Pernambuco, not like Connecticut. Nationality of the colonizer does not
predict the outcome. Something local does.

**The three founding types.** What predicts the outcome is the **initial factor endowment** — what the land
could grow, what was under it, and how many people were already on it:

1. **Plantation colonies.** Climate and soil suited to crops of high world-market value that are most
   efficiently grown at scale with coerced labor — above all sugar, also coffee, cacao, indigo, cotton and
   rice. The Caribbean and coastal Brazil. Populations came to be dominated by enslaved Africans purchased
   on the international market. Extreme inequality of wealth, human capital and political power appeared
   almost immediately and was not a later degeneration.
2. **Mineral-and-dense-population colonies.** Rich mineral deposits sitting under large, sedentary,
   already-organized Indigenous populations, with pre-existing tribute and labor institutions the Spanish
   could take over rather than build. Mexico and Peru. The Spanish practice of granting claims on land,
   Native labor and mineral wealth to a small elite — a practice Engerman and Sokoloff note was itself
   significantly shaped by the Indigenous institutions already in place — produced extreme inequality by a
   different route to the same destination.
3. **Smallholder colonies.** Climates suited to mixed grain-and-livestock farming, which has limited
   economies of scale, uses few slaves profitably, and rewards the family-sized farm. Few Indigenous people
   on the ground. The northern mainland. These places were *unattractive*: only a small fraction of
   migrants to the New World chose them. But that same unattractiveness produced relatively homogeneous
   populations with relatively equal distributions of wealth and human capital.

**The causal chain.** Endowment → initial inequality → the *kind* of institutions a society builds →
persistence. Where the founding distribution was equal, societies built broad-access institutions: wide
suffrage, public schooling, land policies that sold public land cheaply in small parcels, patent systems
open to ordinary people. Where the founding distribution was extreme, elites built institutions that kept
access narrow — restricted franchises, minimal public goods, land policy that concentrated holdings — and
those institutions then reproduced the inequality that created them. The loop is self-sustaining, which is
why it lasts centuries. Their punchline for economists is that **institutions are not exogenous**; you
cannot treat them as a given and ask what they cause, because something caused them.

**The numbers.** These are the most directly usable part of the whole literature for worldbuilding, because
they show the founding populations rather than describing them.

Three things fall out of this table that matter more than anything else in the survey:

- **Spanish America was still half Indigenous in 1935.** Not as a remnant — as half the population. The
  Indigenous substrate in Mexico, Guatemala, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia was never displaced in the way it
  was north of the Rio Grande; it was conquered, taxed and layered over. Ribeiro's Povos Testemunho is
  visible right there in the numbers.
- **Brazil inverted.** From 94% Indigenous to 55.6% Black in 255 years. That is the plantation complex
  doing demographic replacement at a speed the Spanish system never approached.
- **North America is the outlier, not the norm.** 99.6% Indigenous to 79.6% white in the same 255 years is
  the anomalous trajectory in the hemisphere. Woodard's eleven nations are all variations within that one
  outlier case.

measured. It is also, read forward, the Mexican Revolution's cause stated in a single number.

### 2.2 Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson — extractive versus inclusive institutions

Two papers, both retrieved in full.

**"The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical Investigation"** (NBER Working Paper 7771,
2000; published *American Economic Review* 91:5, 2001). The problem they solve is that institutions and
income are obviously correlated but the causation could run either way — rich countries can afford good
institutions. Their instrument is **settler mortality**: European death rates in each colony in the early
colonial period, from records of soldiers, bishops and sailors. Where Europeans could survive, they settled,
brought their families, and built institutions protecting their own property rights, because they expected
to live under them. Where Europeans died fast, they built extraction machinery instead — institutions
designed to move wealth out, with no property-rights protection for anybody, because nobody planned to
stay. Those institutions persisted after independence, because the new local elites found them useful and
inherited them intact.

⚠️ **Standard criticisms, noted because they are well known and this survey should not overstate the
paper.** David Albouy has argued the mortality data are constructed unreliably and that the results are
fragile to reasonable alternative codings. Glaeser, La Porta, López-de-Silanes and Shleifer have argued
what settlers actually brought was human capital rather than institutions, and that the schooling channel
explains the same variance. Neither critique was independently verified in this session. The thesis is
influential and contested, not settled.

**"Reversal of Fortune: Geography and Institutions in the Making of the Modern World Income Distribution"**
(NBER Working Paper 8460, 2001; published *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 117:4, 2002). This is the more
useful of the two for worldbuilding, because it is about persistence and its opposite.

Their design: if geography drives prosperity, relative rankings should persist, because geography does not
change. If institutions drive it, a big enough institutional shock should scramble the rankings. European
colonization was that shock. They proxy 1500 prosperity two ways — **urbanization rate** (share living in
towns above 5,000, following Bairoch's compilations, on the argument that only high agricultural
productivity can support cities) and **population density** (McEvedy and Jones) — and find the correlation
with income today is **negative**. The places that were richest and densest in 1500 are the poorest now.
The result survives controls for continent, identity of the colonizing power, religion, temperature,
humidity, distance from the equator and being landlocked, and it survives dropping the "Neo-Europes" (US,
Canada, Australia, New Zealand) from the sample entirely.

**The mechanism is the part that matters here, and it is precisely a Woodard mechanism.** Colonization
produced an *institutional reversal*: Europeans installed relatively good institutions in previously poor
places and extractive ones in previously rich places. Why? Because **sparse, poor regions could not be
profitably extracted from and could be settled**, so Europeans came in numbers and built institutions
encouraging broad investment — they were building for themselves. **Dense, prosperous regions could be
profitably extracted from**: there was an existing population to force into mines and plantations, and an
existing tax-and-tribute apparatus to capture rather than construct. The Mughal, Aztec and Inca domains were
among the richest places on earth in 1500; the territories they occupied are now far poorer than the
territories of the societies that were, in 1500, materially simplest.

They also date the divergence. The reversal happened **mostly during the nineteenth century**, not at
contact — which is their argument against a "temperate drift" geographic alternative, since European
agricultural technology arrived earlier than that. Their reading is that institutions became decisive
specifically when industrialization arrived, because industrialization requires investment from a broad
cross-section of society, and extractive institutions cannot mobilize that. **Societies with concentrated
political power failed to industrialize not because they were plundered but because they were built wrong
for it.** They are explicit about distinguishing this from dependency theory: in Frank's or Rodney's or
Williams's account, the Caribbean and Central America are poor from "too much capitalism"; in AJR's, from
"the wrong type of capitalism."

**Why this matters for post-collapse design.** It supplies the causal rule in a usable form: *the
institutional character a territory acquires is set by what the founders could profitably do there, and
"what they could profitably do" is determined by how many people were already living there and how
organized those people were.* That rule can be run forward from any catastrophe, not just backward from
1492.

### 2.3 Melissa Dell — the mining mita, and the cleanest persistence result in the literature

⚠️ **Access limitation, stated up front.** The full paper could not be retrieved. The PDF returned 403 or
404 from every mirror tried: `scholar.harvard.edu`, `dash.harvard.edu`, MIT Economics, the Econometric
Society site, and the Wiley DOI resolver. What follows is built from the **complete abstract**, recovered
via the OpenAlex API (`https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.3982/ECTA8121`), with bibliographic details
confirmed against Crossref. Effect sizes and mechanisms below are the author's own, as stated in the
abstract; the body of the paper — the specification details, the robustness checks, the historical
narrative — has **not** been read.

**Melissa Dell, "The Persistent Effects of Peru's Mining Mita," *Econometrica* 78:6 (November 2010),
pp. 1863–1903.**

**The design.** The colonial mita conscripted forced mine labor from a defined set of Andean districts
between **1573 and 1812**. The catchment had a sharp, arbitrary, administratively drawn boundary. Districts
immediately inside and immediately outside that boundary are otherwise near-identical in geography,
altitude, ethnicity and pre-colonial history. Dell compares them using a **spatial regression discontinuity
design** — at the time, a novel technique in economics — which isolates the effect of the institution from
everything geography might otherwise explain.

**The result.** Two centuries after the mita was abolished, districts that were inside it have household
consumption roughly **25% lower** and child stunting roughly **6 percentage points higher** than districts
just outside.

**The mechanism, and it is counterintuitive.** Dell traces the persistence through **land tenure and public
goods provision**. Mita districts historically had **fewer large landowners** and lower educational
attainment; today they are **less integrated into road networks** and their residents are **substantially
more likely to be subsistence farmers**. The Crown kept mita districts free of haciendas so that the labor
supply stayed available for conscription. The result is that the *hacienda zone* — the one with the
oppressive landed estates — got the roads and schools, because the landowners had the political weight to
demand them, while the notionally freer communal districts got nothing and stayed isolated. Extraction's
long shadow is not simply "estates bad, smallholders good."

**Why this is the single most important paper here.** Woodard's entire premise is that an institution
planted centuries ago is still legible in behavior today, even after the founding population has dispersed,
the institution has been abolished, and the state that created it has ceased to exist. Dell demonstrates
exactly that, on a boundary, with a number attached. **A line a viceroy drew in 1573 is still visible in
household consumption data in 2010.** No result in *American Nations* is this clean, because narrative
history cannot produce one. If the author ever wants to justify the premise of the whole New North America
project to a skeptic, this is the citation.

### 2.4 The institutions themselves

Where each one operated, what it actually did, and what it left behind. These are the concrete objects the
statistical literature is measuring.

**Encomienda.** A grant from the Crown to a conquistador or official of **the right to extract labor and
tribute from a specified Indigenous population** — crucially, *not* a grant of land. The Indigenous
community kept its territory; the encomendero got its work and its output. Derived from Reconquista-era
Iberian practice of rewarding soldiers with conquered ground. In theory the encomendero owed protection and
Christian instruction in return; in practice the arrangement was close to slavery, though formally distinct,
because Isabella I had declared Native Americans Crown subjects (1501–03) and forbidden their enslavement.
Tribute was taken in metals, maize, wheat, pork and other produce. Regulated by the Laws of Burgos
(1512–13); substantially curtailed by the **New Laws of 1542**, which limited grants to two generations
instead of perpetuity and triggered open colonial revolt; legally ended in 1720, with regional abolitions
running 1782–91. Heaviest and longest-lasting in **Peru and New Spain**, where it persisted into the
eighteenth century; also Hispaniola and the rest of the Caribbean early, the Philippines outside this scope.

**Repartimiento.** The Crown's replacement for encomienda — instead of a permanent private grant, a
state-managed rotation allocating Indigenous workers temporarily among colonists. Framed as a reform to
curb abuse; functionally a nationalization of forced labor.

**Mita.** The Andean case, and the most consequential. The Inca *mit'a* was a rotating labor tax: adult
males, roughly 15 to 50, owed a set number of days a year to public works, state agriculture, military
service, roads and bridges. **Viceroy Francisco de Toledo** (in office from 1569) rebuilt it in **1573** as
a mining draft: subject communities had to supply **one-seventh of their adult male labor force at any given
time**. The catchment ultimately covered nearly **200,000 square miles** of southern Peru and what is now
Bolivia, feeding the **Potosí** silver mines and the **Huancavelica** mercury mines, mercury being required
to refine the silver. Assignment was understood as a death sentence and some conscripts chose death over
compliance. Abolished 1812. The Spanish did not invent the institution — they captured an existing one and
redirected it from infrastructure to extraction, which is AJR's mechanism in its purest observable form.

**Reducción / congregación.** Forced resettlement of dispersed Indigenous populations into planned,
grid-plan Spanish-style towns with a central plaza, church, jail and administrative building, run by a
Crown-appointed *corregidor de indios* with an Indigenous *cabildo* beneath him and hereditary *caciques*
retaining informal influence. **Toledo resettled roughly 1.4 million people into about 840 reducciones in
Peru and Bolivia between 1569 and 1575.** Purposes: direct state control, evangelization, and — decisively —
making tribute collection and mita conscription administratively possible, since a scattered population
cannot be counted or drafted.

This is the most important entry in this whole list for Woodard purposes, and it deserves emphasis. **The
reducción was a deliberate, state-directed act of first *re*-settlement at continental scale.** It
physically destroyed the Andean vertical-archipelago system — the practice of a single community farming
scattered plots across multiple altitude bands to hedge against frost, drought and blight — by concentrating
people in one place at one elevation. The settlement pattern of the modern Andes is Toledo's, not the Inca's.
Every highland town laid out on a grid around a plaza is a surviving artifact of a 1570s administrative
decision. Some communities negotiated to retain outlying fields; some fled entirely; but the template held.
Jesuit and Franciscan mission versions of the same idea — most famously the Guaraní missions of Paraguay
⚠️ (not independently verified here) — applied it in lowland South America.

**Hacienda.** A large landed estate, the term from *hacer*, to make: a productive enterprise, not merely a
holding. Unlike the plantation it was often diversified — agriculture plus mining plus manufacture — and
frequently oriented to regional rather than export markets. Unlike the encomienda it *was* a grant of land,
and the two were separate systems that ambitious colonists combined by using encomienda labor on land they
had acquired by other means. Mexico's system is conventionally dated from the 1529 grant to Hernán Cortés of
the Marquisate of the Valley of Oaxaca. Haciendas dominated the countryside in **Mexico, Guatemala, El
Salvador, New Granada, Colombia, Peru, Bolivia, Chile and Argentina**. Regional variants: **estancia**
(Argentina, Uruguay — pastoral), **fazenda** (Brazil), **finca** (Central America, often coffee), **rancho**
(smaller holdings). Labor came through permanent resident workforces, seasonal draft from Indigenous
communities, tenant cultivation by *campesinos*, and mounted stockmen — *vaqueros*, *gauchos*, *llaneros*.
⚠️ The specific tenancy forms (*inquilinaje* in Chile, *colonato* in Bolivia, *yanaconaje* in Peru, debt
peonage generally) are named here from general knowledge and were not confirmed in the sources retrieved.

Haciendas outlived independence by a century and a half and were dismantled by twentieth-century land
reform: **Bolivia after the 1952 revolution** under Paz Estenssoro; **Peru from Velasco Alvarado's 1969
agrarian reform**; **Chile 1962–73**. Mexico's ejido redistribution after 1917 belongs on this list ⚠️
(not verified in the sources retrieved). This matters for post-collapse design: the hacienda was abolished
as a *legal* form within living memory, but the settlement geography it created — a dependent village beside
a great house, roads built to serve the estate — is still on the ground.

**Plantation.** The Caribbean and Atlantic-lowland case: monocrop export agriculture, above all sugar, at a
scale requiring capital, a mill and a large coerced workforce, with the workforce bought rather than
conscripted because the local Indigenous population had already been destroyed. This is the founding
institution of Wagley's Plantation America. In the Guianas it took a distinctive form — the **polder
system**, with estates built behind sea defenses on drained coastal marsh several kilometers up the rivers,
a Dutch hydraulic-engineering solution transplanted whole. Coastal Guyana and Suriname are still shaped by
it, which is why populations there hug a narrow coastal strip with near-empty interior behind.

**Summary table.**

| Institution | Where | Founding logic | What survived it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encomienda | Mexico, Peru, Caribbean, New Granada | Capture existing tribute flows | Elite landholding class; Indigenous communal title nominally intact |
| Repartimiento | Same, post-1542 | State-managed rotation of forced labor | Administrative habit of drafting village labor |
| Mita | Southern Peru, Bolivia (Potosí, Huancavelica) | Redirect an Inca labor tax to silver | Measurable poverty inside the 1573 boundary (Dell) |
| Reducción | Andes (1.4M people, 840 towns), Mesoamerica, missions | Make populations countable and draftable | The entire modern highland settlement grid |
| Hacienda | Most of Spanish America | Land as the durable asset once labor grants decayed | Village-beside-estate geography; rural landlessness |
| Plantation | Caribbean, NE Brazil, Guianas, Atlantic lowlands | Sugar at scale with purchased labor | Afro-descended majority populations; coastal-strip settlement |

### 2.5 Indigenous population density at contact

The independent variable underneath everything above.

**William Denevan's 1992 revision** puts the hemisphere at roughly **53.9 million** in 1492, distributed:

| Region | Millions |
|---|---|
| Mexico | 17.17 |
| Andes | 15.70 |
| Rest of Mesoamerica | 5.63 |
| Lowland South America / Amazonia | 8.62 |
| Caribbean | 3.00 |
| North America (US + Canada) | 3.79 |

The range of serious estimates is wide and the debate is real: Alfred Kroeber (1939) put the hemisphere at
**8.4 million**, a figure now read as reflecting the racial assumptions of its period; Henry Dobyns (1966)
put it at **90–112 million**; late-twentieth-century consensus settled around **45–60 million**. A 2019
study cited in the literature has the population falling from over 60 million to about 6 million by 1600 —
a 90% collapse in a century, from epidemic disease, warfare and famine. Central Mexico alone is estimated to
have fallen to around one million by the late 1500s.

**What this table explains.** Engerman & Sokoloff's own footnote makes the point sharply: **Mexico alone
held about 37% of the hemisphere's Indigenous population, and Mexico plus Central America about 47%; South
America 35%; the Caribbean 10%; the future United States and Canada 8%.** The early colonizers went exactly
where the people were — Spain to Mexico, Peru and the Caribbean, Portugal to Brazil. The English, French and
Dutch arrived late and got what was left, which was the thinly populated stuff nobody had wanted. **That is
the whole of AJR's reversal restated as a demographic fact.**

⚠️ **Amazonia is the contested case and the numbers above understate it.** Betty Meggers's mid-century
position, that tropical forest soils could support only about 0.2 people per square kilometer and therefore
only small mobile bands, has been substantially overturned. Denevan's own "pristine myth" argument holds
that the landscape Europeans described as untouched wilderness was in fact a depopulated one — the
collapse ran ahead of the explorers. The evidence since: **terra preta** (Amazonian dark earths), an
engineered anthropogenic soil that made sustained agriculture possible in a hostile substrate, now
understood to underlie substantial portions of the basin; the **Marajoara** mound-builders at the Amazon
mouth and the Santarém complex; **Michael Heckenberger's** 2003 work in the Upper Xingu documenting roads,
bridges and large planned plazas; **geoglyphs in Acre** dated roughly AD 1–1250, found by Ondemar Dias from
1977 and mapped aerially by Alceu Ranzi as deforestation exposed them; raised-field agriculture in the
Llanos de Mojos ⚠️ (named from general knowledge, not confirmed in the sources retrieved); and recently
identified Upano Valley urban sites in Ecuador that predate everything previously known in the region.
Estimates for Amazonia in 1492 now run from about **5 million to over 8 million**. The Indigenous
population of the basin was about 1 million by 1900 and under 200,000 by the early 1980s.

For worldbuilding this is important in a specific way: **Amazonia is not naturally empty. It was emptied.**
A forest that once supported millions through engineered soils and managed forest gardening is a very
different post-collapse proposition from a forest that never supported anyone.

adjustment will systematically under-weight Indigenous continuity. Corrected for in Parts 3 and 5.

## Part 3 — A first-pass regional division (FOR CONSIDERATION ONLY)

**This is not a proposal for a set of nations, and it is not a map.** It is a first-pass division offered so
the author has something concrete to argue with. Every region below is a claim that can be rejected, and
each entry ends with the case against it so that rejecting it is an informed decision rather than a guess.
No borders are drawn, no states are named, no nations are derived. Research proposes; the author disposes.

**Two resolutions are given deliberately**, because merging regions later is trivial and splitting them
later means redoing the analysis.

**The coarse resolution** is Wagley's and Ribeiro's, and it has the authority of two independent scholars
behind it:

| Coarse sphere | Ribeiro's term | Regions below |
|---|---|---|
| Indo-America | Povos Testemunho | 1, 2, 7, 8 (and parts of 3) |
| Plantation America | Povos Novos | 4, 5, 6, 10 (and Brazil's Nordeste) |
| Euro-America | Povos Transplantados | 11, 12 (and Brazil's Sul) |
| *(no scholarly term — terrain-defined and never effectively colonized)* | — | 9, 13 |

That fourth row is this survey's own addition and is the weakest-supported part of the scheme. Wagley and
Ribeiro both sorted by *founding population*; Amazonia and Araucanía–Patagonia are better sorted by *what
defeated every attempt at founding*. That is Woodard's Far West pattern, and per the Part Four checklist
(item 6) a framework for this continent should contain at least one such case. It contains two, by opposite
routes, which is discussed at 9 and 13 below.

**The fine resolution is thirteen regions**, set out below. Merge candidates, if thirteen is too many, are
named in each entry.

### 1. The Mesoamerican Core

**Founding population.** The sedentary maize civilizations of central and southern Mexico — the Nahua
polities of the central basin above all, plus Mixtec, Zapotec, Totonac, Purépecha and Huastec — conquered
rather than displaced from 1519, with a thin Spanish administrative and clerical layer laid over the top.
Per Denevan, **Mexico alone held roughly 17.2 million people in 1492, about 37% of the hemisphere's
Indigenous population**, and central Mexico fell to perhaps one million by the late 1500s.

**Characteristic institution.** Encomienda over an existing tribute system, then congregación, then
hacienda. The Spanish did not have to invent a way to extract from central Mexico; the Triple Alliance had
already built one and they captured it. This is AJR's mechanism in its textbook form.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Valley of Mexico and the central highland basins — Puebla-Tlaxcala,
Toluca, Morelos. Domain: Michoacán, Oaxaca, Hidalgo, Querétaro, Guanajuato, Veracruz's central coast.
Sphere: the Bajío and the Gulf lowlands, shading north into the Aridoamerican frontier and the Republic of
Sonora's southern edge, and south into the Maya zone.

**Durably distinct because** the substrate was never replaced. Spanish America as a whole was still 59.5%
Indigenous in 1825 and 50.4% in 1935, and central Mexico is the heaviest contributor to those numbers. The
languages, the maize-beans-squash agricultural system, the market-day network, the village-and-parish
settlement pattern and the land-tenure concepts all predate Cortés and outlasted the viceroyalty.

**The case against.** "Mesoamerica" is a pre-contact culture area, and using it as a modern culture-region
smuggles in an assumption — that the substrate matters more than four centuries of Hispanic overlay and two
of national-state centralism. A reasonable alternative reading treats central Mexico as a genuinely mestizo
*new* nation in Ribeiro's sense rather than a witness people. Bonfil Batalla's *México Profundo* argues the
substrate reading ⚠️ (not verified here); the Mexican state's own twentieth-century ideology argued the
mestizo reading. The author has to pick, and the pick has consequences for whether region 1 and region 2
are one thing or two.

### 2. The Maya Highlands

**Founding population.** Maya peoples of the Chiapas and Guatemalan highlands — 22 distinct Mayan-language
nations in Guatemala alone — conquered late (Guatemala from 1524, the Petén Itzá not until 1697) and only
partially.

**Characteristic institution.** Encomienda and *repartimiento* over intact village polities, then the
coffee **finca** from the later nineteenth century, which pulled highland labor down to the Pacific piedmont
seasonally without ever dissolving the highland communities themselves.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Totonicapán, Sololá, El Quiché, Alta Verapaz, Huehuetenango. Domain: the
rest of the Guatemalan highlands, the Chiapas highlands, the Petén. Sphere: Guatemala's Pacific piedmont,
Belize's west, the Honduran and Salvadoran western fringe.

**Durably distinct because** the continuity is measurable right now. Guatemala's 2018 census recorded
**6,471,670 Indigenous people, 43.43% of the national population**, with departmental shares of **98.0% in
Totonicapán, 96.4% in Sololá, 93.0% in Alta Verapaz and 89.2% in El Quiché**. Those are not minority
enclaves; those are Indigenous-majority territories with their own languages, their own landholding norms
and their own civil-religious authority structures, presently existing, in contiguous blocks. Guatemala's
literacy figures in the Engerman & Sokoloff series — 11.3% in 1893, still 20% in 1945 — record how little
the national state ever invested in them.

**The case against.** The obvious merge is into region 1, on the grounds that Kirchhoff's Mesoamerica
includes both. Against the merge: the colonial administrations were separate (the Audiencia of Guatemala was
not New Spain's core), the coffee finca is a different founding institution from the central Mexican
hacienda and arrives three centuries later, and the present-day Indigenous share differs by a factor of
several. This survey's judgment is that they are two, but it is a genuine call.

### 3. The Central American Ladino Corridor

**Founding population.** A Spanish-and-mestizo (*ladino*) society along the Pacific slope and central
valleys from Guatemala's coast through El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica, founded on a
thinner and less organized Indigenous base than either Mesoamerica proper or the Maya highlands —
Denevan's "rest of Mesoamerica" figure of 5.63 million covers this zone and it collapsed hard.

**Characteristic institution.** The **finca** and the cattle hacienda; coffee from the mid-nineteenth
century as the organizing export, with land privatization laws that dissolved remaining communal holdings
and created both a planter oligarchy and a landless rural labor force.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the volcanic Pacific slope and central valleys — El Salvador entire, the
Guatemalan piedmont, central Honduras, Nicaragua's Pacific lowland, Costa Rica's Central Valley. Domain:
the interior highlands behind them. Sphere: the transition zones toward the Caribbean side (region 4) and
the Maya highlands (region 2).

**Durably distinct because** it is the classic small-oligarchy export republic, and its characteristic
politics — a narrow landed elite, a large landless peasantry, weak public provision, recurrent
insurgency — are a direct and well-documented product of the endowment-to-inequality-to-institutions chain
Engerman & Sokoloff describe.

**The case against.** Costa Rica is the standing exception and it is a serious one: a genuinely smallholder
coffee frontier with a much weaker oligarchy, a much earlier and broader franchise and far better public
provision, which looks more like region 11 or 12 than like El Salvador. ⚠️ The standard explanation —
that Costa Rica's Central Valley had almost no exploitable Indigenous population and therefore developed a
smallholder rather than an estate economy — is exactly the Engerman & Sokoloff prediction, but it was not
independently verified in this session. If the author accepts it, Costa Rica is a separate small region or
belongs with the Euro-American tier. The corridor is also the most artificial entry in this list: it is
defined partly by what it is not.

### 4. The Circum-Caribbean Littoral

**Founding population.** The Atlantic-facing mainland rim — a genuinely tri-partite founding population of
surviving Indigenous coastal peoples, Afro-descended populations arriving by several separate routes, and a
thin and mostly non-Iberian European presence.

**Characteristic institution.** Not the Spanish colonial system at all. The Spanish largely failed to
control this coast. What organized it instead was **English and Dutch commercial and buccaneering
contact**, logwood and mahogany cutting, and later the banana enclave. The **Miskito Kingdom** is the sharp
case: a Miskito polity in alliance with Britain from 1638, formalized by a Treaty of Friendship and
Alliance in 1740, British protectorate again 1844–60, transferred to Nicaraguan suzerainty by the 1860
Treaty of Managua with an autonomous Mosquito Reserve, and only annexed outright in **1894**. The Miskito
Sambu emerged from intermarriage between local Miskito and survivors of a wrecked slave ship in the
mid-seventeenth century. Modern composition of the coast is roughly 57% Miskito, 22% Creole, 15% Ladino, 4%
Sumu, 1% Garífuna, with Rama and Chinese minorities.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Nicaraguan and Honduran Caribbean coast; Belize; the Garífuna coast
running from Belize through Honduras. Domain: Panama's Caribbean side and the Kuna territory, Colombia's
Caribbean coast, Venezuela's Caribbean littoral. Sphere: the whole Caribbean basin, i.e. overlapping with
region 5.

**Durably distinct because** it was never effectively incorporated by the Spanish system and is still not
fully incorporated by the national states that inherited it. Nicaragua's two **autonomous regions** —
created in the 1980s as RAAN and RAAS, renamed **RACCN and RACCS in 2014** — are formal legal recognition of
exactly that. San Basilio de Palenque in Colombia, a Maroon settlement founded in the early seventeenth
century by Benkos Biohó and given official recognition in **1772**, still exists and still speaks
Palenquero. This is a coast that has been negotiating its separateness from the highland capitals for four
hundred years.

**The case against.** It is a rim, not a bloc: a two-thousand-kilometer arc of enclaves with the sea between
them rather than a contiguous territory, which strains the core/domain/sphere model. The merge candidate is
region 5 — Wagley would put both in Plantation America and Garreau would put both in The Islands — and
that merge is defensible if the author wants a maritime rather than territorial region.

### 5. The Plantation Caribbean — the Islands

**Founding population.** Overwhelmingly **enslaved Africans and their descendants**, on islands whose
Indigenous population — Denevan's 3 million for the Caribbean in 1492 — was destroyed within a century,
earlier and more completely than anywhere else in the hemisphere. The European founding layer is small,
absentee-inclined and non-reproducing. Later additions: indentured South Asian labor in the British
islands after emancipation ⚠️ (the Trinidad and Jamaica figures were not verified here; the Guyanese case
at region 6 was).

**Characteristic institution.** The **sugar plantation**, and nothing else comes close. The demographic
evidence is stark: of all migration to the British colonies 1630–1780, the West Indies took **66% of the
total and 83% of all enslaved Africans** — 1,088,000 of 1,308,000 — against 141,000 whites in 1630–80
falling to 42,000 in 1730–80. The islands consumed people.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the sugar islands — Barbados, Jamaica, Saint-Domingue/Haiti, Cuba's
western plantation zone, Puerto Rico's coast, the Leewards and Windwards, Martinique and Guadeloupe.
Domain: the rest of the Greater and Lesser Antilles. Sphere: the mainland rim (region 4), the Guianas
(region 6), Brazil's Nordeste, and — per Garreau — Miami.

**Durably distinct because** this is where the reversal is most extreme and most legible. **Cuba was at
167% and Barbados at 150% of US per capita income in 1700**; Barbados was at 51% by 1997. Jamaican wealth
per free person in 1774 stood at £1,200 against £38 in New England — a thirty-fold gap that describes a
society, not an economy. And the countervailing detail matters for design: **Barbadian literacy was 92.7%
in 1946 and Jamaican 76.1% in 1943**, far above any Iberian-founded territory in the hemisphere, which is
the late British colonial state's schooling showing up in the data. The Anglo-Caribbean and the
Hispano-Caribbean had the same founding institution and diverged in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

**The case against.** Treating the islands as one region flattens the sharpest internal distinction in the
whole survey — Haiti, the one place where the enslaved population overthrew the plantation and founded its
own state in 1804, is not the same object as Barbados. Four sub-splits are defensible: the Hispanic
Caribbean (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico), the Anglo-Caribbean, the Francophone Caribbean, and
Haiti alone. The survey keeps them together at this resolution and flags that as a deliberate scope choice
of exactly the kind Woodard describes making.

### 6. The Guianas

**Founding population.** The most compositionally complex founding stack anywhere in the Americas.
Indigenous Arawakan and Cariban peoples in the interior; enslaved Africans on the coastal estates;
**Maroon nations** who escaped inland and founded independent polities; **indentured South Asians** after
emancipation, who became a demographic plurality in Guyana and Suriname; **Javanese** recruited by the
Dutch; Chinese and Portuguese-Madeiran indenture on top of that.

**Characteristic institution.** The Dutch **polder plantation** — sugar estates built on drained coastal
marsh behind sea defenses, set several kilometers up the rivers to escape the mangrove fringe. Alongside it,
and just as important, the **Maroon treaty**: the Ndyuka signed the first territorial-autonomy treaty with
the Dutch on **10 October 1760**, and the Kwinti last, in **1887**. The Saramaka, Paramaka, Aluku and
Matawai are the other Surinamese Maroon nations, with their own languages — Saramaccan, Ndyuka.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the coastal polder strip of Guyana and Suriname. Domain: French Guiana's
coast, the Maroon river territories of the Suriname interior. Sphere: the Guiana Shield interior, Amapá,
Guayana Esequiba, the Orinoco delta — shading into regions 9 and 10.

**Durably distinct because** it is the one part of South America where neither Spain nor Portugal
established the founding institutions, where the working languages are English, Dutch, French and the
creoles Sranan Tongo and Guyanese Creole, and where **Maroon polities hold treaty-recognized territory that
predates the modern states by two centuries**. The 1760 Ndyuka treaty still defines Maroon territorial
rights in dealings with the Surinamese government. The population geography is also unlike anything else on
the continent: everyone lives on a narrow coastal strip and the interior is effectively another country.

**The case against.** Very small populations — under two million across all three territories — for a
region on equal footing with Mesoamerica. The merge candidate is region 5, since the founding institution
is the same plantation complex. The counter-argument is that the Maroon nations and the Indo-Guianese
plurality make this a genuinely different society from any island, and that per the coordinator's scoping
decision the Guianas are explicitly in scope and should not be collapsed by default.

### 7. The Central Andes

**Founding population.** The Quechua-speaking agricultural and pastoral societies of the Peruvian and
Ecuadorian sierra, organized by the Inca state at contact and by its predecessors before that. Denevan puts
the Andes at **15.7 million in 1492**, second only to Mexico.

**Characteristic institution.** **Reducción**, then hacienda. Toledo's resettlement of roughly **1.4 million
people into about 840 planned grid towns between 1569 and 1575** is the founding act of the modern Andes in
the most literal sense available: the towns people live in now are the ones he laid out, and the vertical
archipelago system he destroyed — one community farming plots across several altitude bands to hedge against
frost and blight — is the agricultural logic he replaced.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Peruvian sierra from Cajamarca to Cusco, and the Ecuadorian inter-
Andean corridor. Domain: the western slope down toward the coastal valleys, the eastern slope toward the
ceja de selva. Sphere: the Peruvian coast (culturally distinct and heavily creole/mestizo), the Colombian
Andes, the upper Amazon.

**Durably distinct because** the substrate held. Peru recorded **5.9 million Indigenous people in 2017**.
The Quechua language, the community (*comunidad campesina*) as a landholding unit, and the reciprocal-labor
norms survived encomienda, mita, hacienda, republic and the 1969 agrarian reform that dismantled the
haciendas. Peru's 1900 literacy of 17% ⚠️ (the W9259 table gives Bolivia at 17% in 1900; Peru's own figure
was not in the retrieved table) and its slide from 41% of US per capita income in 1800 to 15% in 1997
describe what the extractive founding did to it.

**The case against.** Splitting this from region 8 is contestable — the Inca state spanned both, Quechua and
Aymara communities interpenetrate, and most scholarship treats "the Central Andes" as one culture area.
The survey splits them anyway, for the reason given at 8.

### 8. The Altiplano

**Founding population.** Aymara-speaking societies of the high plateau around Lake Titicaca, with their own
pre-Inca state tradition — Tiwanaku, and Chiripa before it — that predates and was never fully absorbed by
Cusco.

**Characteristic institution.** **The mita**, and it is the reason this is a separate entry. The Toledo
draft of 1573 pulled one-seventh of adult males at any time from a catchment of nearly **200,000 square
miles** into Potosí and Huancavelica. That catchment had a hard administrative boundary, and **Dell's 2010
regression-discontinuity result shows the boundary is still visible: roughly 25% lower household
consumption and 6 percentage points more child stunting inside it**, running through land tenure and public
goods — fewer haciendas historically, and today less road access and more subsistence farming.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Bolivian and southern Peruvian altiplano — La Paz, El Alto, Oruro,
Puno, Potosí. Domain: the Bolivian valleys (Cochabamba, Sucre), northern Chile's and northwestern
Argentina's high country. Sphere: the Pacific slope down to Arica and Iquique, the Yungas, the Chaco fringe.

**Durably distinct because** of three things at once. First, **the mita boundary is a real, measured
institutional border**, which is the closest thing to hard evidence for Woodard's whole premise that exists
anywhere on earth — and it does not follow any modern administrative line. Second, **Bolivia is 62%
self-identified Indigenous**, the only majority-Indigenous country in South America. Third, this is a
**terrain-defined region** in the Woodard Far West sense: at an average of about 3,750 m it is the most
extensive high plateau on the planet outside Tibet, cold, dry, with violent daily temperature swings, and it
supports around 2.85 million people (2012) through a specialized adaptation — freeze-dried tubers, quinoa,
camelid pastoralism — that no incoming population has ever improved on. **It is the one high-altitude zone
on earth that already had a dense civilization adapted to it before any outsider arrived**, which makes it
the exact inverse of Woodard's Far West: environmentally defined, but by the *Indigenous* answer to the
environment rather than by an external corporation's.

**The case against.** Merging into region 7 is the mainstream scholarly position. The rebuttal is the mita
boundary: there is a measured institutional discontinuity here and there is not one between, say, Cusco and
Ayacucho. If the author wants ten regions instead of thirteen, this is the *last* merge to make, not the
first.

### 9. Amazonia

**Founding population.** ⚠️ **Contested, and the contest matters.** The old view — Betty Meggers's — was
that the basin supported only small mobile bands at around 0.2 people per square kilometer. That view has
largely collapsed. Current estimates run from **about 5 million to over 8 million in 1492**, supported by
engineered **terra preta** soils, the Marajoara mound complex at the river mouth, the Santarém culture,
Heckenberger's Upper Xingu roads, bridges and planned plazas, the Acre geoglyphs of roughly AD 1–1250, and
newly identified Upano Valley urban sites in Ecuador. Denevan's "pristine myth" argument is that the
wilderness Europeans described had *already been depopulated* by disease running ahead of them. The
Indigenous population was about 1 million by 1900 and under 200,000 by the early 1980s.

**Characteristic institution.** No durable colonial institution ever held. Mission stations, the
*descimento* slaving raids, the rubber boom of roughly 1880–1920 (which killed 40,000 Amazonians through
imported typhus and malaria alone), and the 1960s–70s road-and-colonization frontier are all episodes, not
founding settlements. **Amazonia is the one large region in this survey where first effective settlement
arguably has not happened yet, or happened before 1492 and is currently being re-attempted.**

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the main-stem Amazon and its major tributaries — Solimões, Negro, Madeira,
Tapajós, Xingu. Domain: the whole basin across Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Ecuador, Bolivia and Venezuela.
Sphere: the Guiana Shield, the Llanos de Mojos, the ceja de selva, the Cerrado fringe.

**Durably distinct because** the terrain defeats external control at a scale nothing else here matches, and
because the Indigenous territorial base is legally recognized and enormous — see region 9's overlap with
Part 5. This is the survey's **second Far West case**, and it is the opposite of the Altiplano: not a place
where an adapted civilization already existed and persisted, but a place that repeatedly destroys
unadapted arrivals and has never been held.

**The case against.** Amazonia is six countries' back country and has no political tradition of its own; it
may be a *zone* rather than a *nation*, in the same way Woodard's Far West is often criticized as a residual
category. Also, it is internally enormous — the Peruvian upper Amazon, the Brazilian main stem and the
Guiana Shield have little in common beyond forest.

### 10. The Llanos and the Orinoco

**Founding population.** Mixed Indigenous, African and European cattle-herding populations on the Orinoco
savannas of Venezuela and eastern Colombia, formed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries around
missions and open-range ranching.

**Characteristic institution.** Open-range cattle on unfenced savanna, worked by the **llanero** — the
direct structural analogue of the gaucho and of Woodard's El Norte vaquero, and, like them, a mobile,
mixed-race, weakly governed population that later supplied the manpower for regional wars.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Venezuelan llanos, Apure to Guárico. Domain: the Colombian llanos
orientales. Sphere: the Orinoco delta, the Andean piedmont, the Guiana Shield edge.

**Durably distinct because** the cattle frontier produces a recognizable and durable social type wherever it
appears — mobile, martial, hierarchical through personal loyalty to a *caudillo* rather than through
institutions, indifferent to fixed property. It is the same pattern Woodard finds in El Norte's *patrón*
system and Appalachia's portable wealth, arriving independently.

**The case against.** **This is the weakest of the thirteen and the first one to cut.** It is a way of life
more than a founding society, it has no institutional boundary anyone has measured, and it could be merged
into region 4, into region 9's sphere, or treated as one instance of a hemisphere-wide pastoral-frontier
*type* alongside the gaucho and the vaquero rather than as a region at all. It is included because the
caudillo politics it generated shaped two national histories, and because leaving it out would make the
northern-South-American interior a blank.

### 11. The Platine Plain

**Founding population.** The clearest **Povo Transplantado** in the hemisphere. A thin Spanish colonial
society on a sparse and largely non-sedentary Indigenous base, followed by **mass European immigration** in
the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — Italian and Spanish above all — that did not assimilate
into an existing majority but effectively became one.

**Characteristic institution.** The **estancia** — extensive cattle ranching on the pampas — later overlaid
by grain export agriculture, refrigerated meat, and a strongly centralized port-capital economy at Buenos
Aires and Montevideo.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the humid pampas of Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, Entre Ríos, Córdoba, plus
Uruguay entire. Domain: the Argentine litoral and the Paraná corridor. Sphere: northwest Argentina
(genuinely Andean and belonging to region 8's domain), Paraguay, Rio Grande do Sul, northern Patagonia.

**Durably distinct because** the founding distribution was comparatively equal and the institutions followed:
**Argentine literacy reached 45.6% by 1895 and 73% by 1925**, far above every Indigenous-substrate or
plantation society in the hemisphere, and Argentina stood at **102% of US per capita income in 1800**. The
counter-evidence is equally instructive and should be kept in view: Argentina's voting share was **1.8% in
1896**, and landownership in the Patagonian and Chaco territories in 1895 ran from **6.6% in Tierra del
Fuego and 9.7% in La Pampa to 35.2% in Chubut** — nothing like the 74.5% US or 87.1% Canadian figures. The
Platine plain is Euro-American in founding population but its land tenure was oligarchic, which is why
Engerman & Sokoloff treat Argentina as a case where a favorable endowment still produced concentrated
institutions.

**The case against.** Paraguay does not fit and is jammed into the sphere here. Paraguay is
Guaraní-speaking, has a Jesuit-mission founding institution unlike anything else in this region, and
Ribeiro classes it as a **Povo Novo**, not a transplanted people — the opposite tier. ⚠️ The Jesuit
reduction system of Paraguay was not independently verified in this session. If the author wants a
fourteenth region, Paraguay–Guaraní is the strongest candidate.

### 12. Central Chile

**Founding population.** Spanish settlers on a moderately dense Mapuche and Picunche base, hemmed into a
narrow Mediterranean-climate valley between the Atacama, the Andes and the Bío-Bío frontier, with later
German, Croatian and Basque immigration ⚠️ (immigration detail not verified here).

**Characteristic institution.** The **hacienda** with resident tenant labor, dismantled by land reform
**between 1962 and 1973**; and, from the nineteenth century, nitrate and then copper mining in the north as
a separate externally-capitalized extraction economy.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: the Central Valley from Santiago to Concepción. Domain: Valparaíso, the
Norte Chico, the near south. Sphere: the northern desert mining zone, and Araucanía to the south.

**Durably distinct because** Chile is the most geographically bounded society in the hemisphere — desert
north, ocean west, Andes east, hostile frontier south — which produced unusually strong state capacity and
an unusually continuous institutional history. Literacy ran 18% in 1865, 43% in 1900 and 76% by 1945, and
relative income held roughly steady (46% of the US in 1800, 42% in 1997) where Peru's and Mexico's fell by
half or more.

**The case against.** Merging into region 11 gives a single "Southern Cone." That merge is common and
defensible. Against it: Chile's hacienda-with-tenants is a different founding institution from the pampas
estancia, Chile received far less mass immigration, and the two societies are separated by the highest
mountain range in the hemisphere, which in a post-collapse setting is not a detail.

### 13. Araucanía and Patagonia

**Founding population.** The **Mapuche**, who are the only people in the Americas to have defeated both the
Inca and the Spanish and held a recognized frontier for three centuries — the Inca advance stopped at the
Maule; after the Battle of Curalaba in 1598 the Mapuche destroyed every Spanish town south of the Bío-Bío,
and the river became a genuine international border with parliaments, treaties and trade across it until
Chile's **Occupation of Araucanía (1861–1883)** and Argentina's Conquest of the Desert took the territory by
force. Beyond them, the Tehuelche, Selk'nam and Kawésqar of the far south, and the Argentine and Chilean
settler and immigrant populations that arrived after 1880.

**Characteristic institution.** For three centuries, **the frontier itself** — a negotiated, armed,
trade-crossed boundary rather than a colonial administration. After 1883, military occupation, land
allotment, and sheep estancias on Patagonian grassland at very low density.

**Core / domain / sphere.** Core: Araucanía and Los Ríos in Chile, Neuquén and Río Negro in Argentina.
Domain: the Chilean Lake District and Chiloé, Chubut. Sphere: Santa Cruz, Magallanes, Tierra del Fuego,
and — for what it is worth here — the South Atlantic approaches.

**Durably distinct because** the Mapuche are still there, still organized, and still contesting the same
territory. Chile's 2024 census records approximately **1.6 million Mapuche, about 8.8% of the national
population and 77.16% of Chile's Indigenous people**; Argentina's 2022 census records **145,783**. Land
recovery movements — the Council of All Lands, the Coordinadora Arauco-Malleco — have pressed ancestral
claims continuously since 1990, and the Chilean state has prosecuted them under counter-terrorism law,
designating several as illegal organizations in 2022. This is a live territorial dispute with a
four-hundred-year record, not a heritage question. Patagonia itself is the third terrain-defined case:
extremely low density, wind, cold, and an economy that has never supported many people.

**The case against.** Araucanía and Patagonia are arguably two things — a contested Indigenous homeland and
an empty settler periphery — bundled because they are adjacent. A defensible alternative is to treat
Araucanía as an Indigenous region on the First Nation model and Patagonia as an unclaimed frontier.

Named rather than silently dropped, following checklist item 9:

- **The Gran Chaco** — a distinct dry-forest zone across Paraguay, Bolivia and northern Argentina with its

- **The Pacific Afro-descendant littoral of Colombia and Ecuador** — Chocó, Esmeraldas: a genuinely distinct

- **Island-by-island distinctions within the Caribbean**, especially Haiti, per region 5.
- **The Peruvian and Ecuadorian coastal strip**, which is creole and mestizo and quite distinct from the

- **Northern Mexico** — out of scope by the author's decision, and already Woodard's El Norte and the

- **Urban primate cities as their own cultures** — Mexico City, São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Lima and Bogotá

### The Falklands corridor — checked

The narrative requirement is a traversable route: Midwestland → Greater New North America → South America →
Falklands. Against the division above, a traveler moving south through the continent crosses, at minimum:
**region 4** (Caribbean littoral landfall), then either the Andean spine (**7**, **8**) or the Atlantic side
(**Brazil**, Part 4), then **11** and/or **12**, then **13**, then the South Atlantic.

**The division does not break the corridor, and one route is notably clean.** The Atlantic route — Caribbean
coast, Brazilian littoral, Platine plain, Patagonia, Falklands — passes through four regions, all of them
coastal, all of them historically connected by sea trade, and all of them with port cities that in any
plausible post-collapse setting would be the surviving nodes. The Andean route is harder: it crosses the
mita boundary, the most institutionally scarred terrain in the hemisphere, and it passes through region 13,
which is the one place on the itinerary with a live four-century territorial dispute.

Two flags for the author:

1. **The Andean route is the dramatically interesting one and the Atlantic route is the plausible one.** If
   the escort sequence needs jeopardy, route it through 7, 8 and 13; if it needs to be survivable, route it
   down the Atlantic.
2. **Region 13 is the unavoidable final leg either way.** Every path to the Falklands ends in
   Araucanía–Patagonia. Whatever the author decides that region is — Mapuche successor nation, settler
   remnant, contested frontier, or empty — it is the last thing the escorted character passes through, and
   it should be designed with that scene in mind rather than as an afterthought.

## Part 4 — Brazil

Brazil needs its own treatment because it is a continent-sized country with genuinely different founding
populations in different parts of it, and because — uniquely in this survey — **the analytical work has
already been done by a named scholar with standing, and the author can adopt it rather than invent it.**

### 4.1 The administrative map (IBGE)

Brazil's official five-region division. Useful for scale and for economic weight; **not** a culture map.

| Region | States | Share of area | Population | Density | Economic character |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Norte | AC, AP, AM, PA, RO, RR, TO | 45.27% | 17.3M | 4.69/km² | Mining, açaí, hydro; river transport, few roads |
| Nordeste | AL, BA, CE, MA, PB, PE, PI, RN, SE | 18.29% | 54.6M | 35.04/km² | Tropical fruit, cocoa, cashew, wind, salt, textiles, tourism |
| Centro-Oeste | GO, MT, MS, DF | 18.86% | 16.2M | 10.05/km² | Soy, maize, cotton, cattle, nickel; least industrialized |
| Sudeste | ES, MG, RJ, SP | 10.85% | 84.8M | 91.44/km² | ~49% of national GDP; autos, steel, electronics, coffee, oil |
| Sul | PR, RS, SC | 6.75% | 29.9M | 51.83/km² | Highest HDI; machinery, autos, IT, soy, wheat, wine, coal |

Two facts to hold on to. **The Sudeste is a tenth of the land and half the economy.** And **the Norte is
45% of the land and 4.7% of the people** — an emptiness that is, per Part 2.5, not natural but historical.

### 4.2 Ribeiro's five Brasis — the culture map

*O Povo Brasileiro* (1995), thirty years in the writing, sorts Brazil by **founding population and founding
economy**, not by state lines:

- **Brasil Crioulo** — the Atlantic coastal zone founded by enslaved Africans and the sugar *engenho*.
  Heaviest in the Northeastern sugar belt, Bahia and Pernambuco above all.
- **Brasil Caboclo** — the Amazonian and riverine zone founded by Indigenous-European mixing, on an
  extractive economy (rubber, forest products) and a river-based settlement logic.
- **Brasil Sertanejo** — the semi-arid Northeastern interior: cattle, drought adaptation, a distinct religious
  and musical culture, a population that is neither plantation nor Indigenous but a third thing made by the
  backlands.
- **Brasil Caipira** — the São Paulo and southern Minas interior, founded by Tupi-Portuguese mixing and the
  bandeirante expeditions, on subsistence farming before coffee arrived.
- **Brasis Sulinos** — the far south: Azorean, German, Italian and Polish immigrant colonies plus the gaúcho
  pastoral tradition of the campanha, with a development trajectory unlike the rest of the country.

Ribeiro's larger argument is about *how* Brazil was made rather than of what: he contrasts a Brazilian
"baroque" racism, assimilationist and organized around *mestiçagem*, with an Anglo-American "gothic" racism
organized around segregation — and concludes, memorably, that Brazilian identity is founded on
***ninguendade***, a condition of belonging to nobody, in which the population is defined by having been
cut loose from every ancestral culture rather than by inheriting one. That is a genuinely different
founding mechanism from anything in Woodard, and it is the single most useful idea in this survey for a
writer: **Povos Novos are not blends of their ancestors. They are what is left after everyone's ancestry was
taken away.**

### 4.3 Where the two maps disagree — which is the interesting part

| Ribeiro's Brasil | IBGE region(s) it sits in | The mismatch |
|---|---|---|
| Crioulo | Nordeste coast, parts of Sudeste (RJ), Bahia | Coastal strip only; the Nordeste's interior is a different Brasil entirely |
| Sertanejo | Nordeste interior, northern MG, parts of GO/TO | Cuts *across* the Nordeste/Sudeste/Centro-Oeste boundaries |
| Caipira | Interior SP, southern MG, northern PR | Cuts across Sudeste/Sul; ignores the SP state line completely |
| Caboclo | Norte, western MA, parts of MT | Roughly matches Norte, but reaches into the Nordeste via Maranhão |
| Sulino | Sul, parts of SP | Roughly matches Sul, but the gaúcho campanha and the immigrant colony belt are two different things inside it |

**The headline mismatch is the Nordeste.** IBGE treats it as one region of 54.6 million people. Ribeiro
splits it in two along the rain line: a coastal **Crioulo** society founded by the sugar plantation and an
African-descended majority, and an interior **Sertanejo** society founded by cattle on semi-arid land with a
completely different demographic and economic history. These are not variants of each other. They have
different founding institutions, different founding populations, different religions in practice and
different politics. Any division that keeps the Nordeste whole is choosing an administrative boundary over
an institutional one, which is exactly the error Woodard's method exists to prevent.

The second mismatch is **Caipira**, which straddles São Paulo, Minas Gerais and Paraná and corresponds to no
administrative unit at all. It is the clearest demonstration that Ribeiro's map is the Woodard-type map.

### 4.4 Settlement history by layer

The five Brasis are the *result*; this is the sequence that produced them.

1. **Donatary captaincies, 1534 onward.** King John III colonized Brazil without spending Crown money by
   granting fifteen hereditary *capitanias* to twelve *donatários*, running coastwise north to south and
   inland to the Tordesillas line. **The first was Pernambuco, granted 24 September 1534.** Almost all
   failed — Indigenous resistance, undercapitalization, donees dying without heirs — and all eventually
   reverted to or were bought back by the Crown. **Only two succeeded: Pernambuco, on sugar, and São
   Vicente, on inland expeditions.** Those two successes are the seeds of Brasil Crioulo and Brasil Caipira
   respectively, and the divergence between them starts in the 1530s. When Bahia's donee was killed in 1548,
   the Crown installed a royal governor at Salvador in **1549** and direct administration began.
2. **The sugar complex, 1550–1650.** Engenhos in Pernambuco and the Bahian Recôncavo, financed partly with
   Dutch capital, worked by enslaved Africans. Portugal moved **1,187,000 Africans against 523,000 Europeans
   across 1500–1760, a ratio of 2.27 to 1** — and in 1640–1700 that ratio hit **4.5 to 1**. Brazil went from
   94.1% Indigenous in 1570 to 55.6% Black by 1825. This is Brasil Crioulo being manufactured.
3. **The bandeirantes and the interior, 1600s.** Out of São Vicente/São Paulo came the *bandeiras* —
   expeditions of largely **mameluco** (Portuguese-Indigenous) men who spoke the **Língua Geral Paulista**
   rather than Portuguese, whose primary business was capturing Indigenous people to enslave, including by
   raiding Jesuit missions, and who incidentally **pushed Portuguese control hundreds of miles past the 1494
   Tordesillas line** — a fait accompli formalized by the Treaties of **Madrid (1750)** and **San Ildefonso
   (1777)**. Brazil is the shape it is because of them. This is Brasil Caipira's founding population, and it
   is worth noticing how closely it parallels Woodard's Greater Appalachia: an unplanned, mixed, mobile,
   violent frontier population beyond the reach of any colonial government, which ended up determining the
   country's borders.
4. **Gold, 1660s–1690s.** The bandeirantes found gold in **Minas Gerais**, triggering the Brazilian gold
   rush, pulling population inland, and shifting the colony's center of gravity south — which is why the
   capital moved from Salvador to Rio de Janeiro. ⚠️ The 1763 capital transfer date is from general
   knowledge, not verified here.
5. **Coffee and mass immigration, 1870–1930.** The single largest demographic event in Brazilian history
   after the slave trade. **At least 5.3 million immigrants arrived 1872–1972**: 31% Portuguese (1.8M), 30%
   Italian (1.7M), 13% Spanish (760,000), 5% Japanese, 4% German. Peak intensity was **1877–1903 (nearly 2
   million, about 71,000 a year)** and **1904–1930 (2,142,781, about 79,000 a year)**. They went two places:
   onto the **São Paulo coffee estates** — by 1897 São Paulo had twice as many Italians as native-born
   Brazilians, and by 1934 a 14.5% foreign population was producing over 40% of the state's coffee — and
   into **state-sponsored smallholder colonies in the south**: Germans at São Leopoldo, Rio Grande do Sul,
   from **1824**; Blumenau, Santa Catarina, **1850**; Joinville, **1851**. The São Paulo Immigrant's Hostel,
   opened **1886**, processed more than 2.5 million people. Settlers were given land, seed, livestock and
   tools. **This is the one place in Brazil where a smallholder-European founding layer was deliberately
   installed**, and it is why Santa Catarina is over 87% European-descended and the Sul has the country's
   highest HDI. In Engerman & Sokoloff terms, the Brazilian south is the only part of Brazil founded on the
   low-inequality endowment pattern — and it happened three hundred years late.
6. **The march west, 1930s–1960s.** Goiânia founded 1933, Brasília inaugurated 1960. The Centro-Oeste is a
   *twentieth-century* settlement project on the Cerrado, which is why Ribeiro has no Brasil for it: at the
   time he was working, there was not yet a distinct people there to name. ⚠️ Whether one has formed since
   — a soy-frontier society with its own character — is an open question this survey cannot answer.
7. **The Amazon road frontier, 1970s onward.** Transamazônica and BR-163 colonization, cattle and soy
   conversion, and the resulting collision with Indigenous territory. **First effective settlement in
   Amazonia is happening now, is contested, and is not settled.** For a post-collapse setting this is the
   most important sentence in Part 4: the Norte's institutional character is not yet fixed, which means a
   catastrophe would interrupt it mid-formation rather than destroy something established.

### 4.5 The author's question answered: one nation or several?

**Several. The evidence is not close.**

The five Brasis have different founding populations (enslaved Africans; Indigenous-Portuguese mamelucos;
backland cattle herders; Azorean and German and Italian smallholders), different founding institutions
(engenho; bandeira; open-range ranching; state-sponsored colony), different founding dates spanning three
centuries, and measurably different outcomes today. Brazil's 15.8% literacy in 1872 and 5.7% voting share in
1930 are national aggregates concealing enormous internal variance. A single "Brazil" in a post-collapse
framework would be the same error as a single "United States" — which is precisely the error Woodard wrote
his book to correct.

**The recommended split, for argument:**

| Proposed unit | Ribeiro basis | Founding institution | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Northeastern Coast | Crioulo | Sugar engenho, enslaved African majority | Oldest effective settlement in Brazil; Salvador and Recife |
| The Sertão | Sertanejo | Open-range cattle on semi-arid land | Cuts across three IBGE regions; drought is its organizing fact |
| The Southeastern Core | Caipira + the coffee/industrial overlay | Bandeira, then coffee, then industry | Half the national economy; the hardest to place, see below |
| The South | Sulino | Smallholder immigrant colony + gaúcho campanha | The only low-inequality founding in Brazil; possibly two units |
| Amazonia | Caboclo | Extraction, rivers, missions — none of them durable | Overlaps region 9 of Part 3; may not be Brazilian at all |

**The dissenting case for one Brazil, stated fairly**, because the author should have it: Brazil is the only
large territory in the Americas that has *never fragmented*. Spanish America broke into eighteen republics
and Brazil did not, despite being larger and more internally varied than all of them combined. It kept a
single monarchy through independence, a single language with no significant regional rival, and a national
culture — football, music, Carnival, telenovela, a shared media sphere — that is genuinely continental and
genuinely felt. Ribeiro himself, having drawn five Brasis, wrote a book called *The Brazilian People*,
singular, and argued that *ninguendade* is what they all have in common. A worldbuilder could reasonably
conclude that whatever holds Brazil together is stronger than what holds most states together, and that it
would survive a collapse.

**Three points against that, in the terms of this survey.** First, the thing holding Brazil together is a
*state* project — a monarchy, then a republic, then a broadcast media system — which is the category
Woodard's method specifically predicts will not survive the state. Second, the Sudeste's dominance is
economic and the economy is the first thing a collapse removes; strip out 49% of GDP and the Northeast's
54.6 million people have no reason to look south. Third, the Sertão and the Northeastern Coast are already
two societies inside one administrative region, which means the fracture line exists now, under load.

**A middle position worth considering:** Brazil fragments into three rather than five, on the fault lines
that are hardest to argue with — **Northeast** (coast plus sertão, held together by shared marginality),
**South-Southeast** (the industrial and immigrant core), and **Amazonia** (which was never really either).
Three is defensible, five is Ribeiro's, one is the state's own claim. The author picks.

## Part 5 — What this implies for post-collapse successor states

Applied inference from Parts 1–4, using Woodard's own logic. Clearly labeled as inference: nothing in this
section is a research finding, and no nations are derived. The purpose is to tell the author which of the
regions in Part 3 are *load-bearing* and which are scaffolding.

### 5.1 The test: pre-state versus state-made

Woodard's argument is that nations made the states, not the other way around — so when a state fails, the
nations underneath are still there. The usable test is therefore: **did this region's character exist before
the state that currently contains it, and was it produced by something other than that state?**

**Pre-state, and would reassert.** These have founding institutions predating the republics by two to four
centuries, and settlement geographies that are physically on the ground rather than legally maintained:

- **The Mesoamerican Core and the Maya Highlands.** The village-and-parish settlement pattern, the market-day
  network, the milpa agricultural system and the languages all predate the Spanish, never mind Mexico and
  Guatemala. Half of Spanish America was still Indigenous in 1935.
- **The Central Andes and the Altiplano.** Toledo's 840 reducciones are the physical towns. The mita
  boundary is measurably present in 2010 data. Neither Peru nor Bolivia created either.
- **The plantation zones** — the Islands, the Guianas, Brazil's Northeastern coast. An Afro-descended
  majority population occupying a coastal strip because that is where the sugar was is not a fact any modern
  state can undo by failing.
- **Brazil's five Brasis**, per Part 4.
- **Araucanía.** The Bío-Bío frontier held for 285 years against Spain. It is older than Chile.

**State-made, and would probably dissolve.** These are administrative artifacts whose coherence depends on
a functioning capital:

- **The Central American republic boundaries.** Five states carved out of one Audiencia and one federation
  between 1821 and 1841, along lines that correspond to no institutional or demographic discontinuity. Region
  3 is a corridor, not five countries.
- **Gran Colombia's successor borders** — Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Panama. Ecuador's sierra belongs
  institutionally with Peru's; Venezuela's llanos belong with Colombia's; the Caribbean coasts of both belong
  with each other. The borders are the residue of nineteenth-century elite politics.
- **The Argentina/Chile partition of Patagonia**, which cuts the same sheep-and-wind economy in half along a
  line negotiated by two distant capitals.
- **Brazil's Centro-Oeste as a unit**, and arguably the IBGE Nordeste, per Part 4.3.
- **Every national border across Amazonia.** Six states claim the basin; none of them administer most of it
  now, under favorable conditions.

**The general rule this yields, stated for reuse:** *an institution that shaped where people physically live
survives the state; an institution that only allocated authority does not.* Reducciones, plantations, mita
catchments and estancias all moved bodies onto specific ground. Republic borders moved paperwork.

### 5.2 Indigenous populations as successor nations in their own right

This is the section the author flagged, and in Latin America the answer is much stronger than in North
America — strong enough that treating these as components of other nations would be the single largest
design error available here.

Woodard's First Nation qualifies as a nation because its people never lost effective control of their
territory and retain functional survival knowledge specific to it. **Several Latin American Indigenous
populations meet that bar more comprehensively than First Nation does, on larger territory, with larger
populations, and in far more survivable climate.**

**The numbers, gathered in one place:**

| Population | Figure | Source year |
|---|---|---|
| Bolivia, self-identified Indigenous | **62% of the national population** | 2012 census |
| Guatemala, Indigenous | **6,471,670 — 43.43%**; Totonicapán 98.0%, Sololá 96.4%, Alta Verapaz 93.0%, El Quiché 89.2% | 2018 census |
| Peru, Indigenous | 5.9 million | 2017 |
| Chile, Indigenous | 2.4 million, of which Mapuche ~1.6M (8.8% of Chile) | 2017 / 2024 |
| Colombia | 1.9 million | 2018 |
| Brazil | 1,694,836 in 279 ethnic groups (0.83%) | 2022 census |
| Argentina | 1.3 million, of which Mapuche 145,783 | 2022 |
| Ecuador | 1.3 million | 2023 |
| South America total | approximately 18 million | — |

**And the territorial base, which is the part that matters most:**

- **Brazil: 805 demarcated or proposed Indigenous territories covering roughly 117.4 million hectares —
  about 13% of the national land area. 98% of that is in the Legal Amazon, where it covers approximately
  21% of the region.** The 1988 Constitution, Article 231, recognizes an *inalienable* right to traditionally
  occupied land; FUNAI (created 1967) runs a seven-stage demarcation process under Decree 1.775 of 1996.
  **FUNAI recorded 115 references to isolated peoples in the Legal Amazon as of 2017 — 28 confirmed, 86
  under investigation.**
- **Nicaragua: two constitutionally autonomous regions**, RACCN and RACCS, covering the entire Caribbean
  coast, majority Miskito, created in the 1980s.
- **Suriname: Maroon territorial rights defined by treaty since 1760**, still the operative legal basis for
  Ndyuka relations with the state.
- **Colombia: San Basilio de Palenque**, recognized 1772, still autonomous, still speaking Palenquero.
- **Jamaica: Accompong Town**, autonomy ratified at independence in 1962, plus Charles Town, Moore Town and
  Scott's Hall.

**Why these are successor *nations* rather than components of someone else's:**

1. **Contiguous, mapped, legally held territory.** This is the decisive difference from a diaspora or an
   urban minority. Brazil's 13% and Nicaragua's two regions are drawn on maps that the surrounding
   populations already recognize. In a collapse, the boundary does not have to be invented — it is the
   boundary that was already there, and the people on both sides know where it runs.
2. **Intact subsistence knowledge matched to specific terrain.** Altiplano freeze-drying, camelid herding
   and frost-resistant tuber varieties; Amazonian terra preta soil management and managed forest gardening;
   Andean vertical-archipelago cropping where it survived the reducciones; Maya highland milpa rotation.
   None of this depends on a supply chain. All of it is the accumulated answer to a specific environment
   that outsiders have consistently failed to solve. **In a post-collapse world these are not disadvantages
   to be overcome; they are the most valuable technology in the region.**
3. **An unbroken record of contesting the state.** The Mapuche have been at it since 1550 and are at it
   now — the Council of All Lands and the Coordinadora Arauco-Malleco since 1990, met with counter-terrorism
   prosecutions and 2022 proscriptions. The Miskito negotiated with Britain from 1638 and only lost
   independence in 1894. These are political actors with continuous institutional memory, not populations
   that would need to organize from scratch.
4. **Majority or near-majority demographics in their own core.** Bolivia at 62% and four Guatemalan
   departments above 89% are not enclaves. They are places where the Indigenous population *is* the
   population and the state is the visitor.

**Named candidates, offered for the author's consideration and not as conclusions:**

- **The Aymara Altiplano** — the strongest single case in the hemisphere. Majority population, its own
  pre-Inca state tradition, a terrain nobody else can farm, a measured institutional boundary (the mita) and
  the largest Indigenous-majority urban population anywhere in the Americas at El Alto.
- **The Maya Highlands** — 43% of Guatemala nationally, over 89% in four departments, 22 language nations,
  contiguous, and a state that has demonstrably never invested in them (20% literacy in 1945).
- **The Quechua sierra** — larger than the Altiplano in absolute numbers, but with a much more mestizo and
  Hispanized institutional layer on top; a harder and more interesting case.
- **Amazonian territorial blocs** — Yanomami, the Upper Xingu, Vale do Javari, and the Peruvian, Colombian
  and Venezuelan equivalents. 21% of the Legal Amazon is already theirs on paper. The 86 unconfirmed
  isolated-people reports are a reminder that some of this population has *already* opted out of the state
  system and would not notice its collapse.
- **The Mapuche of Araucanía** — the one people in the Americas who beat both the Inca and the Spanish and
  held a treaty frontier for three centuries. Also, per Part 3's corridor check, the last thing on the road
  to the Falklands.
- **The Miskito Coast** — already constitutionally autonomous, already majority Miskito, already
  historically a British client rather than a Spanish subject.
- **Maroon polities** — Suriname's Ndyuka and Saramaka, Jamaica's Accompong, Colombia's Palenque. Small, but
  these are the only polities in the Americas founded by people who seized their own freedom and then
  negotiated treaty recognition of territory from the empire they escaped. As a design object that is
  extraordinary and it is almost entirely unused in fiction.

### 5.3 The two terrain-defined cases, and why they behave oppositely

Checklist item 6 calls for at least one Far West case — a region whose character is set by environmental
hostility rather than by any settler group's values. Latin America has two, and they are instructive
because they produce opposite outcomes from the same premise.

**The Altiplano: terrain that was already solved.** Extreme altitude, cold, aridity, 3,750 m average. No
outside population has ever devised a better answer to it than the one the Aymara and Quechua already had.
The colonial system did not settle the Altiplano; it drafted labor out of it and left. In a collapse, the
Altiplano does not become empty or contested — it becomes *more* autonomous, because the only thing the
outside world was ever supplying was demand for silver and tin.

**Amazonia: terrain that defeats everyone, including its would-be settlers.** The basin destroyed the
captaincy system, the missions, the rubber boom and, on current evidence, the road-colonization frontier.
The crucial and counterintuitive point from Part 2.5 is that **it did not defeat its original inhabitants**
— five to eight million people lived there using engineered soils and managed forests — and that the
apparent emptiness is a post-1492 artifact. So a collapse in Amazonia does not produce a wilderness. It
produces the removal of the only force that has ever suppressed the population that knows how to live there.

**Compare Woodard's Far West**, which is defined by externally-directed corporate and federal colonization of
land too arid to settle normally, producing a culture of dependency-plus-resentment. Neither Latin American
case works that way. **The nearest true Far West analogue in this survey is not Amazonia or the Altiplano at
all — it is Patagonia**, low-density sheep country settled late by state-directed military campaign and
foreign capital, and the northern Chilean nitrate and copper desert, which is a textbook externally
capitalized extraction zone. Those two are the Far West pattern proper.

### 5.4 Alliance structure — explicitly the author's to design

Checklist item 7 calls for a separate alliance pass. This survey will not do it, because it would require
inventing nations first and that is the author's decision. What the research does supply is the raw
material for the four required roles:

- **A superpower pair.** The structural candidates are *the Mesoamerican Core* and *whatever Brazil becomes*
  — the two largest populations, the two deepest institutional histories, and the two that have historically
  never had to care what anyone else on the continent thought. Nothing in the research requires them to be
  opposed; that is a design choice.
- **A durable bilateral alliance.** The Altiplano and the Quechua sierra share a founding substrate, an
  economy and an adversary, which is the Yankeedom/Left Coast pattern. Alternatively the Platine plain and
  Central Chile, on shared Euro-American founding and mutual isolation from everyone north of them.
- **A coalition held together by manufactured grievance.** The historical template is available and ugly:
  Ribeiro's *barroco* racism and Wagley's "social race" work both describe how Latin American elites built
  cross-class coalitions on racial anxiety without formal segregation — a different mechanism from the Deep
  South's, and arguably a more durable one because it is deniable.
- **A swing nation.** The Circum-Caribbean Littoral is the natural candidate: maritime, commercially
  oriented, historically a client of whichever outside power was paying, aligned with nobody's interior.
  Structurally this is Woodard's New Netherland.

### 5.5 Three findings worth carrying into the next phase

1. **The Indigenous question is not a subsection here; it is the frame.** In North America, ten of Woodard's
   eleven nations are settler cultures and First Nation is the exception. In Latin America the proportion
   inverts: four of the thirteen regions in Part 3 are Indigenous-substrate, two more are terrain-defined
   around surviving Indigenous populations, and Spanish America was *half Indigenous as recently as 1935*.
   A design that treats Indigenous survivors as a special case rather than as the default will be wrong.
2. **Density at contact is the master variable, and it runs forward as well as backward.** Dense organized
   populations attract extraction; sparse ones attract settlement; extraction produces narrow institutions
   that persist. This rule can be applied to any post-catastrophe repopulation, not just to 1492 — which
   makes it the single most portable thing in this survey.
3. **The blank slate is unusually clean, and unusually well-supplied.** There is no prior canon for Latin
   America in the setting, and there is more rigorous, quantitative, boundary-drawn scholarship available
   for this continent's institutional history than for any other on earth, because economists have spent
   thirty years using it as their natural experiment. The author is not short of material. The author is
   short of a map — and per Part 1.7, so is everyone else.
