# Mainland Southeast Asia — Mandalas, the Massif and the Tai Continuum

2026-09-23. **Survey only. No nations named, no borders drawn.** The analytical layer for
**Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Thailand and Myanmar**, plus the highlands above them.

**Scope, per the author:** mainland first. **Maritime Southeast Asia — Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia,
Brunei, Singapore, Timor-Leste — is a separate later pass**, on the grounds that it is a genuinely different
object: Austronesian rather than Tai/Mon-Khmer/Tibeto-Burman, largely Islamic and Christian rather than
Theravada, and archipelagic rather than riverine. Where it abuts, it is named and nothing more.

**Companion file:** the material-base survey, still to be written.

⚠️ **This is a first pass built from a limited source set.** The web-search budget for the session was
exhausted before it began, so it rests on thirteen directly-fetched references. Every figure below is
sourced, but the survey is **thinner than the Latin America and East Asia passes** and should be treated as a
frame to argue with rather than a finished analysis. Gaps are flagged rather than filled.

---

## Headline findings

**1. The project's core/domain/sphere vocabulary is the mandala, and this is where it is most literal.**
Southeast Asian polities were **not bordered territories.** Authority radiated from a center through personal
allegiance; extent was a function of who acknowledged whom. Tributaries kept internal autonomy, and —
decisively — **a border state could acknowledge several overlords at once**, which is how buffer zones formed.

**That makes three independent arrivals at the same three-tier structure**: Woodard for North America,
**Lattimore for Inner Asia (1940)**, and **O.W. Wolters for Southeast Asia**. Tambiah called it a *galactic
polity*; Victor Lieberman a *solar polity*. This project has treated independent convergence as its evidence
standard all along, and here the convergence is strongest — and the fit is not metaphorical. The
`core_territory` / `domain_territory` / `sphere_territory` columns already in the map CSVs **are** a mandala.

**2. First effective settlement fails here too — but for a new reason.** In East Asia it failed because there
was no founding-settler moment. **Here it fails because the defining cultural layer was chosen.**
Indianization was **voluntary adoption, not conquest**: Southeast Asian principalities took Sanskrit and Pali,
Hindu and Buddhist frameworks, divine kingship, temple architecture, law and the *Ramayana* through centuries
of trade, with no Indian conquest behind it. One scholarly framing has it as "a single cultural process in
which Southeast Asia was the matrix." **Woodard's doctrine describes institutions arriving with a population.
Here the population stayed put and sent for the institutions.**

**3. The Tai continuum is the region's spine, and it runs straight out of the East Asia survey.**
**~93 million Tai people** across Thailand, Laos, Myanmar's Shan State, southern China, Vietnam and northeast
India. Their homeland is placed in **the middle Yangtze basin**, and the southwestward migration ran **out of
Guangxi between the 8th and 10th centuries.** So the **Zhuang** — the 19.6-million question at the center of
the Sinian Federation's southern border — are the **origin population of the Thai, the Lao and the Shan.**
**The two surveys meet here, and the seam is a real one rather than an artifact of where the scope lines fell.**

**4. Isan is the sharpest single finding, and it is a nation cut by a border.** Northeastern Thailand holds
**~22 million people who are ethnically Lao**, on the Khorat Plateau across 20 provinces. **Laos itself holds
just over 7.5 million** — so **roughly three times more Lao live in Thailand than in Laos**, and both figures
are now sourced. Isan speakers number **15–23 million**, the large majority inside Thailand.
So **the Lao nation's demographic center of gravity sits inside the Thai state**, and has been subject to a
century of *Thaification*.

**And the grievance is measurable.** Isan holds **about 34% of Thailand's population** and contributes **10%
of national GDP**, while receiving **5.8% of total expenditure against Bangkok's 72.2%.** That is a
documented, current, quantified internal fault line — the mainland's equivalent of Manchuria's in East Asia.

**5. Myanmar's ethnic geography is already drawable, which nothing in East Asia was.** Seven **States** named
for and associated with ethnic groups, alongside seven majority-Bamar **Regions** — and all fourteen are
first-order subdivisions. In China, Kham and the Tarim could not be drawn without splitting a province. **Here
the lines already exist.** This is the one place in the atlas where the whole-subdivision rule costs nothing.

**6. Vietnam is the outlier, and it belongs to the other survey's world.** 85.32% Kinh, **a thousand years of
direct Chinese rule (111 BC – 939 AD)**, Confucian statecraft, **Mahayana** rather than Theravada Buddhism,
and Chinese characters (*chữ Hán*, *chữ Nôm*) rather than an Indic script. **Vietnam is the fourth Sinosphere
member** — the East Asia survey named it as out of scope and flagged that its exclusion was a scope decision,
not a cultural judgment. It is Sinic, sitting in an Indic neighborhood.

## Part 1 — What replaces first effective settlement

### 1.1 The mandala, stated properly

| | Bordered state | **Mandala** |
|---|---|---|
| **Extent defined by** | territory | **who acknowledges the center** |
| **Boundaries** | fixed lines | **none — influence fades outward** |
| **Overlap** | impossible | **normal, and deliberate** |
| **Subordinates** | administered | **autonomous, tributary** |
| **Loyalty** | to the state | **to a person** |

Tribute flowed inward (*bunga mas*), along with troops when called; protection and reciprocal gifts flowed
back out. **Notable mandalas, roughly 5th–15th centuries:** the Khmer Empire, Srivijaya, Majapahit, Ayutthaya,
Champa, Lan Xang, Lanna.

**The system ended by imposition.** Nineteenth-century colonization introduced fixed territorial sovereignty
and fragmented the patchwork across French Indochina, the Dutch East Indies and British Malaya. **The modern
borders of mainland Southeast Asia are a European artifact laid over a system that had no borders at all** —
which is worth knowing before redrawing them for a post-collapse world.

### 1.2 What this means for the map

**Per the author's decision, the mandala is carried natively rather than flattened.** Cores get hard borders;
**spheres overlap, and the overlaps are correct rather than errors.** The method already permits this and the
CSV schema already has the columns — this is simply the region where that column stops being decoration.

**The practical consequence:** on a mainland Southeast Asia map, expect **fewer clean borders and more
graduated edges** than on the North America or East Asia maps. A reader should be able to see that this
region's polities work differently, because they did.

### 1.3 Indianization — adoption, not conquest

Beginning around **500 BCE** with trade expansion and largely complete **by the early centuries CE**, the
principalities took from India: Sanskrit and Pali alongside local languages; Hindu and Buddhist frameworks;
**divine kingship**; temple architecture; administrative and legal systems; and the *Ramayana* and
*Mahabharata*.

**Indianized polities:** Funan, Chenla, Champa, Dvaravati, Pagan, Angkor on the mainland — Srivijaya,
Majapahit, Tarumanagara, Singhasari and Kutai in the maritime world.

**Contrast with the Sinic mode.** Chinese influence travelled through characters and bureaucracy, and in
Vietnam's case through a millennium of direct rule. Indian influence travelled through religion and
philosophy, with no conquest. **Two neighboring civilizational projects, one imposed and one invited** — and
the line between them runs down the Annamite Range.

## Part 2 — Zomia, the massif above the states

### 2.1 What it is

**Highlands beyond the reach of lowland states.** The term was coined by **Willem van Schendel** in 2002, from
*Zomi* — highlander, in Tibeto-Burman languages of the India–Bangladesh–Myanmar borderlands. **James C.
Scott** popularized it in *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009).

**The Southeast Asian Massif** proper: **highlands above roughly 300 metres across ten countries, with an
indigenous population of about 100 million.** Core areas are northern Indochina (northern Vietnam and **all of
Laos**), the Thai highlands, the **Shan Hills**, and the mountains of southwest China. Some scholars extend it
west to Tibet, northeast India, Pakistan and Afghanistan.

### 2.2 Scott's argument, and the case against it

**The thesis:** hill peoples are *"conscious refugees from state rule"* who fled state-making over two
millennia — slavery, conscription, corvée. Traits read as primitive were **state-avoidance adaptations**:
absence of writing, mobility, shifting cultivation, prophetic religious movements.

**⚠️ It is contested, and the criticism is substantive.**

- **Victor Lieberman** — insufficient Burmese-language sourcing; **maritime commerce rather than manpower**
  determined military outcomes; and **Borneo highlanders show the same cultural profile without any predatory
  lowland state to flee from.** That last is the strongest single objection.
- **Tom Brass** — the uplands were not a voluntary refuge. People were **forced off valley land** and remain
  within state reach.
- **Edward Stringham** supports Scott, on how stateless societies repelled states through location, production
  and culture.

**For this project the dispute matters less than it looks.** Whether the massif's people fled uphill or were
pushed, **the massif is ungoverned ground with its own peoples, and it is where the collapse of lowland states
would matter least.** That is a durable structural fact either way, and the safest way to use Zomia here is
descriptively.

### 2.3 The seam with East Asia

**The Chinese wing of Zomia was already flagged in the East Asia survey** — Yunnan and Guizhou, the ~25-people
shatter zone, and the decision that placed them inside the Sinian Federation anyway on demographic grounds.
**The massif does not stop at that border**, and the same peoples continue south. ⚠️ **The two surveys should
be read together at this seam**, and a later pass should check that nothing is said in one that the other
contradicts.

## Part 3 — The countries, as they stand

**This is a description of present ground, not a proposal.** Regional division comes after the material survey.

### 3.1 Myanmar — the one place the lines already exist

**~55.77 million (2022).** Ethnic composition (2019): **Bamar 68.78%**, Karen 6.69%, Rakhine 4.61%,
Shan 4.51%, Mon 2.19%, Chin 2.09%, Kachin 1.50%, Karenni 0.39%.

**Seven ethnic States:**

| State | People | Population |
|---|---|---|
| **Shan** | Shan (Tai) | **6,412,501** |
| **Rakhine** | Rakhine | 2,526,792 |
| **Kachin** | Kachin | 2,153,483 |
| **Mon** | Mon | 2,054,393 |
| **Kayin** | Karen | 1,502,774 |
| **Chin** | Chin | 373,023 |
| **Kayah** | Karenni | 296,901 |

**Seven majority-Bamar Regions:** Yangon (7,369,989), Mandalay (6,323,554), Sagaing (5,977,292), Ayeyarwady
(5,546,415), Bago (4,453,687), Magway (4,077,653), Tanintharyi (1,399,965). Plus **Nay Pyi Taw Union
Territory** (1,129,322).

**And a further layer worth noting:** a **Wa Self-Administered Division** inside Shan State, plus five
Self-Administered Zones — **Danu, Kokang, Naga, Pa Laung, Pa-O.** ⚠️ The Wa in particular function as a de
facto state; that was not verified here and should be.

**Myanmar is riven by one of the world's longest-running civil wars**, with a 2021 coup, ongoing conflict,
**over 1.3 million refugees and 3.5 million internally displaced.** ⚠️ Which groups hold what territory was
not established and is the single most important gap in this file.

### 3.2 Thailand — and the Isan problem

The Isan figures above are the substance. **Two further observations:**

- **Thailand was never colonized**, alone in the region. ⚠️ Stated from general knowledge, not verified here —
  but if it holds, it means Thailand's borders are *negotiated* rather than *imposed*, unlike every neighbor's,
  and that is a real difference in how they would survive a collapse.
- **Isan's identity is suppressed rather than absent.** People "call themselves Thai Isan or simply Lao," with
  the Lao label carrying stigma in Thai society; the language is a variety of Lao now written in Thai script
  rather than the historical **Tai Noi**. **A century of Thaification did not erase the identity — it renamed
  it**, which is precisely Woodard's claim about how nations persist.

### 3.3 Vietnam — Sinic in an Indic neighborhood

**96.2 million (2019 census), 85.32% Kinh**, 14.68% minorities. ⚠️ Individual minority figures were not
retrieved. ⚠️ **Vietnam reorganized its provinces in 2025** and the current first-order count is uncertain —
**confirm before drawing anything.**

See headline 6. The cultural placement is the load-bearing fact: **Vietnam belongs to the East Asia survey's
world, not this one**, and any map treating mainland Southeast Asia as a unit is making a claim that the
sources do not support.

### 3.4 Laos — the massif with a flag on it

**7.5+ million people. 17 provinces plus Vientiane Prefecture. The only landlocked country in Southeast Asia**,
bordered by all five of its neighbors: Myanmar, China, Vietnam, Cambodia and Thailand.

**The ethnic composition is the finding: Lao 53.2%.** Then **Khmu 11%**, **Hmong 9.2%**, Phu Thai 3.4%,
Tai 3.1%, and a long tail (2015 census).

**So Laos is barely a Lao-majority state, and nearly half of it is highland peoples.** Set beside the Isan
figure, this produces something genuinely strange and worth building on:

> **The Lao nation's demographic center sits in Thailand, and the Lao state is itself only half Lao.**
> There are roughly three times more ethnic Lao in Isan than in Laos, and inside Laos the Lao are 53.2%.

**Laos is therefore the one country in this survey that *is* Zomia rather than merely containing it.** Its
ethnic composition is the massif's composition. Every other mainland state is a lowland core with highlands at
its edges; Laos is highlands with a river down one side.

**Lan Xang**, 13th–18th centuries, was the mandala — a hub for overland trade. It **split into three kingdoms
in 1707** and fell to French rule in 1893. ⚠️ The three-way split is worth following up: it suggests Laos has
a precedent for fragmenting along internal lines, which no other mainland state in this survey does.

**✅ CONFIRMED — Laos classifies its own peoples by elevation.** The traditional geographical scheme is
**Lao Loum (lowland), Lao Theung (midland), Lao Sung (highland)**, and it is in use.

**This is remarkable for the project: Zomia's own organizing axis, adopted as administrative policy by the
state sitting inside it.** Every other country in this atlas classifies its peoples by ethnicity, language or
religion. Laos classifies them by **altitude** — which is Scott's entire argument, rendered as a government
filing category.

**The underlying diversity is enormous for 7.5 million people: 49 main ethnicities, over 160 ethnic groups,
73 languages**, across five language families — **Tai-Kadai** (Lao ~3 million, Tai Lue ~300,000),
**Austro-Asiatic** (Khmu 389,694), **Hmong-Mien** (Hmong Njua 245,600, Hmong Daw 200,000), **Tibeto-Burman**
(Akha ~58,000) and a handful of **Austronesian** Cham.

⚠️ **No population share was retrieved for the three elevation tiers**, and that is the figure worth having —
it would say directly how much of Laos is massif.

⚠️ **Lao authorities do not use the term "indigenous peoples."** Noted because it is a deliberate choice with
consequences, and because it differs from how Latin America's frameworks treat the same question.

### 3.5 Cambodia — the homogeneous one

**~17 million people, and 95.4% Khmer.** Cham 2.4%, Chinese 1.5%, other 0.8%.

**That makes Cambodia the most ethnically homogeneous state on the mainland by a wide margin** — against
Myanmar's 68.78% Bamar, Vietnam's 85.32% Kinh, Thailand's large Isan minority and Laos's 53.2%. **In a survey
whose whole subject is peoples cut across borders, Cambodia is the one country that is simply itself.**

**⚠️ And that homogeneity is partly an atrocity's residue**, which should not be stated without the cause.
The **Khmer Rouge killed an estimated 1–3 million people between 1975 and 1979 — commonly cited as 2 million,
roughly a quarter of the population — and targeted minorities disproportionately.** About **half the Cham
Muslim population** was exterminated; the Vietnamese population fell from 250,000–300,000 in 1969 to roughly
**56,000 by 1984.** Cambodia is homogeneous in part because its minorities were killed.

**The Khmer Empire** was founded in **802 AD** when Jayavarman II unified the Khmer princes as *Kambuja*. It
became the largest Southeast Asian empire of the 12th century, and **Angkor was the largest pre-industrial
city in the world — an urban sprawl of 2,980 km², possibly a million inhabitants.**

**And here is the finding this project should care about most:**

> **Angkor was abandoned around 1431 due to ecological failure and infrastructure collapse.**

**The greatest civilization in Southeast Asian history died of exactly what this setting is about**, and it
died that way six hundred years before the war of 2083. **Mainland Southeast Asia has a collapse in its own
past, at its own center, and remembers it.** No other region in this atlas has that.

### 3.6 How Angkor actually died — the anatomy, because it is the useful part

**Angkor was a "hydraulic city."** An infrastructure system of barays (reservoirs) and canals for storing and
dispersing water, connecting an urban sprawl of **at least 1,000 km²** and supporting **750,000 to 1,000,000
people.** Everything about it depended on that network being maintained.

**The sequence, as the evidence has it:**

1. **Climate shock, and it is measured.** **Dendrochronology is decisive here** — tree-ring chronologies
   establish **severe drought across mainland Southeast Asia in the early 15th century**, and **Angkor's
   canals and reservoirs ran dry.** Earlier work shows that across the 14th–15th centuries the monsoon first
   **weakened and was then followed by extreme flooding.** Not one shock but whiplash, which is worse.
2. **The institutions that justified maintenance lost their legitimacy.** **The rise of Theravada Buddhism may
   have eroded the royal personality cult** that sustained monument construction — and with it, the
   maintenance of the waterways that irrigation depended on.
3. **Maintenance stopped**, and weakened royal authority could not restart it.
4. **An outside blow finished it.** Ayutthaya sacked the city, but **the conquest exploited existing
   vulnerabilities rather than causing the collapse.**

**The scholarly consensus, stated plainly: environmental stress revealed structural vulnerabilities that
military conquest then exploited.**

**Why this is worth more to the project than a date.** Step 2 is the part that does not appear in any other
collapse in this atlas. **The waterworks did not fail technically. They failed because the belief system that
made maintaining them worth doing was replaced.** A civilization can hold all the engineering knowledge it
needs and still stop using it, because the reason went away first.

⚠️ *"May have eroded"* is the source's own hedge and should be kept. The religious mechanism is a proposal,
not a settled finding; the drought evidence is much harder.

### 3.7 The Khmer Krom — the pattern completes

**~1.32 million ethnic Khmer live in Vietnam** (2019 census), concentrated in **Sóc Trăng (362,029), Trà Vinh
(318,231) and Kiên Giang (211,282)** — the Mekong Delta. A further ~1.2 million are counted in Cambodia,
with diaspora in the US, France and Australia. They are one of Vietnam's 54 recognized minorities and have
been UNPO members since 2001.

**The delta was Khmer for over 800 years, and changed hands by settlement rather than conquest.** A weakened
Khmer state left it poorly administered; Vietnamese refugees migrated in; **in 1623 King Chey Chettha II
approved Vietnamese settlement**; by **1698** Vietnamese administrators had reorganized the territory on
Vietnamese lines; and **by 1757 the major centers — Sa Đéc, Châu Đốc — were absorbed.**

**So every major people on the mainland is cut by a border:**

| People | Cut by | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Lao** | the Thai border | ~22M in Isan against 7.5M in Laos |
| **Tai** | five borders at once | ~93M from Guangxi to Assam |
| **Khmer** | the Vietnamese border | ~1.32M in the Mekong Delta |
| **Zhuang / Nùng** | the Sino-Vietnamese border | 15M+ under two names |

**This is not incidental — it is what the region is.** The mandala system had no borders; the European
successor states drew them across peoples who had never been divided by anything.

**And it produces a symmetry worth noticing:** **Vietnam's rice bowl sits on land that was Khmer for eight
centuries, and depends for half its dry-season water on a Cambodian lake.**

## Part 4 — Deliberately excluded, and named

- **Maritime Southeast Asia** — Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore, Timor-Leste. A
  separate pass, by decision. **Indonesia is to get its own internal division**, the way Ribeiro's five Brasis
  and the ten Sinitic nations did.
- **Northeast India's Tai peoples** — Khamti, Ahom, Phake, Aiton, Turung. Out of scope, but they mark the
  western end of the Tai continuum, and the massif runs on past them.
- **Southern China's Tai peoples** — Zhuang, Bouyei, Dai. **In the East Asia survey, inside the Sinian
  Federation.** Named here because the continuum does not respect the scope line.
- **The maritime commerce argument.** Lieberman's claim that sea trade rather than manpower drove military
  outcomes bears directly on how a post-collapse mainland would work, and it is not pursued here.
