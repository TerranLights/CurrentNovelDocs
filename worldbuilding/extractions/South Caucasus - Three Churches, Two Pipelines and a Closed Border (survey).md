# South Caucasus — Three Churches, Two Pipelines and a Closed Border (survey)

**Stage 1 frameworks survey. Started 2026-09-28, first research pass only — not a finished analysis.**
Covers **Georgia, Azerbaijan and Armenia** — added to this project's **West Asia** region on the author's
explicit instruction ("add those to West Asia and do the necessary research for them"), surfaced by the
author's own work developing the Kurdish Nation region on the Middle East/Turkey/Afghanistan map: Turkey's
Kurdish-flagged eastern provinces (Ağrı, Iğdır, Kars) physically border Armenia and Georgia, and Armenia holds
a real Yazidi/Kurdish population this project's data didn't previously carry. Companion to
`Central Asia - Steppe, Oasis and the Fergana Tangle (survey).md` and
`Middle East - Sykes-Picot, Sect and the Kurdish Question (survey).md`, both already built.

**Why this region sat unmapped until now**: Azerbaijan was named once in the Central Asia survey ("Azerbaijan
is Caucasus/Europe side, not Central Asia") and once in the Middle East survey (as a distant Pan-Turkic
relative) — both times explicitly out of scope. Georgia and Armenia were never mentioned at all. The three
sit in the real seam between Central Asia, the Middle East package, Russia (already built, but its own survey
never covered the Caucasus), and Europe (not started).

## Headline findings

1. **First effective settlement fails here for a fifth, distinct reason — three old, self-contained
   civilizations that predate every neighboring empire that has since claimed them, not an imported or
   engineered identity at all.** Unlike Central Asia's Soviet-engineered borders or the Middle East's
   colonial-mandate lines, Georgia and Armenia's national identities are pre-Islamic, pre-Ottoman, and
   pre-Russian: **Armenia became the first state in the world to adopt Christianity as its official religion**
   (301 CE, King Tiridates III converted by St. Gregory the Illuminator), and **the Georgian Orthodox Church's
   own roots trace to the 4th century** (St. Nino's mission to Kartli/Iberia). Both are among the handful of
   oldest continuously-identified Christian nations on Earth, sitting inside a region otherwise organized
   entirely around Islamic sectarian geography (the Middle East survey's own §4) or the Soviet ethnographic
   engineering (the Central Asia survey's own §1). **Azerbaijan is the region's opposite case**: a modern,
   Turkic, Shia-Muslim-majority nation-state whose own founding identity is explicitly bound to Turkey's
   ("one nation, two states," see finding 5) rather than to an ancient, self-contained civilizational core.
   See §1.
2. **Nagorno-Karabakh/Artsakh is this region's most recently and most violently resolved ethnic-territorial
   question anywhere in this project — settled by force in six weeks, not by negotiation.** Azerbaijan's
   September 2023 offensive dissolved the self-declared Republic of Artsakh outright: over 100,000 ethnic
   Armenians (99% of the territory's population) fled within days, and the territory is now fully under
   Azerbaijani control, with resettlement underway. **This is the same shape of fact as Syria's AANES
   dissolution in the Middle East survey (§3.4 there) — a very recent, real, dated resolution, not a live open
   question** — but arrived at through the near-total displacement of an entire ethnic population rather than
   negotiated integration. A U.S.-brokered peace framework (August 2025) and a planned transit corridor
   (the "Zangezur Corridor"/TRIPP, finding 4) are moving forward, but full ratification depends on a 2027
   Armenian constitutional referendum removing territorial claims — **not yet finalized as of this survey's
   research date.** See §2.
3. **Abkhazia and South Ossetia are the region's other, genuinely unresolved ethnic-territorial cases — the
   Kirkuk/Israel-Palestine-tier open question here, not the Kurdish-Nation tier.** Both have functioned as de
   facto independent, Russian-garrisoned, Russian-recognized states since the 1990s/2008 war; almost the
   entire rest of the world still regards both as Georgian territory. Unlike Nagorno-Karabakh, **neither has
   been resolved, by force or negotiation, in either direction** — this survey flags both, per this project's
   "research proposes, the author disposes" rule, exactly the way Kirkuk and Israel/Palestine were flagged
   rather than resolved in the Middle East survey. See §2.
4. **The region's organizing physical fact is a land bridge, not a river or a strait — the same structural
   role the Middle Corridor plays for Central Asia (companion survey §4), except already built and operating
   at far greater scale.** The **Baku-Tbilisi-Ceyhan (BTC) oil pipeline** and **Baku-Tbilisi-Kars (BTK)
   railway** physically route landlocked, oil/gas-rich Azerbaijan's exports through Georgia to Turkey and
   world markets, **explicitly bypassing both Russia and Armenia** — Georgia's entire strategic value to this
   region is that it is the only viable corridor. A newer piece, the **Zangezur Corridor (also called TRIPP,
   "Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity")**, would connect Azerbaijan's mainland to its own
   exclave, **Nakhchivan**, through a 43 km strip of Armenia's Syunik province — construction began January
   2026, and it is explicitly contentious among Armenia, Iran, Turkey and Russia alike. **A single choke
   country (Georgia) already carries the load Central Asia's Middle Corridor is still trying to build.** See
   §3.
5. **Azerbaijan-Turkey is a real, official, explicitly-named "one nation, two states" relationship — the
   direct, concrete anchor this project's existing Pan-Turkic sphere (Middle East package, S3) needs to
   actually reach Azerbaijan rather than stay label-only.** The phrase originates with Heydar Aliyev in the
   1990s and was formalized in the **2021 Shusha Declaration** (Presidents Erdoğan and Aliyev) — shared
   Turkic language and ethnicity, deepening military cooperation (joint exercises, Azerbaijani officers
   training in Turkish military colleges), and Turkey as the principal conduit for Azerbaijani oil/gas exports
   to world markets. See §4 and §7.
6. **Armenia's Yazidi population is the largest, by share, anywhere in the world outside Iraq — a real, direct
   tie to the Kurdish Nation region already drawn on the Middle East map.** Armenia's 2022 census recorded
   31,079 Yazidis (community leaders say the true figure may exceed 50,000) against only 1,663 who self-identify
   as ethnic Kurds specifically — **nearly all self-identified Kurds in Armenia are, in practice, Yazidis who
   also carry a Kurdish identity; almost no Muslim Kurds live in Armenia today.** This is a genuinely different
   population than the Sunni-Kurdish population the Middle East survey's Kurdish Nation region is built around
   (Yazidism is its own distinct religion, not a sect of Islam) — flagged as a real design question for
   whether/how the existing Kurdish Nation sphere should acknowledge Armenia at all. See §5.
7. **The Turkey-Armenia border has been closed since 1993 — one of very few completely closed land borders
   anywhere in the world — and normalization is real but incomplete as of this survey.** Turkey shut the
   border in solidarity with Azerbaijan during the First Nagorno-Karabakh War; it has stayed closed for over
   three decades even as diplomatic normalization has genuinely accelerated in 2025-26 (scheduled
   Istanbul-Yerevan flights resumed March 2026, a joint bridge-restoration agreement signed, land-border
   opening to third-country nationals agreed "in principle" but not yet implemented). **This directly shapes
   how Armenia physically connects, or fails to connect, to the rest of West Asia on this project's own map**
   — Armenia's only fully open land borders are with Georgia and Iran; its borders with both Turkey and
   Azerbaijan are closed or contested. See §6.

## Section 1 — Method: three old civilizations, not an engineered or imported identity

Per this project's established practice, Woodard's Doctrine of First Effective Settlement requires an arriving
population founding a self-perpetuating society in open territory. The South Caucasus fails this test for a
reason distinct from every region this project has surveyed so far:

- **East Asia and Russia** failed it because an old imperial core absorbed already-populated neighbors.
- **Mainland Southeast Asia** failed it because a cultural layer (Indianization) was *chosen* by a population
  that stayed put.
- **Central Asia** failed it because the borders themselves were a deliberate outside-power artifact (Soviet
  national delimitation).
- **The Middle East** failed it because colonial powers (Sykes-Picot/the Mandate system) drew lines through a
  more continuous space for their own administrative convenience.
- **Georgia and Armenia fail it for a fifth, distinct reason: both are old, self-contained, pre-Islamic
  civilizational cores that predate every empire that has since claimed them** — Persian, Roman/Byzantine,
  Arab, Mongol, Ottoman, Safavid, Russian/Soviet, in roughly that order for both countries, depending on the
  era. Neither nation's identity was founded by, chosen from, or engineered by any of those outside powers;
  both survived all of them. **Azerbaijan is the exception inside this exception**: a modern nation-state
  whose own founding identity (per §4/§7 below) is explicitly bound to Turkey rather than to an ancient,
  self-contained core of its own the way Georgia's and Armenia's are.

**Tier 1 grouping this survey uses, following the same table format Central Asia and the Middle East
established:**

| Tier 1 | Example Tier 2 identities | Where | Historical mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kartvelian/Georgian Orthodox | Georgian, Mingrelian, Svan, Laz (outside scope — Turkey) | Georgia | Ancient, self-contained civilizational core; earliest attested Christianization 4th century CE |
| Armenian/Apostolic | Armenian | Armenia | Ancient, self-contained civilizational core; first state to adopt Christianity officially, 301 CE |
| Turkic/Shia-Muslim | Azerbaijani | Azerbaijan | Modern nation-state, explicitly twinned with Turkey's own founding-culture identity ("one nation, two states") |

**⚠️ This three-way split is real and old for Georgia and Armenia specifically, but Azerbaijan's own case is
qualitatively different and should not be flattened into the same "ancient civilization" framing as its two
neighbors.** Azerbaijan's Shia-Muslim majority also gives it a real, if unexplored-this-pass, tie to the
Middle East survey's own Sunni-Shia geography (§4 there) — Iran's own Shia-majority character sits directly
across Azerbaijan's southern border, and Iran's own Azeri-minority provinces (Ardabil, East Azerbaijan, West
Azerbaijan — already flagged in the Middle East package's `subdivisions.csv`) are ethnically the same people
as Azerbaijan's own titular nationality. **Not resolved here** — flagged as a second cross-reference point
alongside the Pan-Turkic sphere, see §7.

## Section 2 — Two territorial questions, resolved very differently

### 2.1 Nagorno-Karabakh/Artsakh — resolved by force, 2023

A self-declared, ethnically Armenian-majority republic inside internationally-recognized Azerbaijani
territory fought two wars over its status (1988-94, then 2020's Second Nagorno-Karabakh War) before
**Azerbaijan's September 2023 offensive ended the question outright**: the de facto Artsakh leadership
surrendered within 24 hours, and in the days that followed **over 100,617 people — effectively the entire
ethnic Armenian population of the territory — fled to Armenia proper.** Azerbaijan now holds full control and
has begun resettling the territory.

**⚠️ Not yet fully closed diplomatically, even though the territorial question is closed militarily.** An
August 2025 U.S.-brokered summit produced a joint peace declaration and an initialed peace agreement,
including the planned Zangezur Corridor/TRIPP transit route (§3). **Full ratification requires Armenia to
amend its own constitution to remove territorial claims to the former Artsakh** — a process tied to a
scheduled **2027 referendum**, i.e. still pending as of this survey's research date. **Confidence: high** on
the 2023 displacement and current control facts (CFR, RFE/RL, House of Commons Library, Minority Rights Group
all converge); **medium** on how durable/final the 2025-26 peace process proves, since ratification remains
incomplete.

**How this compares to this project's other recently-resolved cases**: structurally closest to Syria's AANES
dissolution (Middle East survey §3.4) — both are very recent (within 1-3 years), both were flagged there as
"real, dated, not a live open question" — but Karabakh's resolution involved near-total forced displacement of
an ethnic population rather than negotiated integration into a surviving state structure. **Treat as settled
territorial fact (Azerbaijani control), with the diplomatic/legal closure still pending.**

### 2.2 Abkhazia and South Ossetia — unresolved since the 1990s/2008

Both broke away from Georgian control amid the USSR's collapse (early-1990s wars) and, in South Ossetia's
case, a second war in **August 2008** that ended with Russian troops remaining and Russia formally recognizing
both territories' independence. **Neither is resolved, by force or negotiation, as of this survey.** Russia
maintains military bases in both; almost the entire rest of the international community (with a handful of
exceptions — Syria's transitional government formally recognized both in 2026, a genuinely new addition to the
short recognition list) still treats both as Georgian territory under occupation. **2026 developments**: Russia
is actively deepening institutional/cultural integration programs with both, especially South Ossetia
(described by regional sources as "moving toward Russia" more explicitly than Abkhazia, which is described as
still "seeking international recognition" on its own account); both territories hold elections in 2026-27
under their own de facto governments. **One 2026 recognition reversal is worth flagging as a live data point**:
Nauru withdrew its recognition of both territories on 30 July 2026, following a change of government —
evidence that even the small list of states recognizing Abkhazia/South Ossetia is not fixed.

**Populations, current estimates**: Abkhazia ~243,897 (2025), split across seven districts (Aqwa/Sukhumi,
Gagra, Gal/Gali, Gwdouţa/Gudauta, Gwylryphš'/Gulripshi, Očamčyra/Ochamchira, Tqwarčal/Tkvarcheli). South
Ossetia ~57,528 (2025), split across Dzau/Java, Leningor/Akhalgori, Tskhinvali city, Tskhinvali district, and
Znauyr/Znauri — considerably smaller in both population and administrative complexity than Abkhazia.

**⚠️ A real formal-status asymmetry, worth getting right for Stage 3**: Abkhazia is one of Georgia's two
constitutionally-designated Autonomous Republics (the other being Adjara, §2.3) — a status it held before the
1990s war and formally retains on paper. **South Ossetia does not carry the same formal designation** —
Georgia abolished the Soviet-era "South Ossetian Autonomous Oblast" in 1990 and refers to the territory today
as the "Tskhinvali Region," not an autonomous republic. This is a real legal/administrative distinction, not
just a naming difference, and should not be flattened into treating both territories identically at Stage 3.

**Per this project's "research proposes, the author disposes" rule**: this survey takes no position on
whether either territory should be drawn as Georgian, independent, or contested on any future map — it
records the facts and flags the same live, unresolved status Kirkuk and Israel/Palestine carry elsewhere in
this project.

### 2.3 Adjara — the clean, uncontested case

**Adjara (Ajaria)** is Georgia's other constitutionally-designated Autonomous Republic, capital Batumi, on the
Black Sea coast bordering Turkey. **Unlike Abkhazia and South Ossetia, Adjara has remained under full Tbilisi
control throughout the post-Soviet period** — a 2004 political crisis (the removal of local strongman Aslan
Abashidze) was resolved without secession, and Adjara today functions as an ordinary, uncontested autonomous
region within Georgia, formally established by a 2000 constitutional amendment. **Worth a clean distinction
from Abkhazia/South Ossetia at Stage 3**: same "autonomous republic" formal category as Abkhazia, but zero
live dispute, the same way this project distinguished Iraq's Kurdistan Region (settled) from Kirkuk (disputed)
within the same country.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29, researched further and confirmed.** Real facts checked beyond the above: Adjara's
religious distinctness traces to the 1614 Ottoman conquest and roughly 200 years of gradual Islamization, but
the *current* 2024 census actually shows Adjara Orthodox-majority again — 59.5% Orthodox Christian, 33.9%
Muslim — with real internal variation this map's whole-governorate resolution can't further resolve
(mountainous Khulo ~95.3% Muslim vs. coastal Kobuleti ~71% Orthodox). Most load-bearing fact confirming the
"clean, uncontested" framing above: **Adjara is the only autonomous region in the entire South Caucasus with
no secessionist conflict since Soviet dissolution** — Adjarians maintained a strong Georgian national identity
throughout, including under Abashidze's genuinely authoritarian rule, despite real ongoing Turkish and Russian
geopolitical interest in the region. Author's own call, treating this as Georgia's stable long-term status
rather than a live question: "if its long-term status is as an Autonomous Zone, then let's just go ahead and
keep it that way." No change to this survey's own framing or to `middle-east-map-data/regions.csv`'s existing
`adjara_muslim` flag — already correctly drawn as a religious-minority hatch within Georgia's core, not a
separate or contested region.

**Sources**: [Adjara — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjara) (2024 census religious breakdown, Ottoman-era Islamization history, secession-free status among South Caucasus autonomous regions).

## Section 3 — The land bridge: BTC, BTK, and the Zangezur question

**This region's organizing physical fact, carried forward for Stage 2**: Azerbaijan is a hydrocarbon-wealthy,
landlocked-on-the-Caspian state (like Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan in the companion Central Asia survey) whose
entire export route to world markets runs through exactly one country — Georgia.

- **The Baku-Tbilisi-Ceyhan (BTC) oil pipeline** carries Azerbaijani crude from Baku across Georgia to the
  Turkish Mediterranean port of Ceyhan, explicitly built to bypass both Russia and Armenia.
- **The Baku-Tbilisi-Kars (BTK) railway**, formally inaugurated in stages through 2026 (passenger service
  between Tbilisi and Baku resumed 26 May 2026 after a six-year pause; the full Georgia-Turkey rail link's own
  passenger service was not yet running as of this survey), does the same for rail freight and passengers.
- **The Southern Gas Corridor** (~$40 billion), carrying gas from the Shah Deniz field's second-stage
  expansion across Georgia and Turkey to Europe, completes the third leg of the same structural fact: **almost
  everything Azerbaijan exports overland runs through Georgian territory.**

**A newer, more contentious piece: the Zangezur Corridor / TRIPP.** Azerbaijan's Nakhchivan Autonomous
Republic is a true exclave — separated from the rest of Azerbaijan by a strip of Armenian territory (Syunik
province) — and the proposed corridor would connect the two via a 43 km route through that Armenian territory.
**Construction of an associated energy transmission line began January 2026** (a 330 kV line integrating
Nakhchivan into Azerbaijan's national grid), under the US-brokered "Trump Route for International Peace and
Prosperity" framework tied to the broader 2025 peace process (§2.1). **This remains actively contentious as of
this survey** — Iran in particular has objected to any arrangement that could functionally sever its own
existing land route to Armenia, and Russia's position is unsettled. **Confidence: high** on the corridor's
existence and January 2026 construction start; **medium** on its ultimate political/legal shape, since full
ratification is tied to the same pending 2027 Armenian referendum as the broader peace deal.

**Structural read, echoing the Central Asia survey's own Middle Corridor finding**: where Central Asia is
still trying to build a Russia-bypass corridor at meaningful scale (companion survey §4), the South Caucasus
already has one, fully operational, carrying oil, gas and rail freight today — **Georgia is to this region
what the still-nascent Middle Corridor hopes to become for Central Asia**, and Azerbaijan's entire national
economy depends on it in a way directly comparable to Kazakhstan's CPC-pipeline dependency on Russian transit
territory (material survey §2.1 there) — except here the transit state (Georgia) is a willing, integrated
partner rather than a geopolitical rival.

### 3.1 Who actually lives on either end of the corridor — added 2026-09-28, a real and symmetric population history

⚠️ **Added on a targeted research pass after the author asked directly who lives in Nakhchivan and in
Armenia's Syunik province** (the two ends of the Zangezur Corridor question above). Both territories are
today close to ethnically homogeneous — but arrived there by different mechanisms, on very different
timescales, and each is the demographic mirror of the other.

**Nakhchivan — a century-long, Soviet-policy-driven decline, ending in deliberate cultural erasure.** As
recently as 1916, the territory was 59% Azerbaijani / 40% Armenian — not a token minority. Under Soviet rule
the Armenian share fell steadily, from 15% (1926) to just 1.4% (1979); sources describe the Soviet-era
resettlement/oppression policy as the direct driver. Today the Armenian population is effectively zero — "at
last depleted," in one source's own words. The clearest single marker of that process: **Julfa (Jugha)**, an
old Armenian trading city in Nakhchivan, had a cemetery with what may have been the largest collection of
khachkars (carved Armenian cross-stones) anywhere — an estimated 10,000+ at its height per 17th-century
traveler accounts. It was progressively destroyed across the 20th century and the remainder was leveled
entirely in **December 2005**, filmed from across the border in Iran by an Armenian priest, showing Azerbaijani
soldiers demolishing the last stones with sledgehammers — one of the more internationally documented cases of
deliberate cultural-heritage destruction tied to an ethnic-demographic shift.

**Syunik (Armenia) — the mirror image, compressed into a few violent years instead of a century.**
Azerbaijanis were actually the single largest minority in Armenia as a whole across the entire 1831–1989
census record — a real, substantial, long-standing population, not a footnote. That population is now
virtually gone from Syunik and from Armenia generally: most fled or were forced out during the **First
Nagorno-Karabakh War, concentrated around 1988–91**. One specific Syunik village is recorded as having been
home to 70 Azerbaijani families before the war. Syunik's own older, Turkic-language name — **Zangezur** — is
the direct namesake of the "Zangezur Corridor" itself: the corridor Azerbaijan wants built through Armenian
territory today runs through the same province that used to carry an Azerbaijani-language name, before the
population that used it was displaced.

**Why this matters for the map, and why it's flagged rather than resolved**: both ends of the proposed
corridor are now ethnically homogeneous specifically *because of* the same broader Armenian-Azerbaijani
conflict complex that makes the corridor itself contentious today — this isn't incidental background, it's the
same underlying history recurring at both ends. **⚠️ FLAGGED AS UNCERTAIN TERRITORY, explicitly parked by the
author for a later session, not resolved here**: what this means for how the corridor, Nakhchivan, and Syunik
should eventually be treated on this project's own map is an open design question, not decided by this
research pass — record the history, do not default into a resolution.

**✅ SETTLED 2026-09-29, one piece of the above now resolved: Nakhchivan's own status specifically.** Author
asked directly how Nakhchivan would fare as a fully independent country, separate from both Armenia and
Azerbaijan. Researched and found no plausible basis for it: formally the **Nakhchivan Autonomous Republic**
(same formal category as Georgia's own Adjara, settled the same session, §2.3) — population ~459,600 (2019),
99.7% Azerbaijani, 0.3% Kurds, with no separatist identity basis at all; its one brush with "independence"
(January 1990, the first part of the entire USSR to declare it, weeks before Lithuania) was a protest against
Soviet suppression of Azerbaijan's own national movement, done in solidarity with Baku, not a bid to separate
from Azerbaijan. Economically fragile (real unemployment/energy shortages, emigration to Turkey so heavy that
Istanbul's Besler district is now mostly Nakhchivanis) and totally geography-dependent (landlocked, decades of
Armenian-then-Turkish blockade since the late 1980s/1993). Author's own call: "keep it as an Azerbaijani
Autonomous Republic." **This resolves only Nakhchivan's own independence question** — Syunik's own status, the
Zangezur Corridor/TRIPP question itself, and the deeper population-history "uncertain territory" flag above
all remain exactly as parked.

**Sources**: [Armenians in Nakhchivan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Nakhchivan); [Cemetery of Old Jugha (Julfa) — ArcGIS StoryMaps](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/a3a8b82d7a9d4b8d829ef9d271526acf); [When The World Looked Away: The Destruction Of Julfa Cemetery — RFE/RL](https://www.rferl.org/a/armenia-azerbaijan-julfa-cemetery-destruction-unesco-cultural-heritage/30986581.html); [Azerbaijanis in Armenia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijanis_in_Armenia); [Syunik Province — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syunik_Province); [Syunik Province (Simple Wikipedia)](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syunik_Province); [Nakhchivan Autonomous Republic — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakhchivan_Autonomous_Republic).

## Section 4 — Azerbaijan and Turkey: "one nation, two states"

**A real, official, frequently-invoked diplomatic formula**, not merely a cultural observation. Originating
with President Heydar Aliyev in the 1990s and formalized in the **June 2021 Shusha Declaration** (Presidents
Erdoğan and Aliyev), the phrase describes shared Turkic ethnicity and language, deepening bilateral military
cooperation (joint exercises, Azerbaijani officers training at Turkish military academies), and Turkey's role
as the principal conduit for Azerbaijani oil and gas reaching world markets (§3). **Popular support for the
concept in Azerbaijan is described as broad, and reportedly strengthened further after Azerbaijan's victory in
the 2020 Second Nagorno-Karabakh War.** ⚠️ The relationship was not always this clean — 2000s-era tension
specifically over Turkey's on-again-off-again Armenia normalization efforts frayed the concept before it was
re-consolidated in the 2020s.

**Direct relevance to this project's existing map data**: the Middle East package's Pan-Turkic sphere (S3)
already names Azerbaijan explicitly as a real, well-grounded but previously out-of-scope relative of Turkey's
own Turkic-language identity. **This survey provides the concrete anchor to actually draw that connection**
rather than leave it label-only — see §7 for the specific cross-reference recommendation.

## Section 5 — Armenia's Yazidi/Kurdish population, and the Kurdish Nation seam

Armenia's 2022 census recorded **31,079 Yazidis** and **1,663 Kurds** as separate self-identified groups —
but per Minority Rights Group's own account, **"practically all of those who identified as Kurds... are
members of the Yazidi community who [also] embrace a Kurdish identity; extremely few Muslim Kurds live in
Armenia today."** Yazidi community leaders themselves dispute the census figure as an undercount, citing a
true population that may exceed 50,000. **Either figure makes Armenia's Yazidi population the largest, by
population share, anywhere in the world outside Iraq** — a real, distinctive fact this project's existing
Kurdish Nation region (Middle East package) does not currently reflect at all.

**⚠️ A genuine design question, not resolved here**: Armenia's Yazidi population is religiously and, in
practice, largely ethnically distinct from the Sunni-Muslim-majority Kurdish population the Middle East
survey's Kurdish Nation region is built around (§3 there) — Yazidism is its own distinct religion, not a sect
of Islam, and the community's relationship to a pan-Kurdish national identity is itself debated within Kurdish
and Yazidi politics generally (some Yazidis assert a fully separate ethnic identity from Kurds; the Armenian
census data above suggests most Armenian Yazidis do self-identify with a Kurdish ethnic label when given the
choice). **Whether the existing Kurdish Nation sphere/region should extend any domain flag into Armenia, and
at what confidence level, is left for the author** — flagged with the same "research proposes" posture used
throughout this project, not decided here.

## Section 6 — The closed border, and what it means for connectivity on this project's map

**Turkey closed its land and air borders with Armenia in 1993**, in solidarity with Azerbaijan during the
First Nagorno-Karabakh War (the air border reopened in 1995; the land border has stayed closed for over three
decades). **As of this survey, the two countries still have no formal diplomatic relations, and the land
border remains closed** — but normalization has genuinely accelerated through 2025-26: scheduled
Istanbul-Yerevan flights resumed March 2026, a joint agreement to restore the historic Ani Bridge (spanning
the border itself) was signed, and the two sides have agreed "in principle" to open the land border to
third-country nationals and diplomatic-passport holders, **though implementation had not yet occurred as of
this survey's research date.**

**What this means structurally for Armenia's position on this project's own map**: Armenia's fully open land
borders run only to **Georgia** (north) and **Iran** (south) — its borders with **Turkey** (closed since 1993)
and **Azerbaijan** (closed by the broader conflict, and further complicated by the Zangezur Corridor question,
§3) are both non-functional for ordinary trade and travel as of this survey. This is a genuinely severe
connectivity constraint, worth carrying into Stage 2 the way the Central Asia survey treated Turkmenistan's
self-imposed isolation (companion survey §3.3) — except here the isolation is imposed by two neighbors' closed
borders rather than by Armenia's own policy choice.

## Section 7 — Cross-references to already-built project data, flagged not resolved

### 7.1 The Pan-Turkic sphere (Middle East package, S3) — Azerbaijan is the missing anchor

The Middle East survey's own §7.3 names Azerbaijan explicitly as a real Pan-Turkic relative of Turkey, "outside
this survey's scope but geographically adjacent" — and the Middle East map-data package's `spheres.csv` S3 row
already labels Azerbaijan as sphere-territory to be drawn on "the companion Central Asia map" (which does not,
in fact, cover Azerbaijan either — a genuine gap this survey closes). **Recommendation for Stage 3 (not
decided here, per this project's rule): S3 should be updated to reach into this new South Caucasus package,
with Azerbaijan added as real drawable domain/core territory rather than staying label-only**, given §4's
"one nation, two states" finding is exactly the concrete grounding the Middle East survey's own S3 row was
