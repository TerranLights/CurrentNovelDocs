Fact research, 2026-11-01. Real-world history of the people behind the existing Tepenian Saints and behind the names of Tepenian cities, and of the other figures most directly tied to Antarctica. Facts only: no patronages, Saints' days, virtues or iconography are assigned here (that is later creative work).
- **First round:** the existing Saints and city namesakes ("Figures"), then other major figures who are not city namesakes.
- **Second round** (brief: `RESEARCH_BRIEF_2026-11-01.md`): the companions of the existing Saints, the founders and firsts of the station era, and the namesakes of major places and the organizers.
- **Companion file:** `The_Seven_Saints_in_the_Record.md` holds the record behind the seven existing Saints: their own words (with doubtful quotes marked), faith, graves and memorials, surviving objects, key dates and present-day commemoration.

What a Saint is: cultural and historical reverence, not religious (the author's ruling, 2026-11-01; see `README.md`).

Reminder from canon: `DR-32` (InnerTepeniaGDD Developer Rulings Log) rules that a city's namesake is "flavor only", never data or a tie. This survey records the real people; it does not imply that any city reveres its namesake.

## Figures

### Robert Falcon Scott (1868-1912)
- **Origin:** born June 6, 1868, at Stoke Damerel, Devonport (Plymouth), England. Royal Navy officer; joined as a cadet on the training ship *Britannia* in 1881.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Led the British National Antarctic (*Discovery*) Expedition, 1901-04. Its hut at Hut Point, Ross Island (1902) still stands. With Edward Wilson and Ernest Shackleton he reached a farthest south of about 82°17′S on December 30, 1902. In 1903 he led the western journey up onto the Polar Plateau of Victoria Land.
  - Led the *Terra Nova* (British Antarctic) Expedition, 1910-13, from a base at Cape Evans on Ross Island. The expedition ran a large scientific program alongside the polar attempt. The polar party (Scott, Wilson, Henry Bowers, Lawrence Oates, Edgar Evans) reached the South Pole on January 17, 1912, and found that Amundsen's party had been there about five weeks earlier.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** a disciplined naval leader who put science at the center of both expeditions; on the return the party kept hauling about 16 kg of geological specimens, including *Glossopteris* fossils from the Beardmore Glacier. His journals and "Message to the Public" (published 1913 as *Scott's Last Expedition*) made him a national hero in Britain. Later historians split: Roland Huntford (*Scott and Amundsen*, 1979) faulted his planning and reliance on ponies, motor sledges and man-hauling; Susan Solomon (*The Coldest March*, 2001) documented unusually severe temperatures in March 1912, and Ranulph Fiennes (*Captain Scott*, 2003) defended him. His widow was the sculptor Kathleen Scott; their son was the naturalist Peter Scott. The Scott Polar Research Institute (Cambridge, 1920) was founded in his memory.
- **Death:** died of cold and starvation on the Ross Ice Shelf, about 11 miles (18 km) south of One Ton Depot, around March 29, 1912, in the tent where Wilson and Bowers also died (Evans had died on February 17, Oates walked out about March 16-17). A search party found the tent on November 12, 1912.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Scott (Scott Base site, Ross Island; Janbogo subnet), which keeps Hut Point within its footprint; co-namesake of the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station site, now **Amundsen Station**.
- **Saint status:** St. Robert (project canon; national roster).
- **Sources:**
  - [Terra Nova Expedition (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_Expedition)
  - [Robert Falcon Scott, New Zealand Antarctic Heritage Trust](https://nzaht.org/?p=5997)
  - [Smithsonian Magazine: the search party's discovery of Scott's tent](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-a-search-party-discovered-the-frozen-body-of-a-british-explorer-who-raced-to-the-south-poleand-lost-180985164/)

### Ernest Shackleton (1874-1922)
- **Origin:** born February 15, 1874, at Kilkea, County Kildare, Ireland, to an Anglo-Irish family that moved to London; went to sea in the merchant navy at 16.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Third officer on Scott's *Discovery* Expedition; on the 1902-03 southern journey with Scott and Wilson; sent home in 1903 after he broke down with scurvy.
  - Led the British Antarctic (*Nimrod*) Expedition, 1907-09. On January 9, 1909, his party reached 88°23′S, 97 nautical miles (about 180 km) from the Pole, and turned back to survive. Members of his expedition made the first ascent of Mount Erebus (1908) and reached the area of the South Magnetic Pole (1909). Knighted in 1909.
  - Led the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition, 1914-17, planned as the first crossing of the continent. *Endurance* was beset in the Weddell Sea in January 1915 and sank on November 21, 1915. The crew camped on the floes, reached Elephant Island by boat in April 1916, and from there Shackleton and five men sailed the lifeboat *James Caird* about 800 nautical miles (1,300 km) to South Georgia in 16 days. He, Frank Worsley and Tom Crean then crossed the island's unmapped mountains in about 36 hours to the whaling station at Stromness. On his fourth attempt, in the Chilean tug *Yelcho*, he rescued the Elephant Island party on August 30, 1916. All 28 men of the *Endurance* party survived; the expedition's Ross Sea party lost three men. The wreck was found in March 2022 at about 3,000 m depth.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** remembered above all for leadership under extreme conditions and for bringing every man of the *Endurance* home; the record also shows a charismatic, restless man whose business ventures repeatedly failed and who was often in debt.
- **Death:** died of a heart attack aboard *Quest* at Grytviken, South Georgia, on January 5, 1922, at the start of the Shackleton-Rowett Expedition; buried at Grytviken at his wife Emily's request.
- **Tepenian city or place:** no city carries his name. Signy (South Orkney Islands, Palmer subnet) holds the strongest canon claim; Rothera's claim is contested. (Geography check: Elephant Island lies roughly 500 km west of Signy Island, which fits the Signy spec's "approximately 520 km"; the culture sheet's "within roughly 300 km" refers to the boat route and is not checked here.)
- **Saint status:** St. Ernest (project canon; national roster; which city claims him is open).
- **Sources:**
  - [Ernest Shackleton, Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ernest-Henry-Shackleton)
  - [Sir Ernest Shackleton (1874-1922), ChristchurchNZ Antarctic Office](https://www.christchurchnz.com/christchurch-antarctic-office/christchurch-s-antarctic-heritage/knighted-explorer-sir-ernest-shackleton)
  - [Sir Ernest Henry Shackleton, Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/shackleton-sir-ernest-henry)

### Roald Amundsen (1872-1928)
- **Origin:** born July 16, 1872, at Borge, near Fredrikstad, Norway, into a family of shipowners and captains; gave up medical studies for the sea.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - First mate on Adrien de Gerlache's *Belgica* expedition (1897-99), the first expedition to winter in the Antarctic, when the ship was beset in the Bellingshausen Sea. With the physician Frederick Cook he helped keep the crew alive through scurvy with fresh seal and penguin meat.
  - Led the *Fram* expedition (1910-12). He had announced an Arctic drift, then turned south, telling his crew only at Madeira (September 1910) and warning Scott by telegram. From the base Framheim at the Bay of Whales on the Ross Ice Shelf, he and four companions (Olav Bjaaland, Helmer Hanssen, Sverre Hassel, Oscar Wisting) reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911, by dog sledge and ski, by way of the Axel Heiberg Glacier. All five returned to Framheim on January 25, 1912.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** first through the Northwest Passage (*Gjøa*, 1903-06), where he learned fur clothing and dog driving from the Netsilik Inuit; first at the South Pole; on the *Norge* airship's 1926 flight over the North Pole. The record shows a meticulous, methodical planner, but also a hard man to work for: a premature start for the Pole in September 1911 led to a bitter quarrel with Hjalmar Johansen, whom he dropped from the polar party.
- **Death:** disappeared on June 18, 1928, over the Barents Sea while flying in a French Latham 47 flying boat to search for the survivors of Umberto Nobile's airship *Italia*; neither he nor the crew was ever found, though wreckage from the aircraft was later recovered.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Amundsen Station (the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station site, Amundsen-Scott subnet), and the Amundsen Tower there; canon also calls him the patron Saint of Troll's region. (Record check: he never visited Dronning Maud Land, where Troll lies. the aviators Hjalmar Riiser-Larsen and Finn Lützow-Holm named it in 1930 (see Queen Maud below); the Queen Maud Mountains that Amundsen named in 1911 are on his route from the Ross Ice Shelf.)
- **Saint status:** St. Roald (project canon; national roster).
- **Sources:**
  - [Roald Amundsen (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Amundsen)
  - [Roald Amundsen, The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/article/roald-amundsen)
  - [Roald Amundsen summary, Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Roald-Amundsen)

### Douglas Mawson (1882-1958)
- **Origin:** born May 5, 1882, at Shipley, Yorkshire, England; his family emigrated to Sydney in 1884. Trained as a mining engineer and geologist at the University of Sydney; lecturer, later professor of geology, at the University of Adelaide.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Physicist on Shackleton's *Nimrod* Expedition (1907-09); in the party that made the first ascent of Mount Erebus (1908); with Edgeworth David and Alistair Mackay he reached the area of the South Magnetic Pole on January 16, 1909.
  - Instigator and leader of the Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911-14), with its main base at Cape Denison, Commonwealth Bay, one of the windiest places at sea level on Earth. On the Far Eastern sledging journey, Belgrave Ninnis fell into a crevasse with the best dogs and most of the food (December 14, 1912), and Xavier Mertz died on the return (January 8, 1913). Mawson went on alone, at one point hanging in a crevasse from his sledge rope, and reached Cape Denison on February 8, 1913, hours after the *Aurora* had sailed; he wintered a second year with a small relief party.
  - Led the British Australian and New Zealand Antarctic Research Expedition (BANZARE, 1929-31), whose claims became the Australian Antarctic Territory.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** a scientist first; his expeditions mapped long stretches of the East Antarctic coast and gathered geological, magnetic and biological data. Knighted in 1914. His solitary survival in 1912-13 is one of the best-known endurance stories of the heroic age. He appeared on the Australian $100 note (1984-96).
- **Death:** died at his home in Brighton, Adelaide, on October 14, 1958, of a cerebral hemorrhage.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Mawson (city and subnet; Mawson Station site, Mac.Robertson Land). Denison (Cape Denison) stands on his 1911-14 expedition base.
- **Saint status:** St. Douglas (project canon; national roster).
- **Sources:**
  - [Mawson, Sir Douglas (1882-1958), Australian Dictionary of Biography](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/mawson-sir-douglas-7531)
  - [Douglas Mawson, Australian Antarctic Program](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/history/people/douglas-mawson/)
  - [Douglas Mawson, Mawson's Huts (Australian Antarctic Division)](https://mawsonshuts.antarctica.gov.au/cape-denison/the-people/douglas-mawson/)

### Richard E. Byrd (1888-1957)
- **Origin:** born October 25, 1888, in Winchester, Virginia, USA, to a prominent Virginia family; U.S. Naval Academy graduate (1912), then a naval aviator and navigation specialist.
- **In Antarctica:** led or commanded five American Antarctic undertakings.
  - First expedition (1928-30): built the base Little America at the Bay of Whales on the Ross Ice Shelf (from January 1, 1929). On November 28-29, 1929, as navigator, with pilot Bernt Balchen, radioman/co-pilot Harold June and photographer Ashley McKinley, he made the first flight over the South Pole and back, about 19 hours. He named Marie Byrd Land, the region east of the Ross Sea, for his wife. Promoted to rear admiral.
  - Second expedition (1933-35): wintered alone from March to August 1934 at Bolling Advance Base, a weather hut buried in the Ross Ice Shelf about 196 km (123 miles) south of Little America; carbon monoxide from a faulty stove nearly killed him before a party reached him. Told in his book *Alone* (1938).
  - Commander of the U.S. Antarctic Service Expedition (1939-41); officer in charge of Operation Highjump (1946-47), a U.S. Navy operation of about 4,700 men and 13 ships that photographed much of the coast from the air; officer in charge of U.S. Antarctic programs during Operation Deep Freeze (1955-56), the prelude to the International Geophysical Year.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** a gifted organizer, fundraiser and publicist who used aircraft, radio and aerial photography to explore and map large unknown areas, especially Marie Byrd Land. Awarded the Medal of Honor for his claimed 1926 flight to the North Pole; that claim has long been doubted (his own flight diary, published in 1996, raised further questions), while his South Pole flight is not disputed.
- **Death:** died in his sleep at his home in Boston, Massachusetts, on March 11, 1957, after a long illness (heart disease); buried at Arlington National Cemetery.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Byrd (city and subnet; Byrd Station site, Marie Byrd Land, about 1,530 m). `DR-58`: Byrd keeps its name.
- **Saint status:** St. Richard (project canon for the honorific; national roster; the Byrd spec leaves reverence for him to the city's culture).
- **Sources:**

### Carsten Borchgrevink (1864-1934)
- **Origin:** born December 1, 1864, in Christiania (Oslo), Norway, son of a Norwegian lawyer and an English-born mother (Annie Ridley). Trained in forestry in Germany, emigrated to Australia in 1888, worked with survey teams in Queensland and New South Wales and taught at Cooerwull Academy in Bowenfels.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Signed on to Henrik Bull's whaling voyage in the *Antarctic*. On January 24, 1895, the party landed at Cape Adare, claimed as the first landing on the Antarctic mainland; who stepped ashore first (Borchgrevink, Captain Leonard Kristensen or seaman Alexander von Tunzelmann) was disputed.
  - Led the British-financed (Sir George Newnes) *Southern Cross* Expedition, 1898-1900. The ship reached Cape Adare on February 17, 1899, and landed ten men and about seventy dogs, who put up two prefabricated huts and spent the 1899 winter there, the first expedition to winter deliberately on the Antarctic mainland. The zoologist Nicolai Hanson died on October 14, 1899, the first person buried on the continent. On February 16, 1900, Borchgrevink sledged on the Ross Ice Shelf to a farthest south of 78°50′S. The expedition was back in New Zealand on April 1, 1900. Its huts still stand at Cape Adare.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** his expedition proved people could winter on the mainland and pointed the way the British expeditions later took. The record of his leadership is poor: the physicist Louis Bernacchi wrote that he "was not a good leader" and described "dirt, disorder and inactivity" in camp. The British geographical establishment, led by Sir Clements Markham, opposed and largely ignored him; recognition came late, with the Royal Geographical Society's Patron's Medal in 1930. Roald Amundsen was a long-time supporter.
- **Death:** died in Oslo on April 21, 1934, aged 69 (cause not found in the sources checked). After fieldwork on Mount Pelée in Martinique (1902) he had settled at Slemdal in Kristiania, by the Norwegian national biography "mainly a disappointed man, most often hard up, bordering on poverty" (translated); late recognition came with a state stipend voted by the Storting (1929), the Patron's Medal (1930) and honorary membership of the Norwegian Geographical Society (1931). He is buried in a family grave at Vår Frelsers gravlund (Our Savior's Cemetery), Oslo.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Cape Adare (Janbogo subnet). The cape itself was named by James Clark Ross for Viscount Adare (skipped above).
- **Saint status:** St. Carsten (canon in Cape Adare's city files; not on the national roster; observance date open under `DRQ-01b`). For that open question, the record gives: arrival at Cape Adare February 17, 1899; the wintering, February 1899 to the ship's return on January 28, 1900.
- **Sources:**
  - [Carsten Borchgrevink (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carsten_Borchgrevink)
  - [Southern Cross Expedition (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Cross_Expedition)
  - [Carsten Borchgrevink, Store norske leksikon / Norsk biografisk leksikon](https://snl.no/Carsten_Borchgrevink)

### Jules Dumont d'Urville (1790-1842)
- **Origin:** born May 23, 1790, at Condé-sur-Noireau, Normandy, France; entered the French Navy in 1807. A naval officer who was also a serious botanist and linguist.
- **In Antarctica:** commanded the *Astrolabe*, with the *Zélée*, on his third great voyage (1837-40), sent in part to beat James Weddell's farthest south.
  - In early 1838 the ships were stopped by pack ice in the Weddell Sea, then explored and charted the northern tip of the Antarctic Peninsula (Joinville Island and the land he named Louis Philippe Land).
  - In January 1840, sailing south from Hobart, he sighted the coast of East Antarctica on January 20. On January 21, 1840, a party landed on a small offshore islet, raised the French flag and took rock samples. He named the land Adélie Land (Terre Adélie) for his wife Adèle; the Adélie penguin carries the same name. On January 29 his ships met the U.S. Exploring Expedition's *Porpoise* off the same coast.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** before Antarctica he secured the Venus de Milo for France (1820) and led the *Astrolabe*'s 1826-29 Pacific voyage, which found relics of the lost La Pérouse expedition at Vanikoro. He proposed the division of the Pacific into Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia (1832). The record shows a scientifically driven, independent-minded commander who was often at odds with his superiors; scurvy hit his crews hard on the Antarctic voyage. Promoted rear admiral in 1840.
- **Death:** killed on May 8, 1842, with his wife Adèle and their son, in the Meudon rail disaster on the Versailles-Paris line, the first major railway accident in France.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Dumont d'Urville (Petrel Island, Géologie Archipelago, Adélie Land; Janbogo subnet). Founders are Japan; `DR-58` gives it a new name, not yet chosen.
- **Saint status:** St. Jules (canon in the city's culture file; not on the national roster). St. Jules Day's January 21 matches the landing date in d'Urville's own log; by the corrected calendar it was January 22, because the expedition had not adjusted for crossing the 180th meridian (details in `The_Seven_Saints_in_the_Record.md`).

### Nathaniel B. Palmer (1799-1877)
- **Origin:** born August 8, 1799, in Stonington, Connecticut, USA, a shipbuilder's son; went to sea young and was an experienced sealer by his early twenties.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Captain, at 21, of the sloop *Hero* (about 14 m long, crew of four besides himself), the scouting vessel of a Stonington sealing fleet under Benjamin Pendleton working the South Shetland Islands.
  - On November 17, 1820, sailing south from Deception Island, he recorded "land not yet laid down on my chart": the mountains of the Antarctic Peninsula, one of the earliest sightings of the continent. In February 1821 the *Hero* met Bellingshausen's Russian expedition near the South Shetlands.
  - In December 1821, with the British sealer George Powell, he discovered the South Orkney Islands, where Signy Island lies.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** a skilled, enterprising young seaman whose priority claim for sighting the continent became a long-running American-British-Russian dispute (Edward Bransfield and Bellingshausen both have earlier 1820 claims). He later became a leading clipper and packet ship captain and designer in the China trade. Palmer Land and the Palmer Archipelago are named for him.
- **Death:** died in San Francisco on June 21, 1877, aged 77, while traveling (June 21 is also the calendar date of the Falkland Treaty in canon; noted as coincidence only).
- **Tepenian city or place:** Palmer City (Palmer Station site, Anvers Island, Palmer Archipelago; Palmer subnet), the first settled place in Tepenia.
- **Saint status:** none.

### John M. Rothera (born 1934)
- **Origin:** John Michael Rothera, born January 17, 1934, in Huddersfield, West Riding of Yorkshire, England; his father ran a small cloth-trade business. He went to King James's School, Huddersfield, took a geography degree at Leeds University, and joined the Falkland Islands Dependencies Survey straight from university, training at the School of Military Survey near Newbury (from his own 2013 oral-history interview).
- **In Antarctica:** surveyor with the Falkland Islands Dependencies Survey (FIDS), 1956-60, at the Horseshoe Island station (1957-58) and the Detaille Island station (1958-59). With Peter Gibbs he surveyed the point on Adelaide Island in the 1957-58 season, after it had been photographed from the air by the Falkland Islands and Dependencies Aerial Survey Expedition. The UK Antarctic Place-Names Committee named it Rothera Point for him in 1960; the British Antarctic Survey's Rothera Research Station (opened 1975) stands there.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** a working field surveyor of the postwar mapping era, not an expedition leader. At Rothera Point his party built a trig point and a large cairn on the exposed rock, visible from miles around. Back in England he worked a few weeks on Antarctic place-names at the Foreign Office research department, spent a year at the Directorate of Overseas Survey, and then made his career in town planning (Dorset County Council, then the Basingstoke Development Group); he retired as head of forward planning and design at Basingstoke Council. He never went back to Antarctica and did not know of the station until it was built: "it isn't actually my base, it's only by chance they put the base at Rothera Point."
- **Death:** no death record found; he was alive in January 2013.

### Gustavo Argentino Marambio (1918-1953)

### Edmond Halley (1656-1742)
- **Origin:** born November 8, 1656 (October 29, Old Style) at Haggerston, near London, England, son of a wealthy soap-maker; educated at St Paul's School and Queen's College, Oxford.
- **In Antarctica:** only at its edge. Commissioned as a captain in the Royal Navy, he commanded the pink *Paramour* on the first English naval voyage made purely for science, to map the variation of the compass. On his second voyage (September 1699 to September 1700) he sailed to 52°24′S. On February 1, 1700, north of South Georgia, he met three great tabular icebergs, first taking them for land, and left what may be the earliest drawings and written descriptions of Antarctic tabular icebergs. The results appeared as his *General Chart of the Variation of the Compass* (1701).
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** astronomer and mathematician; calculated the orbit of the comet named for him and predicted its 1758 return; persuaded Isaac Newton to write the *Principia* and paid for its printing; mapped the southern sky from St Helena; Astronomer Royal from 1720.
- **Death:** died at Greenwich on January 14, 1742, aged 85; buried at St Margaret's, Lee.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Halley (Halley Research Station site, Brunt Ice Shelf; namesake of the Halley subnet). The station, founded by the Royal Society's expedition for the International Geophysical Year (1956), took its name from Halley Bay, named for him.
- **Saint status:** none.

### Georg von Neumayer (1826-1909)
- **Origin:** Georg Balthasar Neumayer, born June 21, 1826, at Kirchheimbolanden in the Palatinate (then Bavaria), son of a notary; studied at the polytechnic and university in Munich; went to sea and to the Australian goldfields as a young man.
- **In Antarctica:** never went. For about fifty years he was the leading German advocate of Antarctic research, above all of geomagnetic observation in the far south.
  - In Melbourne he founded and directed the Flagstaff Observatory for geophysics, magnetism and nautical science (1857-64) and made a magnetic survey of Victoria.

### Mikhail Lazarev (1788-1851)
- **Origin:** Mikhail Petrovich Lazarev, born November 14, 1788 (November 3, Old Style) in Vladimir, Russia, into a minor noble family; trained in the Naval Cadet Corps and served as a volunteer in the British Royal Navy (1803-08).
- **In Antarctica:** second-in-command of the First Russian Antarctic Expedition (1819-21) under Bellingshausen and captain of its second ship, the sloop *Mirny* ("Peaceful"). He had fitted out the expedition's ships. The two sloops kept company through the circumnavigation of Antarctica, and on January 27-28, 1820 (New Style) both sighted the ice of the continent's coast, one of the first sightings of Antarctica. They also discovered Peter I Island and Alexander I Land (January 1821).
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** made three circumnavigations (in *Suvorov* 1813-16, which reached Russian America; in *Mirny* 1819-21; in the frigate *Kreiser* 1822-25). Commanded the ship of the line *Azov* at the Battle of Navarino (1827), where *Azov* won the first St. George ensign in the Russian navy. As commander of the Black Sea Fleet (1833-51) and military governor of Sevastopol and Nikolayev, he rebuilt the fleet and Sevastopol's naval base, and trained the admirals Nakhimov, Kornilov and Istomin. Remembered as a strict, exacting and incorruptible commander.
- **Death:** died in Vienna on April 23, 1851 (April 11, Old Style), of illness (cancer, by the standard Russian accounts), while seeking medical treatment; buried in Sevastopol.
- **Tepenian city or place:** **Lazar** (the Novolazarevskaya Station site, "New Lazarev"; Halley subnet). **Mirny** (Mirny Station site, named for his ship; Mirny subnet hub).
- **Saint status:** none.

### Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen (1778-1852)
- **Origin:** born September 20, 1778 (September 9, Old Style) on the island of Ösel (Saaremaa), in present-day Estonia, a Baltic German noble; in Russian, Faddei Faddeevich Bellinsgauzen. Entered the Russian navy as a boy cadet.
- **In Antarctica:** commander of the First Russian Antarctic Expedition (1819-21) and captain of its flagship, the 28-gun sloop *Vostok* ("East"), with Lazarev's *Mirny*. The expedition left Kronstadt on July 3, 1819, and returned on August 5, 1821. It circumnavigated Antarctica at high southern latitudes, crossing the Antarctic Circle several times. On January 28, 1820 (New Style) it sighted the ice shelf of the coast now called the Princess Martha Coast, a candidate for the first sighting of the Antarctic continent (Edward Bransfield and Nathaniel Palmer have rival 1820 claims). In January 1821 it discovered Peter I Island and Alexander I Land, the first land found south of the Antarctic Circle, and in February 1821 met Palmer's *Hero* in the South Shetlands.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** earlier sailed in Adam Johann von Krusenstern's first Russian circumnavigation (1803-06), as a cartographer. The record shows a careful navigator and a humane commander who lost very few men on a two-year polar voyage. His account was published in 1831. Later an admiral, he fought in the Russo-Turkish War of 1828-29 and became military governor of Kronstadt in 1839.
- **Death:** died in Kronstadt on January 25, 1852 (January 13, Old Style), aged 73; a monument to him stands there.
- **Tepenian city or place:** the central scientific district of **Ariun Nuur** keeps the name "Vostok" (city formerly Vostok, after Vostok Station, which is named for his ship; renamed by `DR-42`). The Bellingshausen Sea and Russia's Bellingshausen Station (King George Island) are not Tepenian cities in the register.
- **Saint status:** none.

### Archibald McMurdo (1812-1875)
- **Origin:** born September 24, 1812, in Scotland (Dumfriesshire family), son of Lieutenant Colonel Archibald McMurdo and grandson of John McMurdo, chamberlain at Drumlanrig Castle. Joined the Royal Navy at 12 (October 6, 1824).
- **In Antarctica:** first lieutenant (senior lieutenant) of HMS *Terror*, under Captain Francis Crozier, on James Clark Ross's Antarctic expedition with HMS *Erebus* (1839-43). The expedition discovered the Ross Sea, the Victoria Land coast, Ross Island with Mount Erebus and Mount Terror, and the Ross Ice Shelf. In February 1841 Ross named the sound west of Ross Island (McMurdo Sound) for him, and recommended him to the Admiralty for promotion.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** promoted lieutenant in 1836 for skill and courage in rescuing the crew of a shipwrecked whaler in New Zealand; before the Antarctic voyage he served in *Terror* on George Back's Arctic expedition to Hudson Bay (1836-37), when the ship was frozen in and so damaged that she barely reached Ireland. Later commanded HMS *Contest* off West Africa (from 1846); captain 1851; retired as a rear admiral, promoted vice-admiral on the retired list in 1873. His sound became the gateway for Scott's and Shackleton's expeditions and, from 1956, the site of McMurdo Station, Antarctica's largest settlement.
- **Death:** died on December 11, 1875, aged 63, at Cargenholm, in Troqueer parish, Kirkcudbrightshire, near Dumfries, Scotland (cause not found in the sources checked).
- **Tepenian city or place:** Fort McMurdo (McMurdo Station site, Ross Island; Janbogo subnet), the largest pre-war Tepenian city and de facto capital. Canon's "Fort" refers to Fort McMurray, Alberta, not to the person.
- **Saint status:** none.

### John King Davis (1884-1967)
- **Origin:** born February 19, 1884, at Kew, Surrey, England; went to sea young in sailing ships; later settled in Australia.
- **In Antarctica:**
  - Chief officer of the *Nimrod* on Shackleton's British Antarctic Expedition (1907-09), and master of the ship on the homeward voyage.

### Hugh Denison (1865-1940)

### Mario Zucchelli (1944-2003)
- **Origin:** Italian engineer and scientist, born July 13, 1944, at Crevalcore (province of Bologna); director of ENEA's Brasimone Research Center.
- **In Antarctica:** from September 1987 head of the Antarctic project of ENEA (the Italian national agency for new technologies and energy) and responsible for carrying out Italy's National Antarctic Research Program (PNRA, begun 1985). He directed the ENEA Antarctic Technical Unit for sixteen years and took part in fifteen Antarctic expeditions. He turned Italy's Terra Nova Bay base on the Ross Sea (operating from 1986) from a wooden hut and tents into a permanent station of about 7,000 square meters, and brought Italy into the major international programs, notably the Franco-Italian Concordia Station at Dome C and its ice-core drilling for climate research.
- **Notable deeds and character in the record:** an engineer-administrator and builder rather than an explorer; the record emphasizes organization, logistics and international partnership. Italy's President Carlo Azeglio Ciampi awarded him the Gold Medal for Merit in School, Culture and Art on October 16, 2003. France issued a stamp in his honor in 2019, for his role in Concordia.
- **Death:** died October 24, 2003, in Bologna, eight days after the medal award (Italian Wikipedia; the October 16 date in one press source is the medal's). Cause not found in the sources checked. The Terra Nova Bay station was renamed Stazione Mario Zucchelli after his death.
- **Tepenian city or place:** Zukelli (Mario Zucchelli Station site, Terra Nova Bay; Janbogo subnet), to be renamed (`DR-58`; founders Australia). Factual note: he was also a builder of the real Concordia Station, the site of the game's main city, Concordia.
- **Saint status:** none.

### David E. Bunger (life dates not found)

### James Cook (1728-1779)
- **Life and origin:** Born October 27, 1728 (Old Style; November 7 New Style) at Marton, Yorkshire, England, the son of a farm laborer. He trained in the North Sea coal trade at Whitby and joined the Royal Navy in 1755.
- **Antarctic role:** On his second voyage (1772-1775, HMS *Resolution* with HMS *Adventure*) he was sent to settle whether a great southern continent existed. On January 17, 1773, *Resolution* became the first ship on record to cross the Antarctic Circle, and he crossed it twice more. On January 30, 1774, he reached 71°10′S, a farthest south that stood for nearly 50 years, and turned back from pack ice about 120 km from a coast he never saw. He circumnavigated the Southern Ocean at high latitude, charted and claimed South Georgia, and found the South Sandwich Islands (1775).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He concluded that any southern land lay locked in ice and would be worthless, and his report of abundant seals at South Georgia opened the Southern Ocean sealing trade that later found the continent. He is remembered as a precise surveyor and a commander who kept scurvy down by enforcing diet and hygiene. Accounts of his third voyage record a sharper temper and harsher discipline.
- **Death:** Killed on February 14, 1779, at Kealakekua Bay, Hawaii, during his third voyage. He was struck and stabbed on the shore while trying to take the chief Kalaniʻōpuʻu hostage for the return of a stolen ship's boat.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Edward Bransfield (c. 1785-1852)
- **Life and origin:** Born about 1785 at Ballinacurra, County Cork, Ireland. He was impressed into the Royal Navy in 1803 at about 18 and rose to the warrant rank of master.
- **Antarctic role:** In 1819-1820 the Royal Navy chartered William Smith's brig *Williams* from Valparaíso to survey Smith's newly found South Shetland Islands, with Bransfield in command. On January 30, 1820, crossing the strait now named for him, he sighted and charted the Trinity Peninsula, the northern tip of the Antarctic Peninsula. That is widely credited as one of the first sightings of the Antarctic mainland, days after Bellingshausen's sighting of the ice shelf.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He charted much of the South Shetlands and claimed them for Britain. Little personal detail survives, and his own journal is lost; he received little public credit in his lifetime, and his reputation was revived only in the 20th and 21st centuries (Irish commemorations in 2020 for the bicentenary).
- **Death:** Died October 31, 1852, at Brighton, England, aged about 67.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (The real Bransfield Strait does not appear in the glossary.)

### James Weddell (1787-1834)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 24, 1787, at Ostend (now Belgium) to a Scottish father; raised in Britain. He went to sea young, served in the Royal Navy and then the merchant service.
- **Antarctic role:** A sealing captain. Commanding the brig *Jane* with the cutter *Beaufoy*, he reached 74°15′S on February 20, 1823, in open water in the sea later named for him, beating Cook's farthest south by about 214 nautical miles. The record stood until James Clark Ross passed it in January 1841.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He named the sea for George IV; it was renamed the Weddell Sea in 1900. He published *A Voyage Towards the South Pole* (1825), and the Weddell seal carries his name. The record shows a capable navigator and businessman whose ventures failed after the sealing boom collapsed.
- **Death:** Died September 9, 1834, in London, aged 47, in relative poverty.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him as a settlement. The real Weddell Sea appears throughout canon (Halley subnet coast, the Peninsula-Belgrano radio link and Weddell shipping).

### John Biscoe (1794-1843)
- **Life and origin:** Born April 28, 1794, at Enfield, Middlesex, England. He served in the Royal Navy in the War of 1812 and then in merchant ships.
- **Antarctic role:** In 1830 the whaling and sealing firm Samuel Enderby & Sons gave him the brig *Tula* and the cutter *Lively* to look for new sealing grounds. On February 24-28, 1831, he sighted Enderby Land and judged correctly that it was part of a continent. In February 1832 he found Adelaide Island and the Biscoe Islands, landed on the Antarctic Peninsula and claimed it as Graham Land. His was the third circumnavigation of Antarctica, after Cook and Bellingshausen.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The voyage found few seals and cost heavily. When *Tula* reached Hobart in May 1831 after the Enderby Land leg, two men had died of scurvy and only Biscoe and four others could stand; *Lively* fared worse, with only three of its ten-man crew surviving. On his return in 1833 the Royal Geographical Society gave him its Royal Premium (the forerunner of its gold medal). The record shows endurance and sound geographic judgment rather than commercial success.
- **Death:** Died at sea in 1843, in poor health and impoverished, on a voyage from Australia to England.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.
- **Sources:**

### James Clark Ross (1800-1862)
- **Life and origin:** Born April 15, 1800, in London, England, nephew of the Arctic explorer Sir John Ross. He entered the Royal Navy at 12 and served on his uncle's and William Edward Parry's Arctic expeditions; on June 1, 1831, he located the North Magnetic Pole.
- **Antarctic role:** Commanded the Royal Navy's Antarctic expedition of 1839-1843 in HMS *Erebus*, with Francis Crozier in HMS *Terror*, chiefly to study terrestrial magnetism. In January 1841 he broke through the pack into the Ross Sea, discovered Victoria Land, the volcanoes Mount Erebus and Mount Terror and the great ice cliff now called the Ross Ice Shelf. On February 23, 1842, he reached 78°10′S, a farthest south that stood until 1900. Later seasons charted parts of the Weddell Sea side and James Ross Island.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He brought both ships and nearly all their men home across four years of ice navigation, including a collision between the two ships among icebergs in March 1842. His *A Voyage of Discovery and Research in the Southern and Antarctic Regions* (1847) is the classic account. He was knighted in 1844, declined command of the 1845 Franklin expedition (by a promise to his wife), and led the first search for it in 1848-1849.
- **Death:** Died April 3, 1862, at Aylesbury, Buckinghamshire, England, aged 61, some years after the death of his wife Anne in 1857.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him as a settlement. Real names derived from him appear in canon: the Ross Sea (Janbogo subnet coast), Ross Island (Fort McMurdo and Scott), the Ross Ice Shelf (Byrd subnet) and James Ross Island (Highway 1 causeway chain).
- **Sources:**

  - EBSCO Research Starters, "James Clark Ross": https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/james-clark-ross
  - Wikipedia, "James Clark Ross": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clark_Ross

### Charles Wilkes (1798-1877)
- **Life and origin:** Born April 3, 1798, in New York City, United States. He joined the U.S. Navy as a midshipman in 1818 and specialized in surveying and astronomy.
- **Antarctic role:** Commanded the United States Exploring Expedition (1838-1842), a six-ship naval scientific expedition. In January-February 1840 his squadron sailed west along about 1,500 miles (2,400 km) of the East Antarctic coast and reported land at several points, which he took as evidence of a continent. That stretch is now Wilkes Land.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The expedition charted much of the Pacific and the Pacific Northwest, and its collections became a founding core of the Smithsonian Institution. It lost two ships and 28 men. Wilkes pressed on along the ice against his surgeons' advice; he was a harsh and quarrelsome commander, and on return he was court-martialed and publicly reprimanded for excessive punishment of sailors. In the Civil War he seized Confederate envoys from the British mail ship *Trent* (1861, the Trent Affair), and a second court-martial in 1864 for disobedience suspended him. He retired as a rear admiral.
- **Death:** Died February 8, 1877, at his home in Washington, D.C.; his remains were moved to Arlington National Cemetery in 1909.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him as a settlement. Canon has the "Wilkes Station ruins" (a pre-exile American station near Casey, itself named for Wilkes Land) and uses the real name Wilkes Land for the Mirny subnet coast.
- **Sources:**

### Adrien de Gerlache (1866-1934)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 2, 1866, at Hasselt, Belgium; Adrien Victor Joseph, later Baron de Gerlache de Gomery. A Belgian naval officer.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the Belgian Antarctic Expedition (1897-1899) in *Belgica*, a refitted Norwegian whaler. In January-February 1898 he explored and named features along the west side of the Antarctic Peninsula (the Gerlache Strait). He then took the ship into the Bellingshausen Sea pack, where it was beset from early March 1898 to March 1899, so that *Belgica* was the first expedition ship to winter in the Antarctic.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** His international crew included Roald Amundsen, the American doctor Frederick Cook, the Polish scientists Henryk Arctowski and Antoni Dobrowolski and the Romanian biologist Emil Racoviță. During the polar night two men died (the sailor Carl Wiencke drowned; the geophysicist Emile Danco died of heart failure), scurvy spread, and some of the crew suffered breakdowns; Gerlache himself fell seriously ill, and Cook and Amundsen led the fresh-meat regime that saved the rest. He later commanded voyages for the Duke of Orléans in the Arctic. The ship *Polaris* was built in Norway for a polar-cruise venture of his with Lars Christensen; when the venture fell through, Shackleton bought her in 1914 and renamed her *Endurance*.
- **Death:** Died December 4, 1934, in Brussels, aged 68, of illness (paratyphoid fever, per Wikipedia).
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Otto Nordenskjöld (1869-1928)
- **Life and origin:** Nils Otto Gustaf Nordenskjöld, born December 6, 1869, at Hässleby, Småland, Sweden, nephew of the Arctic explorer Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld. A geologist, he became professor of geography at Gothenburg.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the Swedish Antarctic Expedition (1901-1903/04), with Carl Anton Larsen as captain of the ship *Antarctic*. In February 1902 he and five companions (among them the Argentine naval officer José María Sobral) landed at Snow Hill Island to winter. The ship failed to return through the ice in 1902-1903 and was crushed and sank on February 12, 1903. The expedition then split into three groups: Nordenskjöld's party at Snow Hill, three men in a stone hut at Hope Bay, and Larsen's crew on Paulet Island. They survived a second winter, Nordenskjöld met the Hope Bay men by chance on Vega Island in October 1903, and the Argentine corvette *Uruguay* under Julián Irízar rescued all three groups in November 1903.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Only one man died (the seaman Ole Wennersgaard, on Paulet Island). The expedition did significant geology and paleontology, including fossil finds on Seymour Island. Nordenskjöld is remembered as a scholar-explorer; his account, *Antarctica, or Two Years Amongst the Ice of the South Pole* (1904), is the classic record.
- **Death:** Killed on June 2, 1928, in Gothenburg, aged 58, when he was struck by a bus.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Canon's Highway 1 crosses Snow Hill Island, the site of his hut; the island is not named for him.)

### Carl Anton Larsen (1860-1924)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 7, 1860, at Østre Halsen, Tjolling, near Larvik, Norway, the son of a sea captain. He went to sea young and became a whaling and sealing captain.
- **Antarctic role:** On a whaling reconnaissance in the *Jason* (1892-1894) he found the first fossils known from Antarctica, on Seymour Island (1892), and sailed down the east coast of the Antarctic Peninsula past the ice shelf later named for him. He captained *Antarctic* for Nordenskjöld's Swedish expedition and, after the ship sank in 1903, led its crew of about 20 through a winter on Paulet Island in a stone hut. In 1904 he founded the whaling station at Grytviken, South Georgia, for the Compañía Argentina de Pesca, founded with Buenos Aires capital, which began the modern Antarctic whaling industry. In 1923-1924 he took the factory ship *Sir James Clark Ross* into the Ross Sea, opening whaling there.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Practical seaman and businessman; the record credits his leadership with the survival of the *Antarctic* crew on Paulet Island. He became a British subject in 1910. His legacy includes the industrial whaling that devastated Southern Ocean whale stocks.
- **Death:** Died December 8, 1924, aboard the *Sir James Clark Ross* on the Ross Sea whaling grounds, after his health had been failing; buried at Sandefjord, Norway.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (His 1892 fossil finds were on Seymour Island, the site of the Tepenian city Marambio, which is not named for him.)
- **Sources:**

### Jean-Baptiste Charcot (1867-1936)
- **Life and origin:** Born July 15, 1867, at Neuilly-sur-Seine, France, son of the neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot. He qualified as a physician and practiced medicine before turning to the sea in his thirties.
- **Antarctic role:** Led two French Antarctic expeditions in ships built for them, the first French polar ships. In the *Français* (1903-1905) he wintered at Booth Island and surveyed the west coast of the Antarctic Peninsula; the voyage was partly organized to help the missing Nordenskjöld expedition. In the *Pourquoi-Pas?* (1908-1910) he wintered at Petermann Island and charted farther south, including Marguerite Bay (named for his second wife) and the land he named for his father (Charcot Island).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** His surveys of the peninsula's west coast were among the most accurate of the era, and the expeditions returned large scientific collections. Contemporaries, Scott among them, called him "the gentleman of the pole." His first marriage, to Victor Hugo's granddaughter Jeanne Hugo, ended in divorce after his first expedition. He later ran oceanographic cruises to Greenland and the Arctic.
- **Death:** Died September 16, 1936, aged 69, when the *Pourquoi-Pas?* was wrecked in a storm on reefs off western Iceland; of those aboard only the master steersman Eugène Gonidec survived.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Wilhelm Filchner (1877-1957)
- **Life and origin:** Born September 13, 1877, in Munich, Bavaria, Germany. A Bavarian army officer and surveyor, already known before Antarctica for a ride across the Pamirs (1900) and a Tibet expedition (1903-1905).
- **Antarctic role:** Led the Second German Antarctic Expedition (1911-1912/13) in the *Deutschland* to the Weddell Sea, partly to test whether the Weddell and Ross Seas were joined. In January-February 1912 he discovered the Luitpold Coast and the ice front of what is now the Filchner Ice Shelf (the Kaiser asked that it carry the discoverer's name). An attempt to build a wintering station on the ice failed when the ice broke away. *Deutschland* was then beset and drifted with the Weddell Sea pack for about nine months before breaking free in late 1912.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The expedition was crippled by a feud between Filchner and the ship's captain, Richard Vahsel, who died in the ice in August 1912; the quarrels and the recriminations after the return damaged Filchner's reputation in Germany. Its drift observations still disproved the supposed "New South Greenland." He spent most of his later life on geomagnetic surveys in Central Asia, was interned by the British during the Second World War, and returned to Europe in 1949.
- **Death:** Died May 7, 1957, in Zurich, Switzerland, aged 79.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Nobu Shirase (1861-1946)
- **Life and origin:** Born June 13, 1861, at Konoura, Akita Prefecture, Japan, son of a Buddhist temple priest. An army officer, he served in a Kuril Islands expedition in the 1890s and suffered through a scurvy-ridden winter there.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the Japanese Antarctic Expedition (1910-1912) in the *Kainan Maru*, a small converted fishing vessel, the first Antarctic expedition by a non-Western nation, funded largely by public subscription with backing from Count Ōkuma Shigenobu. The first attempt (1911) was turned back by ice and the party wintered in Sydney. In January 1912 the ship reached the Bay of Whales, where it met Amundsen's *Fram*. Shirase's "Dash Patrol" sledged onto the Ross Ice Shelf to 80°05′S on January 28, 1912, naming the area Yamato Yukihara, and a separate party made the first landing on King Edward VII Land.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Undeterred by scorn from officials and the press, he went with minimal money, equipment and polar experience and brought all his men home. Fame faded quickly, and he spent decades paying off the expedition's debts, finishing in 1935.
- **Death:** Died September 4, 1946, aged 85, in relative poverty, nearly forgotten, in a rented room above a fish shop in Aichi Prefecture. Japan later named its Antarctic icebreakers *Shirase* after him.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (The Mirny-subnet city Shirayuki means "white snow" and is not named for him.)
- **Sources:**

### Frank Wild (1873-1939)
- **Life and origin:** John Robert Francis Wild, born April 18, 1873, at Skelton-in-Cleveland, Yorkshire, England, one of a large family of the schoolteacher Benjamin Wild. He went to sea in the merchant service at 16 and joined the Royal Navy in 1900.
- **Antarctic role:** Served on five Antarctic expeditions, more than nearly anyone of the heroic age. He was an able seaman on Scott's *Discovery* expedition (1901-1904); one of Shackleton's four-man southern party on *Nimrod* (1907-1909), which reached 88°23′S; leader of the Western Base on Mawson's Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911-1914); second-in-command of Shackleton's *Endurance* expedition (1914-1917); and second-in-command of the *Quest* expedition (1921-1922).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** On Elephant Island he led the 22 men left behind from April 24 to August 30, 1916, while Shackleton sailed for help; the record credits his calm, routine and optimism (he had the camp pack up each morning in case "the Boss" returned that day) with keeping them all alive. When Shackleton died at South Georgia in January 1922, Wild took command of *Quest* and finished the voyage. He was steady, modest and much trusted, Shackleton's right-hand man. His later life in South Africa was hard: failed farming, drink, and poorly paid work.
- **Death:** Died August 19, 1939, at Klerksdorp, Transvaal, South Africa, aged 66, of pneumonia and diabetes. His ashes were later found in a vault at Braamfontein Cemetery, Johannesburg, and interred at Grytviken, South Georgia, on November 27, 2011, beside Shackleton's grave.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (The real Point Wild on Elephant Island is named for him; it does not appear in the glossary.)

### Tom Crean (1877-1938)
- **Life and origin:** Born about February 16 (or July 20), 1877, at Gurtuchrane near Annascaul, County Kerry, Ireland, to a farming family. He joined the Royal Navy at 15.
- **Antarctic role:** Served on three expeditions: Scott's *Discovery* (1901-1904), Scott's *Terra Nova* (1910-1913) and Shackleton's *Endurance* (1914-1917). On *Terra Nova* he was in the last supporting party sent back from the Beardmore plateau. When Lieutenant Edward Evans collapsed with scurvy on the Ross Ice Shelf in February 1912, Crean walked about 35 statute miles (56 km) alone in roughly 18 hours to Hut Point to bring help; he and William Lashly received the Albert Medal for saving Evans. He was in the party that found Scott's tent in November 1912. On *Endurance* he sailed in the *James Caird* to South Georgia and crossed the island's mountains with Shackleton and Worsley.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Strong, of great endurance and cheerful under strain, valued by both Scott and Shackleton. He spent more time in the Antarctic than Scott or Shackleton and outlived both. After retiring from the Navy in 1920 he opened the South Pole Inn at Annascaul and rarely spoke of his exploits.
- **Death:** Died July 27, 1938, at the Bon Secours Hospital, Cork, aged 61, after his appendix burst and treatment came too late.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Frank Worsley (1872-1943)
- **Life and origin:** Frank Arthur Worsley, born February 22, 1872, at Akaroa, New Zealand, son of a farmer. He went to sea with the New Zealand Shipping Company in 1888 and later joined the Royal Naval Reserve.
- **Antarctic role:** Captain of *Endurance* on Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914-1917). After the ship was crushed in the Weddell Sea, he navigated the 22.5-foot boat *James Caird* about 800 nautical miles from Elephant Island to South Georgia in April-May 1916, working from only a few sextant sights in storm conditions, and then crossed South Georgia's unmapped mountains with Shackleton and Crean. He later commanded the vessel that rescued the Elephant Island party and was master of *Quest* (1921-1922).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Ebullient, restless and an exceptional small-boat navigator. In the First World War he commanded the anti-submarine patrol boat *PC.61*, which rammed and sank the German submarine *UC-33* in September 1917, and he served in North Russia; he was awarded the DSO twice. He co-led an Arctic expedition in 1925 and joined a treasure hunt in the 1930s. In 1941 he understated his age to get back to sea.
- **Death:** Died February 1, 1943, at Claygate, Surrey, England, aged 70, of lung cancer; his ashes were scattered at the mouth of the Thames near the Nore lightship.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Edward Wilson (1872-1912)
- **Life and origin:** Edward Adrian Wilson, born July 23, 1872, at Cheltenham, Gloucestershire, England, son of a physician. He studied natural sciences at Cambridge and medicine at St George's Hospital, London, qualifying in 1900, and was an accomplished ornithologist and watercolor artist.
- **Antarctic role:** Junior surgeon and zoologist on Scott's *Discovery* expedition (1901-1904), and one of the three-man southern journey with Scott and Shackleton that reached 82°17′S in December 1902. Chief of scientific staff on the *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-1913). He led the Winter Journey of June-August 1911 with Bowers and Cherry-Garrard to the emperor penguin colony at Cape Crozier in the polar night, to collect eggs for embryological study. He was one of the five-man polar party that reached the South Pole on January 17, 1912.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** His paintings and sketches are a major visual record of early Antarctica. Expedition members called him "Uncle Bill"; he was a devout Christian, a peacemaker and confidant to the whole party, and the man Scott trusted most. On the return march he kept up his geological collecting, and the party hauled about 35 lb (16 kg) of rock specimens to the end.
- **Death:** Died on the Ross Ice Shelf, probably on or about March 29, 1912, in the tent with Scott and Bowers, of cold and starvation, about 11 miles (18 km) from One Ton Depot. The search party found the tent on November 12, 1912, and left the bodies where they lay under a snow cairn.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Lawrence Oates (1880-1912)
- **Life and origin:** Lawrence Edward Grace Oates, born March 17, 1880, in Putney, London, England, to a wealthy family with roots in Leeds; the family later lived at Gestingthorpe Hall, Essex. An officer of the 6th (Inniskilling) Dragoons, he was badly wounded in the thigh in the Second Boer War in 1901.
- **Antarctic role:** Joined Scott's *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-1913), contributing £1,000, and was put in charge of the ponies. Scott chose him for the five-man polar party that reached the South Pole on January 17, 1912.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Taciturn, blunt and dry-humored (nicknamed "Titus" and "the Soldier"); he disagreed openly with Scott about the quality of the ponies and the expedition's management. On the return his feet and hands became badly frostbitten and his old war wound reportedly reopened from scurvy. Believing he was slowing his companions, he left the tent in a blizzard on or about March 17, 1912, his 32nd birthday, saying, by Scott's diary, "I am just going outside and may be some time."
- **Death:** Walked out to his death on the Ross Ice Shelf on or about March 17, 1912; his body was never found. The search party built a cairn and cross near the spot, inscribed "Hereabouts died a very gallant gentleman."
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Edgar Evans (1876-1912)
- **Life and origin:** Born March 7, 1876, at Middleton near Rhossili on the Gower peninsula, Wales. He joined the Royal Navy in 1891 and met Scott while serving in HMS *Majestic*.
- **Antarctic role:** A petty officer on Scott's *Discovery* expedition (1901-1904), where he was one of the three-man party (with Scott and William Lashly) that made the 1903 western journey onto the Victoria Land plateau. On the *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-1913) he was chosen for the five-man polar party that reached the South Pole on January 17, 1912, the only rating among them.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A big, powerful man who looked after the sledging equipment, valued by Scott for strength and practical skill. Before the pole he cut his hand badly while working on the sledges, and the wound would not heal. On the return he deteriorated after falls on the Beardmore Glacier. Some later writers blamed him for slowing the party; Welsh biographers and others have since defended his record.
- **Death:** The first of the polar party to die, on February 17, 1912, at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, after collapsing; a brain injury from a fall, together with scurvy, cold and starvation, are the usual explanations. His body was left on the glacier and never recovered.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Canon's Cape Evans hut site on Ross Island is named for a different man, Lieutenant Edward Evans.)

### Henry Bowers (1883-1912) (swapped in)
- **Life and origin:** Henry Robertson Bowers, born July 29, 1883, at Greenock, Scotland, the only son of a master mariner who died when he was a small child. He trained as a cadet in HMS *Worcester* from age 14, served in merchant sail, and then became a lieutenant in the Royal Indian Marine.
- **Antarctic role:** Joined Scott's *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-1913) on Sir Clements Markham's recommendation, as stores officer in the ship, and was moved to the shore party for his energy and competence. He went with Wilson and Cherry-Garrard on the 1911 Winter Journey to Cape Crozier. At the start of January 1912, on the polar plateau, Scott added him to the polar party as its fifth man, although he had left his skis at a depot, and he walked to the pole on foot while the others skied. He was the party's navigator and took its photographs at the South Pole on January 17-18, 1912; he first spotted Amundsen's marker flag.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Short (about 5 ft 4 in), with a large beak-like nose that gave him the nickname "Birdie," and remarkable stamina and resistance to cold. Cheerful, deeply religious and meticulous with stores and records; Scott and Cherry-Garrard both wrote of him with admiration.
- **Death:** Died on the Ross Ice Shelf, probably on or about March 29, 1912, aged 28, in the tent with Scott and Wilson, of cold and starvation about 11 miles from One Ton Depot; the bodies were found on November 12, 1912, and left under a snow cairn.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Apsley Cherry-Garrard (1886-1959)
- **Life and origin:** Apsley George Benet Cherry-Garrard, born January 2, 1886, at Bedford, England, heir to a landed family. He studied classics and modern history at Oxford and was badly short-sighted.
- **Antarctic role:** Joined Scott's *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-1913) as assistant zoologist after contributing £1,000. He went with Wilson and Bowers on the 1911 Winter Journey to Cape Crozier for emperor penguin eggs, in temperatures near -60°C, and brought three eggs back. In the polar season he hauled as far as the top of the Beardmore Glacier with a supporting party. In March 1912 he took the dog teams to One Ton Depot to meet the returning polar party, waited there about a week and turned back; Scott, Wilson and Bowers later died about 11 miles south of that depot. He was in the search party that found their tent in November 1912.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He wrote *The Worst Journey in the World* (1922), with encouragement from George Bernard Shaw; it is widely held to be the finest book of Antarctic exploration. Gentle and self-critical, he was haunted for the rest of his life by whether he could have saved the polar party, and he suffered long bouts of depression and illness. In the First World War he commanded armored cars in Flanders until he was invalided out.
- **Death:** Died May 18, 1959, in London, aged 73, after years of poor health.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Hubert Wilkins (1888-1958)
- **Life and origin:** George Hubert Wilkins, born October 31, 1888, at Mount Bryan East, South Australia, the youngest of 13 children of a sheep farmer. He became a newsreel cameraman and, in the First World War, an official Australian war photographer (Military Cross and bar).
- **Antarctic role:** Naturalist on Shackleton's *Quest* expedition (1921-1922). Leading the Wilkins-Hearst Antarctic Expedition (1928-1930), he made the first powered flight in Antarctica on November 16, 1928, from Deception Island, with Carl Ben Eielson piloting a Lockheed Vega. On December 20, 1928, they flew about 1,300 miles (2,100 km) south along the Antarctic Peninsula and back, the first long exploratory flight over the continent. He later managed Lincoln Ellsworth's Antarctic expeditions (1933-1939).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Before Antarctica, he and Eielson made the first airplane crossing of the Arctic Ocean (April 1928), for which he was knighted. In 1931 he took the submarine *Nautilus* under the Arctic ice. Restless, inventive and self-effacing. Some of his 1928 aerial "discoveries" were wrong: he reported channels cutting the peninsula into islands, which later ground surveys disproved.
- **Death:** Died suddenly on November 30, 1958, in a hotel room at Framingham, Massachusetts, aged 70. In March 1959 the U.S. Navy submarine USS *Skate* surfaced at the North Pole and scattered his ashes there.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (In reality Australia's Wilkins Aerodrome, serving Casey Station, is named for him; it does not appear in the glossary.)

### Lincoln Ellsworth (1880-1951)
- **Life and origin:** Born May 12, 1880, in Chicago, Illinois, United States, son of the wealthy coal-mine owner James Ellsworth. He trained as a civil engineer and surveyor.
- **Antarctic role:** Financed and led four Antarctic expeditions (1933-1939) with the ship *Wyatt Earp*. On November 23 to December 5, 1935, with the Canadian pilot Herbert Hollick-Kenyon, he made the first trans-Antarctic flight in the Northrop Gamma *Polar Star*, from Dundee Island off the peninsula to the Ross Ice Shelf, about 2,200 miles (3,500 km) with several landings to wait out weather. Out of fuel about 25 miles short of Byrd's Little America, they walked the rest and waited there until the British research ship *Discovery II* reached them in January 1936. On the flight he discovered the Ellsworth Mountains and named the interior plateau he crossed James W. Ellsworth Land, for his father (now Ellsworth Land). In 1939 he claimed about 80,000 square miles of East Antarctica for the United States.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Before Antarctica he financed and joined Amundsen's 1925 flying-boat attempt on the North Pole and the 1926 *Norge* airship flight over the pole. A shy, wealthy amateur with a romantic streak who admired the frontier marshal Wyatt Earp, he relied on professionals (Wilkins, Balchen, Hollick-Kenyon) for the work he paid for.
- **Death:** Died May 26, 1951, in New York City, aged 71, after a heart attack.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Lars Christensen (1884-1965)
- **Life and origin:** Born April 6, 1884, at Sandar (now Sandefjord), Vestfold, Norway, son of the whaling and shipping pioneer Christen Christensen. He became one of Norway's leading whaling shipowners.
- **Antarctic role:** Financed nine expeditions to Antarctic waters from 1926/27 to 1937 (the *Odd I*, *Norvegia* and *Thorshavn* voyages), partly to find new whaling grounds. They claimed Bouvet Island (1927) and Peter I Island (1929) for Norway and discovered and surveyed long stretches of coast now called the Lars Christensen Coast and the Ingrid Christensen Coast (after his wife). On his 1936-1937 voyage, seaplane flights took some 2,200 aerial photographs of the coast, the basis of the maps behind Norway's 1939 claim to Queen Maud Land. His ships also carried the first women known to see Antarctica (his wife Ingrid and Mathilde Wegger, 1931) and to set foot there (Caroline Mikkelsen on an offshore island, 1935; Ingrid on the mainland, 1937).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A pragmatic businessman who publicly warned about overwhaling and argued for regulation, yet was criticized for putting commercial interest first in practice. He was a generous philanthropist (the Sandefjord Whaling Museum, the Whalers' Monument, help in preserving the *Fram*). In the Second World War he served in Washington as financial counselor to the Norwegian embassy and on the Nortraship council.
- **Death:** Died December 10, 1965, in New York, aged 81 (Store norske leksikon; English Wikipedia gives 1964 in one place).
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Finn Ronne (1899-1980)
- **Life and origin:** Born December 20, 1899, at Horten, Vestfold, Norway, son of Martin Rønne, sailmaker on Amundsen's South Pole expedition. He emigrated to the United States in 1923, became a citizen in 1929, and worked as an engineer for Westinghouse.
- **Antarctic role:** Served on Byrd's second Antarctic expedition (1933-1935) and at East Base, Stonington Island, in Byrd's United States Antarctic Service Expedition (1939-1941), where he and Carl Eklund made a sledge journey of about 1,200 miles. He then led the privately funded Ronne Antarctic Research Expedition (1947-1948) from East Base, which mapped by air and sledge the last unknown stretch of the Weddell Sea coast and named Edith Ronne Land (now the Ronne Ice Shelf) for his wife. In the IGY (1957) he commanded the U.S. Ellsworth Station on the Filchner Ice Shelf.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He was said to have covered about 3,600 miles by ski and dog sled, more than any other explorer. His wife Edith "Jackie" Ronne and Jennie Darlington, wife of the chief pilot, were the first women to winter in Antarctica, on his expedition. He was a driven and demanding leader; accounts of the 1947-1948 winter (Darlington's among them) describe sharp tensions between him and members of the party.
- **Death:** Died January 12, 1980, in Bethesda, Maryland, aged 80; buried at Arlington National Cemetery.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Paul Siple (1908-1968)
- **Life and origin:** Paul Allman Siple, born December 18, 1908, at Montpelier, Ohio, United States; raised in Erie, Pennsylvania. An Eagle Scout (1923, with 59 merit badges), he earned a PhD in geography at Clark University (1939) with a dissertation on how explorers adapt to the Antarctic climate.
- **Antarctic role:** Chosen through a national search as the Boy Scout on Byrd's first expedition (1928-1930), he went on five expeditions under Byrd: both private Byrd expeditions of 1928-1935 (chief biologist on the second), the United States Antarctic Service Expedition (1939-1941, as leader of West Base), Operation Highjump (1946-1947) and Operation Deep Freeze I (1955-1956). In the International Geophysical Year he was the first scientific leader of the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station and led its first winter (1957), the first humans ever to winter at the pole.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** With Charles Passel he measured how fast water froze in wind at Little America in 1941 and produced the wind chill index; Siple coined the term "wind chill." The record shows a steady, practical scientist who spent most of his career as a geographer in Army research; he wrote four books on polar exploration.
- **Death:** Died November 25, 1968, aged 59, at the Army Research Office in Arlington, Virginia, where he worked; he had suffered a stroke in 1966 while serving as U.S. science attaché to Australia and New Zealand.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Real Siple Coast, Siple Island, Mount Siple and the former Siple Station carry his name.)

### Vivian Fuchs (1908-1999)
- **Life and origin:** Vivian Ernest Fuchs, born February 11, 1908, at Freshwater, Isle of Wight, England, to a German father and an English mother. A Cambridge geologist, he went to East Greenland with James Wordie in 1929 and led expeditions in East Africa in the 1930s.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the Falkland Islands Dependencies Survey field base at Stonington Island (1948-1950) and then directed the Survey (later the British Antarctic Survey) from 1950 to 1973. He led the Commonwealth Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1955-1958), which achieved Shackleton's planned crossing: his party left Shackleton Base on the Filchner Ice Shelf on November 24, 1957, reached the South Pole on January 19, 1958, and arrived at Scott Base on March 2, 1958, about 2,160 miles (3,470 km) in 99 days, the first overland crossing of Antarctica.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Nicknamed "Bunny." Methodical and immovable once committed: when the crossing ran late and Hillary publicly urged him to stop at the pole and finish the following season, Fuchs refused and completed it. He used the journey for seismic soundings of the ice thickness. Knighted in 1958; later president of the Royal Geographical Society.
- **Death:** Died November 11, 1999, in Cambridge, England, aged 91.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (His crossing ended at Scott Base, the site of the Tepenian city Scott, which is not named for him.)

### Edmund Hillary (1919-2008)
- **Life and origin:** Born July 20, 1919, in Auckland, New Zealand, and raised at Tuakau; a beekeeper by trade and a mountaineer. With a climbing partner, he made the first ascent of Mount Everest on May 29, 1953, and was knighted.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the New Zealand party of the Commonwealth Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1955-1958). His team built Scott Base on Ross Island, opened January 20, 1957, and pioneered a route up the Skelton Glacier and laid depots toward the pole for Fuchs. He then drove on with modified Ferguson farm tractors and reached the South Pole on January 4, 1958, the first party to reach it overland since Scott in 1912 and the first in motor vehicles. He returned to Antarctica several times, including a 1967 New Zealand expedition he led that made the first ascent of Mount Herschel.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The dash to the pole went beyond his brief and annoyed the expedition's London committee, but it was celebrated in New Zealand and made Scott Base central to New Zealand's Antarctic identity. Plain-spoken, competitive and modest about fame. In 1979 his close friend Peter Mulgrew, who had taken Hillary's place as guide-commentator on an Air New Zealand sightseeing flight, died when Flight 901 crashed into Mount Erebus.
- **Death:** Died January 11, 2008, at Auckland City Hospital, aged 88, of heart failure; New Zealand gave him a state funeral.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Note: he built Scott Base, and the Tepenian city Scott stands on the Scott Base site, though canon names it for Robert Falcon Scott. (The real Hillary Coast does not appear in the glossary.)

### Laurence McKinley Gould (1896-1995)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 22, 1896, at Lacota, Michigan, United States. A geologist trained at the University of Michigan, he did field work in Greenland and Baffin Island in the 1920s.
- **Antarctic role:** Chief scientist and second-in-command of Byrd's first Antarctic expedition (1928-1930). In March 1929 a blizzard wrecked the plane that had flown him, Bernt Balchen and Harold June to the Rockefeller Mountains, and they were marooned about ten days until Byrd flew in to rescue them. From November 4, 1929, he led a 2½-month, roughly 1,500-mile geological dog-sledge journey to the Queen Maud Mountains in support of Byrd's polar flight; his finds of sandstone layers (at Mount Fridtjof Nansen) helped tie Antarctica's geology to the other southern continents. In the IGY (1957-1958) he directed the U.S. Antarctic scientific program, and he was a leading American advocate of the Antarctic Treaty of 1959.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Educator and public speaker as much as explorer: professor at Michigan and Carleton College, president of Carleton (1945-1962), president of the American Association for the Advancement of Science (1964), later on the University of Arizona faculty; he received 26 honorary degrees. His *Cold: The Record of an Antarctic Sledge Journey* (1931) is the classic account.
- **Death:** Died June 21, 1995, in Tucson, Arizona, aged 98.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Real Gould Bay, Gould Coast and Mount Gould, and the research ship *Laurence M. Gould*, carry his name.)

### Phillip Law (1912-2010)
- **Life and origin:** Phillip Garth Law, born April 21, 1912, at Tallangatta, Victoria, Australia. A schoolteacher turned physicist (cosmic-ray research at the University of Melbourne), and an amateur boxer.
- **Antarctic role:** Senior scientific officer of the Australian National Antarctic Research Expeditions (ANARE) from 1947, then director of the Antarctic Division and ANARE from 1949 to 1966. He led the voyages that founded Mawson Station (1954), Australia's first permanent station on the Antarctic continent, and Davis Station (1957); he took over the U.S. Wilkes Station for Australia in 1959 and planned its replacement, Casey (opened 1969). He personally explored over 5,000 km of coast, and his expeditions mapped vast areas by air, tractor and dog team.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Described by his division as "demanding but fair," energetic, persuasive and a gifted publicist for Antarctic science; he built Australia's Antarctic program from very limited resources. Australia's Antarctic presence in the Australian Antarctic Territory rests largely on stations he chose and founded.
- **Death:** Died February 28, 2010, in Melbourne, aged 97. His ashes, with those of his wife Nel, were interred near Mawson Station on June 19, 2011.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: the Tepenian cities Mawson, Davis and Casey all stand on station sites he founded or secured, and his ashes lie at the Mawson site. (The real Law Dome near Casey does not appear in the glossary.)

### William Speirs Bruce (1867-1921) (swapped in)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 1, 1867, in London, England, son of a Scottish physician. He studied medicine in London and then natural science in Edinburgh, where he settled; a naturalist and oceanographer.
- **Antarctic role:** Naturalist on the Dundee whaling expedition of 1892-1893. He organized and led the Scottish National Antarctic Expedition (1902-1904) in the *Scotia*, financed largely by the Coats thread family. In 1903 the party wintered at Laurie Island in the South Orkneys and built the stone hut Omond House as a meteorological station. On February 22, 1904, Bruce handed the station to Argentina, which still runs it as Orcadas, the oldest continuously operating station in Antarctica. In March 1904 the *Scotia* discovered Coats Land on the eastern Weddell Sea coast, and the expedition made extensive ocean soundings and biological collections.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A dedicated scientist ("to lift the veil from the unknown, to add to human knowledge"), described as exceptionally kind, but he grew embittered by the London geographical establishment, which gave his expedition little support or recognition (its members were denied the Polar Medal). He later founded the Scottish Oceanographical Laboratory in Edinburgh and led Spitsbergen ventures that lost money.
- **Death:** Died October 28, 1921, in Edinburgh, aged 54, after years of declining health; by his wish his ashes were scattered in the Southern Ocean off South Georgia (1923, per Wikipedia).
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: canon places the city Belgrano in Coats Land, which his expedition discovered. Orcadas and Laurie Island do not appear in the glossary, though the city Signy is also in the South Orkneys.

### Erich von Drygalski (1865-1949) (swapped in)
- **Life and origin:** Erich Dagobert von Drygalski, born February 9, 1865, at Königsberg, Prussia (now Kaliningrad). A geographer and geophysicist, he led Greenland ice studies for the Berlin Geographical Society in 1891-1893.
- **Antarctic role:** Led the first German Antarctic expedition (1901-1903) in the *Gauss*, part of a coordinated international program with Scott's *Discovery* and Nordenskjöld's expeditions. In February 1902 the ship was beset off a new coast, which he named Kaiser Wilhelm II Land, and stayed frozen in for nearly a year. A sledge party reached and named Gaussberg, an extinct volcano. On March 29, 1902, he went up in a tethered hydrogen balloon to about 500 m, the first balloon ascent in East Antarctica. The expedition also kept a station on Kerguelen.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A careful academic scientist rather than a record-seeker; at home the expedition was judged a disappointment beside the British, but he spent decades editing its results into 20 volumes and two atlases (1905-1931). Professor of geography at Munich from 1906 to 1934.
- **Death:** Died January 10, 1949, in Munich, aged 83.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him as a settlement. The real Drygalski Ice Tongue (named for him by Scott's expedition) appears in canon as part of what keeps Janbogo's port on Terra Nova Bay open.

## Companions of the Saints

Second round, 2026-11-01. The people who shared the existing Saints' journeys or made them possible. Each entry has a "Tie to an existing Saint" line and a "What they left behind" line. The "Tepenian place" line checks the DerivationMechanisms Glossary only; none of these figures has a Tepenian place named for them, and real sites that touch a canon city are noted. **St. Robert: Scott's *Terra Nova* expedition**

### William Lashly (1867-1940)
- **Life and origin:** Born December 25, 1867, at Hambledon, Hampshire, England, the son of a farm worker. He joined the Royal Navy in 1889 and became a stoker; he was leading stoker on HMS *Duke of Wellington* before going south.
- **Antarctic role:** Lead stoker on Scott's *Discovery* Expedition (1901-04). In 1903 he was one of the three men, with Scott and Edgar Evans, of the "Farthest West" journey onto the Polar Plateau of Victoria Land. On the *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-13) he ran the motor sledges until they failed, then man-hauled. On December 25, 1911, his 44th birthday, he fell into a crevasse on the plateau and was hauled out on an Alpine rope. On January 4, 1912, at 87°32′S, he turned back with Lieutenant Edward Evans and Tom Crean as the last supporting party. When Evans collapsed with scurvy about 100 miles from Hut Point, Lashly stayed and nursed him in the tent while Crean walked about 35 miles alone for help.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** St. Robert's chosen man on both his expeditions: with him on the Farthest West journey of 1903, and among the last three men to see him alive on the plateau in January 1912.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Described as quiet, strong, good-natured and dependable, and called "the best man far and away in the ship" (quoted in Wikipedia). He and Crean received the Albert Medal from King George V at Buckingham Palace on July 26, 1913, for saving Evans's life.
- **What they left behind:** His diaries, published in 1969 as *Under Scott's Command: Lashly's Antarctic Diaries*; the Lashly Mountains (named by Scott) and the Lashly Glacier (named by the New Zealand party of the Commonwealth Trans-Antarctic Expedition, 1956-58); a plaque in Cardiff; Lashly Meadow, a housing development in Hambledon; and a tapeworm species named for him by the expedition surgeon Edward Atkinson.
- **Death:** June 12, 1940, aged 72, after retiring in 1932 to Hambledon, where he named his house "Minna Bluff" after the Antarctic landmark. No place or cause given in the sources found. After the navy (World War I service included HMS *Irresistible* and HMS *Amethyst*) he had been a customs officer in Cardiff.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.
- **Sources:**
  - [William Lashly (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lashly)
  - [William Lashly's legacy, William Lashly Antarctic Explorer (family and local history site)](https://sites.google.com/site/lashlyantarcticexplorer/william-lashly-s-legacy)

### Edward "Teddy" Evans (1880-1957)
- **Life and origin:** Edward Ratcliffe Garth Russell Evans, born October 28, 1880, in London, England, the son of a barrister. He was expelled from Merchant Taylors' School for fighting and truancy, did well at a school for "troublesome boys," and trained on the private training ship HMS *Worcester* from 1895, winning a Royal Navy cadetship.
- **Antarctic role:** Second officer of the relief ship *Morning* (1902-04), sent to find Scott's *Discovery*. Second-in-command of the *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-13) and captain of the ship on the voyage south. He led the last supporting party, which turned back on January 4, 1912, at about 87°32′S. On the return he went down with scurvy and snow blindness; on February 13 he ordered Lashly and Crean to leave him, and they refused. Crean's solo walk brought Atkinson out with dogs, and Evans was carried in "near to death." Sent home to recover, he came back in command of *Terra Nova*, reached McMurdo Sound on January 18, 1913, about a year after Scott reached the Pole (January 17, 1912), and learned there of the polar party's deaths. He took the ship home to Cardiff on June 14, 1913.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** St. Robert's second-in-command and the leader of the last party to see him alive. Scott named Cape Evans, the 1911 base, for him. Scott's diary was less kind about him than the public record: "well-meaning, but terribly slow to learn" (quoted in Wikipedia).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Of the refused order he later wrote that it was "the first and last time my orders as a naval officer were disobeyed." His colleague Frank Debenham blamed his scurvy partly on diet: "Teddy really was a very naughty boy and wouldn't eat his seal meat." On the night of April 20-21, 1917, commanding the destroyer HMS *Broke* off Dover, he rammed a German destroyer, which won him the name "Evans of the Broke." He later commanded the Royal Australian Navy's squadron as a rear admiral, served as a liaison to King Haakon VII in Norway in 1940, and was made Baron Mountevans on November 12, 1945, sitting as a Labour peer. His first wife, Hilda, died of peritonitis on the voyage home in April 1913.
- **What they left behind:** Cape Evans on Ross Island, where Scott's 1911 hut still stands; Mount Evans; his book *South with Scott* (1921).
- **Death:** August 20, 1957, at Gålå, Norway, aged 76; buried in the family plot at Vår Frelsers gravlund, Oslo. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him as a city. The "Cape Evans and Cape Royds huts" near Fort McMurdo are project canon (Glossary `C.md`); the Cape Evans hut stands on the cape named for him.
- **Sources:**

  - [Edward Ratcliffe Garth Russell "Teddy" Evans, 1880-1957, Cool Antarctica](https://coolantarctica.com/Antarctica%20fact%20file//History/biography/Evans-Edward-Ratcliffe-Garth-Russell-Teddy.php)

### Edward Atkinson (1881-1929)
- **Life and origin:** Edward Leicester Atkinson, born November 23, 1881, on Saint Vincent in the Windward Islands. He was educated at Forest School, Snaresbrook, and trained in medicine at St Thomas's Hospital, London, where he was the hospital's light-heavyweight boxing champion; he qualified in 1906 and joined the Royal Navy's medical service in 1908.
- **Antarctic role:** Surgeon and parasitologist of the *Terra Nova* expedition. He hauled with the polar journey up the Beardmore Glacier and returned to base on January 29, 1912. In late February 1912 he went out by dog team with Dimitri Gerov and brought in the dying Teddy Evans. As senior officer at Cape Evans in the winter of 1912 he led the base. On October 29, 1912, he led the search party south, and on November 12, 1912, about 11 miles south of One Ton Depot, it found the tent with the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He led the eleven-man party that found St. Robert's tent. He read Scott's diary, as Scott had asked the finder to, then read the burial service from Corinthians and prayers over the tent. The bodies were left in their sleeping bags in the tent, which was covered with a snow cairn topped by a cross. The party searched for Oates, found only his sleeping bag, and was back at Hut Point on November 25.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A capable, steady officer who held the shaken base together through the winter of 1912. A line from the expedition's account of the burial, quoted by Cool Antarctica: "it is a grave which kings must envy." He won the DSO on the Somme in 1916. On September 16, 1918, he was badly burned and partly blinded in the explosion of HMS *Glatton* in the harbor at Dover while carrying men to safety, and received the Albert Medal. After the war he served with the Royal Hellenic Navy and studied the nutritional deficiencies of Scott's sledging rations.
- **What they left behind:** The finding and burial of the polar party, and the recovery of Scott's journals and letters; parasitology collections; Atkinson Cliffs in Victoria Land.
- **Death:** February 20, 1929, suddenly, aboard ship in the Mediterranean, aged 47; buried at sea. He had retired from the navy in 1928 on health grounds. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Tryggve Gran (1888-1980)
- **Life and origin:** Jens Tryggve Herman Gran, born January 20, 1888, in Bergen, Norway, into a shipbuilding family. He graduated from the Royal Norwegian Naval Academy in 1910. Fridtjof Nansen recommended him to Scott as a ski expert.
- **Antarctic role:** Ski instructor of the *Terra Nova* expedition (1910-13) and its youngest member. He took part in depot laying and the western geological journey, was in the November 1912 search party, and climbed Mount Erebus in December 1912. Polar Medal, 1913.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** A Norwegian on St. Robert's expedition during the race with St. Roald. At the tent he set his skis as a cross over the grave, and he skied back to Cape Evans on Scott's own skis, reasoning that at least Scott's skis should finish the journey.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** On July 30, 1914, he became the first pilot to fly across the North Sea, from Cruden Bay, Scotland, to Norway in a Blériot XI monoplane, in 4 hours 10 minutes. In World War I he flew with the Royal Flying Corps under the name "Teddy Grant," made night bombing raids and won the Military Cross; he claimed to have fought Hermann Göring in the air (Canterbury Museum calls this a claim). In World War II he was a member of Vidkun Quisling's Nasjonal Samling; in 1948 he was convicted of treason and sentenced to 18 months, already served in custody. He lectured on polar history into his eighties.
- **What they left behind:** Two expedition diaries and four medals (Polar Medal, Military Cross, Legion of Honor, Order of the Crown of Italy), bought at a London auction for £105,000 by Canterbury Museum, Christchurch; books on the expedition; Mount Gran, the Gran Glacier and Tryggve Granfjellet.
- **Death:** January 8, 1980, at his home in Grimstad, Norway, aged 91. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.
- **Sources:**

**St. Ernest: the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition, 1914-1917**

Context: the Ross Sea party sailed in *Aurora* to lay depots from Ross Island toward the Beardmore Glacier for Shackleton's planned crossing from the Weddell Sea. It did not know that *Endurance* had never landed its party.

### Aeneas Mackintosh (1879-1916)
- **Life and origin:** Aeneas Lionel Acton Mackintosh, born July 1, 1879, in British India, one of six children of Alexander Mackintosh, a Scottish indigo planter, and Annie Mackintosh. He grew up in England and was educated at Bedford Modern School. He went to sea in the merchant navy in 1894, aged 16, and by 1899 held a master's certificate, serving with the P&O line. He married Gladys Campbell in 1912.
- **Antarctic role:** Second officer and navigator of Shackleton's *Nimrod* expedition (1907-1909). On January 31, 1908, he lost his right eye in an accident while stores were being unloaded in McMurdo Sound; it was removed, and he was sent back to New Zealand. He returned south in 1909, and on January 3, 1909, set out across about 25 miles of sea ice to carry the mail to Cape Royds, a journey that nearly killed his party. In 1914 Shackleton gave him command of *Aurora* and the Ross Sea party. They reached Cape Evans on January 16, 1915. On May 6-7, 1915, a gale tore *Aurora* from her moorings and carried her out to sea, leaving ten men ashore with few supplies. In two seasons (January-March 1915, then September 1915-March 1916) the party laid the depots across the Ross Ice Shelf to Mount Hope, at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, reached on January 26, 1916. Mackintosh collapsed with scurvy on the return and was hauled on the sledge for weeks. Joyce and Richards brought Hayward in to Hut Point on March 11, 1916, then went back for Mackintosh, whom Ernest Wild had stayed with; he reached Hut Point on March 16.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** Shackleton's officer on two expeditions and the commander of the depot line St. Ernest's crossing depended on. The crossing never happened: *Endurance* was crushed in the Weddell Sea and never landed a party. Shackleton himself came south on *Aurora* to rescue the survivors in January 1917.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** His leadership is the most debated in the party's story. Ernest Joyce disputed his authority and methods from the first season, called his decision to winter *Aurora* at Cape Evans "the silliest damn rot," and took de facto charge on the way back when Mackintosh was helpless. Shackleton wrote that the men who died "gave their lives for their country as surely as those who gave up their lives in France or Flanders." On the loss of the ship, Mackintosh wrote that they must "conserve and economize on what we have, and seek and apply what substitutes we can gather."
- **What they left behind:** The Mount Hope depot line, laid at great cost and never used. The memorial cross on Wind Vane Hill, Cape Evans, erected by the survivors to Mackintosh, Spencer-Smith and Hayward, to which Shackleton attached a copper tube with their epitaph in 1917; it is Antarctic Historic Site and Monument No. 17. Mount Mackintosh (74°20′S, 162°15′E) is named for him. He received the silver Polar Medal.
- **Death:** On May 8, 1916, despite the misgivings of Joyce and the others at the hut, he and Victor Hayward left Hut Point to walk the roughly 13 miles of new sea ice to Cape Evans. A blizzard rose within the hour, and they were never seen again. They are presumed to have gone through thin ice or been carried out on a breaking floe; searches in 1916 and 1917 found nothing. He was 36.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. He died on the sea ice between Hut Point (in canon, the city of Scott's most sacred civic site) and Cape Evans, whose hut canon lists among "Cape Evans and Cape Royds huts" near Fort McMurdo; the Wind Vane Hill cross stands at Cape Evans.
- **Sources:**
  - [Scott Polar Research Institute, Shackleton biographies, "Mackintosh, Aeneas Lionel Acton"](https://www.spri.cam.ac.uk/museum/shackleton/biographies/Mackintosh,_Aeneas_Lionel_Acton/)
  - [Wikipedia, "Ross Sea party"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Sea_party)
  - [Wikipedia, "Historic Sites and Monuments in Antarctica" (HSM 17)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_Sites_and_Monuments_in_Antarctica)

### Ernest Joyce (1875-1940)
- **Life and origin:** Ernest Edward Mills Joyce, born December 22, 1875, in Sussex, England (SPRI gives Bognor; Wikipedia gives Felpham, beside Bognor), and educated at the Greenwich Royal Hospital School, a school for the sons of seamen. He joined the Royal Navy as a boy in 1891, aged 15, rose to petty officer first class, and served with the Naval Brigade in the Boer War.
- **Antarctic role:** Able seaman on Scott's *Discovery* expedition (1901-1904), which he joined from HMS *Gibraltar* at Cape Town in October 1901 (SPRI gives October 14) as one of four seamen chosen when Scott asked for volunteers, sledging and coming home a petty officer. On Shackleton's *Nimrod* expedition (1907-1909) he ran the dogs, sledges, stores and zoological collection and led the depot-laying journeys south from September 1908; he also worked on *Aurora Australis*, the first book printed in Antarctica. For Mawson's Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911) he chose the sledge dogs in Denmark. On the Ross Sea party (1914-1917) he was in charge of sledging and the dogs, and on the second season's return, when Mackintosh and Spencer-Smith could no longer walk, he took de facto command. In a blizzard on February 17-22, 1916, ten miles short of the Bluff depot, he, Richard Richards and Victor Hayward went on through the storm for food while Ernest Wild cared for the invalids; Joyce wrote, "I have never known such shocking conditions." He was rescued at Cape Evans on January 10, 1917.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He served on three expeditions of the existing Saints: under St. Robert (*Discovery*), under St. Ernest twice (*Nimrod* and the Ross Sea party), and he chose the dogs for St. Douglas's expedition, the dogs that pulled Mawson, Ninnis and Mertz east. He brought the survivors of the depot line for St. Ernest's crossing back to Hut Point.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Shackleton thought well of him on *Nimrod* ("Joyce knows his work well"). The record credits him with saving lives on the second depot journey, and he received the Albert Medal on July 4, 1923, with Richards (and posthumously Wild and Hayward). Opinion of him is mixed: he quarreled with Mackintosh throughout, and the historian Roland Huntford called him "a strange mixture of fraud, flamboyance and ability." His published diaries have been called unreliable and in places invented, "a self-aggrandizing epic" (Wikipedia): he styled himself "Captain" afterward, claimed to have seen Scott's death tent, and wrote anonymously to newspapers praising himself. Even so, colleagues granted his skill and courage in the field, and Lord Shackleton (Edward, the explorer's son) named him as one of the heroes of the Ross Sea party story.
- **What they left behind:** *The South Polar Trail* (1929), built on his expedition diaries. The Polar Medal with four clasps, which SPRI says only he and Frank Wild received. Joyce Glacier, Lake Joyce and Mount Joyce are named for him.
- **Death:** Died May 2, 1940, in London, aged 64, after years of working as a hotel porter. Wikipedia gives natural causes. He had married Beatrice Curtlett of Christchurch, New Zealand.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. He knew Hut Point (where *Discovery* wintered and the Ross Sea survivors sheltered in 1916), Cape Royds and Cape Evans, all canon sites near Fort McMurdo and the city of Scott.
- **Sources:**
  - [Scott Polar Research Institute, Shackleton biographies, "Joyce, Ernest Edward Mills"](https://www.spri.cam.ac.uk/museum/shackleton/biographies/Joyce,_Ernest_Edward_Mills/)
  - [Wikipedia, "Ross Sea party"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Sea_party)
  - [Wikipedia, "Ernest Joyce"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Joyce)

### Arnold Spencer-Smith (1883-1916)
- **Life and origin:** Arnold Patrick Spencer-Smith, born March 17, 1883, at Streatham, Surrey (now London), England. He attended Westminster City School (SPRI adds Woodbridge School; Wikipedia adds King's College London) and read history at Queens' College, Cambridge (1903-1906). He was ordained deacon in the Scottish Episcopal Church in 1910 at St Mary's Cathedral, Edinburgh, and was a schoolmaster at Merchiston Castle School near Edinburgh; he was ordained priest by the Bishop of Edinburgh before sailing. He was elected a fellow of the Royal Historical Society in November 1913. His sister Joan became a notable Anglican deaconess in New Zealand.
- **Antarctic role:** Chaplain and photographer of the Ross Sea party (1914-1917). One account says he had wanted to enlist in 1914 but as a clergyman was barred from combat. He was the first priest to land in Antarctica and the first to celebrate the Eucharist there (the date was not found in the sources checked). In the second season he first stayed at Cape Evans taking weather readings, then joined the sledging on January 1, 1916, after a stove failure changed the plan. He broke down with scurvy near 83°S, short of Mount Hope, and at his own suggestion was left alone in a tent while the others laid the last depot. Picked up on January 29, helpless, he was hauled north on the sledge for about six weeks.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He died laying the depots for St. Ernest's crossing, which never came. Shackleton's tribute to the dead of the Ross Sea party, that they "gave their lives for their country as surely as those who gave up their lives in France or Flanders," covers him.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as the expedition's priest and for his long, helpless decline on the sledge; his diaries survive at Canterbury Museum, Christchurch, and at SPRI. Julian Dowdeswell, director of SPRI, said in Dunedin that "Arnold Spencer-Smith's sacrifice... remains a powerful lesson to us all, over 100 years later."
- **What they left behind:** His chalice and paten, given to his cousin Canon Arthur Gaze, vicar of All Saints' Church, Dunedin, New Zealand (1964-1980), are still used there at services. All Saints' commissioned a stained-glass window in his memory, designed by Jenna Packer and made by Peter Mackenzie, endorsed by his family, the New Zealand Antarctic Heritage Trust and SPRI. He is named on the Ross Sea party's memorial cross on Wind Vane Hill, Cape Evans (HSM 17). Cape Spencer-Smith on White Island (78°00′S, 167°27′E) is named for him. He received the silver Polar Medal posthumously.
- **Death:** Died March 9, 1916, aged 32, of scurvy and exhaustion, in the tent on the Ross Ice Shelf during the return toward Hut Point. He was buried in the ice shelf where he died. Note: some sources call him the "first to die and be buried" in Antarctica; that holds only for clergy, since Nicolai Hanson was buried at Cape Adare in 1899.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. The Wind Vane Hill cross that names him stands at Cape Evans, a canon heritage site near Fort McMurdo.
- **Sources:**

### Victor Hayward (1887-1916)
- **Life and origin:** Victor George Hayward, born October 23, 1887, at Harlesden, north London, England, the 12th of 14 children of Francis Checkley Hayward and Mary Jane Fairchild. He was educated at an Essex boarding school and a London business college and worked as an accounts clerk in the City of London. He spent about seven months on a ranch in northern Canada. (SPRI lists his nationality as Canadian; the fuller accounts make him a Londoner who had worked in Canada.)
- **Antarctic role:** A general hand of the Ross Sea party (1914-1917), taken on after writing to Shackleton's office offering to "do anything"; the Canadian ranch work counted in his favor. After *Aurora* was blown away in May 1915 he became one of the party's best seal hunters. He was in the six-man party on the final leg toward Mount Hope, which the fit men reached on January 26, 1916. In the February 1916 blizzard he went with Joyce and Richards to fetch food from the Bluff depot, then broke down with scurvy himself and was hauled on the sledge for the last stretch to Hut Point.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He died in the service of St. Ernest's depot line. He was recruited by Shackleton's office, and Shackleton's own rescue voyage of January 1917 came too late for him.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** An eager amateur rather than a professional explorer, recruited for willingness. His diary (more than 24,000 words, with sketches) was written mostly as letters to his fiancée, and the entries shrink to bare distances as the scurvy took hold. He was awarded the Albert Medal posthumously on July 4, 1923, for his efforts to save his disabled companions on the ice-shelf journey.
- **What they left behind:** His diary. He is named on the memorial cross on Wind Vane Hill, Cape Evans (HSM 17). The posthumous Albert Medal and the silver Polar Medal.
- **Death:** On May 8, 1916, aged 28, he set out with Aeneas Mackintosh at about 1:00 p.m. from Hut Point to walk across the new sea ice of McMurdo Sound to Cape Evans. A blizzard rose within two hours, and neither man was seen again; their bodies were never found. They probably went through the ice or were carried off on a breaking floe.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. He died on the sea ice between Hut Point and Cape Evans, both canon sites near Fort McMurdo and the city of Scott.

  - [Wikipedia, "Victor Hayward"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hayward)

### Ernest Wild (1879-1918)
- **Life and origin:** Henry Ernest Wild, born August 10, 1879, at Stickford, Lincolnshire, England (per the Bedfordshire archives; Wikipedia gives the date without a place), the sixth of thirteen children and second son of the schoolteacher Benjamin Wild; Frank Wild was his elder brother. The family came from Skelton, North Yorkshire, and in January 1885 moved to the School House at Eversholt, Bedfordshire, where his father was headmaster. He joined the Royal Navy in 1894 and served twenty years before the expedition.
- **Antarctic role:** Petty officer and general hand of the Ross Sea party (1914-1917). He lost a toe and damaged an ear to frostbite on the 1915 depot journey. After *Aurora* broke away on May 6-7, 1915, he and Joyce improvised gear and supplies from the stores of the old huts; his tobacco substitute of tea, coffee, sawdust, grass and dried herbs was known as "Hut Point Mixture." In the second season he was in the six-man party on the final leg to Mount Hope (reached January 26, 1916). In the February 1916 blizzard he stayed alone with the invalids Mackintosh and Spencer-Smith while the others went for food, and he nursed them on the march back to Hut Point.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He served St. Ernest's plan on the Ross Sea side of the continent while his brother Frank, Shackleton's second-in-command, was beset with *Endurance* on the Weddell Sea side; neither knew how the other's half was going. The depots he helped lay were for Shackleton's crossing party, which never came.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Mackintosh called him a "popular, cheerful, willing soul"; Alexander Stevens, the party's chief scientist, called him "consistent... long-suffering, patient, industrious, good-humoured, unswervingly loyal." He was unassuming and never published an account. He was credited with saving two lives and was awarded the Albert Medal posthumously on July 4, 1923, with Joyce, Richards and Hayward.
- **What they left behind:** His expedition papers (the Henry Ernest Wild collection, 1914-1916, at SPRI). The Albert Medal and the Polar Medal.
- **Death:** After the rescue he returned to the Royal Navy and served in the Mediterranean as a chief petty officer on the minesweeper HMS *Biarritz*. He died of typhoid on March 10, 1918, aged 38, at the Royal Naval Hospital, Malta, and is buried in the naval cemetery at Kalkara (Capuccini Naval Cemetery), Malta. (One online record places his death at sea off Malta; the archive and Wikipedia accounts say the hospital.)
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. His "Hut Point Mixture" is named for Hut Point, the city of Scott's most sacred civic site in canon.
- **Sources:**
  - [Bedfordshire Archives, Eversholt people, "Ernest Wild"](https://virtual-library.culturalservices.net/bedfordshire/vlib/0.digitised_resources/eversholt_digitisation_people_ernest_wild_stewart.htm)
  - [Wikipedia, "Ernest Wild"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Wild)
  - [Wikipedia, "Ross Sea party"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Sea_party)

### Harry McNish (1874-1930)
- **Life and origin:** Henry "Harry" McNish, born September 11, 1874, at Port Glasgow, Scotland, the third of eleven children of a journeyman shoemaker. His name also appears as McNeish (Shackleton's spelling) and MacNish; McNish is taken as correct. A ship's carpenter, he married three times. He was known on *Endurance* as "Chippy," the usual nickname for a ship's carpenter.
- **Antarctic role:** Carpenter of *Endurance* on Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914-1917). He built the men's quarters in the hold while the ship was beset, including "The Ritz," and a cofferdam against the leaks as the ice crushed her. On the ice he raised and decked the 22-foot boat *James Caird* for the ocean crossing: he strengthened her keel with the mast of the *Stancomb Wills*, built a frame of packing-case wood and sledge runners covered in canvas, and caulked with seal blood, flour, lamp wick and oil paint. He was one of the six who sailed her about 800 nautical miles from Elephant Island to South Georgia (April 24 to May 10, 1916). At King Haakon Bay he took screws from the boat to make cleats for the boots of Shackleton, Worsley and Crean for their crossing of the island, and stayed at Peggotty Camp with John Vincent and Timothy McCarthy until they were collected.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He built the boat on which St. Ernest's rescue of his men depended, and sailed in it with him. His relationship with Shackleton was hostile: Shackleton had his cat shot when the ship was lost, and later kept him from the Polar Medal.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Frank Worsley called him a "splendid shipwright." On December 27, 1915, while the men hauled boats across the ice, he refused duty, arguing that his obligations ended with the loss of the ship. Shackleton read the ship's articles to the crew, which bound them to obey on shore as well as afloat, and McNish went back to work. Shackleton later left McNish, Vincent, William Stephenson and Ernest Holness off the Polar Medal list. Alexander Macklin called McNish's exclusion "a grave injustice." His cat, Mrs Chippy (a tom despite the name), was shot on Shackleton's orders after the ship was lost in late 1915, since the party could not feed or carry it, and he is said never to have forgiven him.
- **What they left behind:** His work on the *James Caird*, which survives at Dulwich College, London. McNish Island (first named McNeish Island, 1958) in South Georgia. His grave at Karori Cemetery, Wellington, New Zealand, unmarked until the New Zealand Antarctic Society put up a headstone in 1959; in 2004 the society added a life-size bronze of Mrs Chippy by Chris Elliott, paid for by public subscription. A plaque at Port Glasgow Library (2006).
- **Death:** After about 23 years in the merchant navy, he moved to New Zealand in 1925 and worked on the Wellington waterfront until an injury ended his work. Destitute, he slept in wharf sheds and lived on collections from the dockworkers, then entered the Ohiro Benevolent Home. He died on September 24, 1930, at Wellington Hospital, aged 56; the cause is not given in the sources checked. He was buried at Karori with full naval honors, with a firing party from HMS *Dunedin*.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. The *James Caird* voyage is the "open-boat journey" that canon's St. Ernest's Passage and the proposed Endurance Span at Signy commemorate.
- **Sources:**

  - [*Taranaki Daily News*, "Naval honours at funeral," September 26, 1930 (Papers Past)](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/TDN19300926.2.46)

### Luis Pardo (1882-1935)
- **Life and origin:** Luis Alberto Pardo Villalón, born September 20, 1882, in Santiago, Chile. He entered the Chilean Naval Pilots' School in July 1900, joined the Chilean Navy as a pilot third class in June 1906, and was promoted pilot second class in September 1910. He was posted to the Magallanes naval base at Punta Arenas as captain of the *Yelcho*.
- **Antarctic role:** Commander of the rescue of the Elephant Island party in 1916. Shackleton had already failed three times to reach the 22 men left under Frank Wild: in the *Southern Sky* (May 23-31), the Uruguayan *Instituto de Pesca No. 1* (June 10-16) and the *Emma* (July 12-August 8). The Chilean government then lent the *Yelcho*, a 36.5-meter steam tug with no ice-strengthening, double hull, heating or proper radio. Pardo sailed from Punta Arenas on August 25, 1916, with Shackleton, Worsley and Crean aboard, reached Point Wild around 11 a.m. on August 30, took off all 22 men and was back in Punta Arenas on September 3.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He completed St. Ernest's rescue of his own men, the voyage that let Shackleton bring back every member of the *Endurance* party alive. Shackleton sailed with him and fetched the men from the shore.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Chilean accounts stress his calm, sober command of an unsuitable ship in winter ice. He was promoted pilot first class at once, received civil and naval honors and was credited with ten extra years of service. He declined a large money reward offered from Britain. The amount and his words differ by source: the Anglo-Chilean Society gives £25,000 and the quote "I did nothing more than fulfill my duty as a Chilean sailor"; Wikipedia gives no figure and paraphrases him as saying he was simply fulfilling a mission assigned by the Chilean Navy. The £25,000 figure was not confirmed in an official source in this run. He retired from the navy in 1919 and was Chilean consul in Liverpool from 1930 to 1934.
- **What they left behind:** The rescue itself, a fixed point of Chilean Antarctic history and of the Shackleton story. Pardo Ridge, the highest point of Elephant Island, and Cape Yelcho at its northern tip. A bust of Pardo at Point Wild, Elephant Island. The *Yelcho*'s bow is preserved at Puerto Williams, and a statue of Pardo stands on the Punta Arenas waterfront beside a *Yelcho* prow (Wikipedia names both). Two Chilean Navy ships named *Piloto Pardo* (an Antarctic supply ship of 1959 and an offshore patrol vessel of 2008), and a memorial statue and plaque at the International Maritime Organization in London.
- **Death:** Died February 21, 1935, in Santiago, Chile, aged 52, of bronchopneumonia.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Canon's St. Ernest's Passage at Signy honors the open-boat journey whose end the *Yelcho* rescue completed; it does not name Pardo.)
- **Sources:**
  - [Anglo-Chilean Society, "Piloto Pardo: 109 years since the heroic rescue of Shackleton's crew"](https://www.anglochileansociety.org/single-post/piloto-pardo-109-years-since-the-heroic-rescue-of-shackleton-s-crew)
  - [BioBioChile, "Efemérides: El 21 de febrero de 1935 falleció el piloto Luis Pardo"](https://www.biobiochile.cl/biobiotv/programas/efemerides/2023/02/21/efemerides-el-21-de-febrero-de-1935-fallecio-el-piloto-luis-pardo.shtml)
  - [Wikipedia, "Luis Pardo"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Pardo)

**St. Roald: the *Belgica*, his first Antarctic voyage**

Context: Adrien de Gerlache's Belgian Antarctic Expedition (1897-99) was beset in the Bellingshausen Sea from about February 28, 1898, and became the first expedition to winter in the Antarctic. The polar night lasted from May 17 to July 23, 1898. The ship broke free on February 15, 1899, and cleared the pack on March 14, 1899. Amundsen served as first mate. Earlier, in the Gerlache Strait, the expedition named Anvers Island (the site of Palmer City), Wiencke Island and the Danco Coast.

### Frederick Cook (1865-1940)
- **Life and origin:** Frederick Albert Cook, born June 10, 1865, at Hortonville, Sullivan County, New York, to German immigrant parents, Theodor and Magdalena Koch. He took his medical degree in 1890 at the University of the City of New York (now NYU) and was surgeon on Robert Peary's North Greenland expedition of 1891-92.
- **Antarctic role:** Surgeon, anthropologist and photographer of the *Belgica*. He read in the *New York Sun* in August 1897 that the expedition needed a doctor and joined the ship at Rio de Janeiro. When scurvy appeared in the winter of 1898, he put the men on fresh seal and penguin meat, which he had seen keep the Greenland Inuit healthy. When de Gerlache and Captain Georges Lecointe were both laid low, he and Amundsen in effect ran the ship. On December 31, 1898, he proposed sawing and blasting a channel through the ice; the crew worked at it for about a month before the ship moved on February 15, 1899.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He and St. Roald formed a lasting bond on the *Belgica*. Amundsen stood by him through all his later disgrace and visited him at least once in prison (Ohio State University's Cook archive; Wikipedia).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Widely credited with saving most of the crew. His account, *Through the First Antarctic Night* (1900), records the strain of the winter: "We are imprisoned in an endless sea of ice" (March 1898, as quoted by Cool Antarctica). His later fame turned to scandal. His 1906 claim to the first ascent of Denali (Mount McKinley) is judged false: his "summit" photograph was taken on an outcrop about 19 miles away. His claim to have reached the North Pole on April 21, 1908, was rejected by a University of Copenhagen commission for lack of proof. In 1923 he was convicted on 14 counts of mail fraud over oil-company promotions and sentenced to 14 years and 9 months; he was held at Leavenworth and paroled in 1930.
- **What they left behind:** The medical record of the first Antarctic wintering and its scurvy cure; his photographs and book of the *Belgica* voyage; his papers, held at Ohio State University.
- **Death:** August 5, 1940, at New Rochelle, New York, of a cerebral hemorrhage, a few months after President Franklin D. Roosevelt pardoned him; aged 75. Buried at Forest Lawn Cemetery, Buffalo, New York.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Henryk Arctowski (1871-1958)
- **Life and origin:** Born July 15, 1871, in Warsaw, in Russian-ruled Poland. He studied mathematics, physics and astronomy at the University of Liège and chemistry and geology at the Sorbonne, Paris.
- **Antarctic role:** Geologist, oceanographer and meteorologist of the *Belgica*, and coordinator of its physical science. Through the year-long drift he kept hourly weather observations, with his Polish assistant Antoni Dobrowolski; the series is usually described as the first year-round weather record from the Antarctic.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** A shipmate of St. Roald, then first mate, through the first Antarctic winter. He wrote the record's best-known line on Émile Danco's burial (see below).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A broad, cosmopolitan scientist. He held a science post at the New York Public Library (1910-20), was professor of geophysics and meteorology at Jan Kazimierz University in Lwów (1920-39), and was a research associate at the Smithsonian Institution (1939-50). He was in the United States for a geophysics congress in 1939 when Germany and the Soviet Union invaded Poland, and he never went home. The Wikipedia article on his station credits him with an early form of the wind-chill idea; the usual credit for the wind-chill index goes to Paul Siple and Charles Passel (1945), so treat that claim as unconfirmed.
- **What they left behind:** The *Belgica*'s meteorological and oceanographic results. Poland's Henryk Arctowski Polish Antarctic Station at Admiralty Bay, King George Island, opened February 26, 1977, and is run by the Polish Academy of Sciences. The Arctowski Peninsula and the U.S. National Academy of Sciences' Arctowski Medal (for solar physics) also bear his name.
- **Death:** February 21, 1958, in Bethesda, Maryland (Wikipedia) or Washington, D.C. (Cool Antarctica), aged 86. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Note: his station stands on King George Island, where canon places Contrapunto (formerly Sejong; Glossary `C.md`) and the island's other national communities; the station itself does not appear in the glossary.

### Emil Racoviță (1868-1947)
- **Life and origin:** Born November 15, 1868, in Iași, Romania. He studied natural science in Paris, taking a degree in 1891 and a doctorate in 1896 under the zoologist Henri de Lacaze-Duthiers.
- **Antarctic role:** Naturalist (zoologist and botanist) of the *Belgica*. He collected plants and animals in the Gerlache Strait and through the drift south of the Antarctic Circle, including the type specimens of the wingless midge *Belgica antarctica*; Wikipedia also credits him with the first Antarctic flowering plants collected.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** A shipmate of St. Roald through the first Antarctic winter.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Romania remembers him as its first Antarctic explorer and as a founder, with René Jeannel, of biospeleology, the biology of cave life. He founded the world's first Institute of Speleology at Cluj on April 26, 1920, and explored more than 1,400 caves in France, Spain, Algeria, Italy and Slovenia. He was president of the Romanian Academy (1926-29) and rector of the University of Cluj (1929-30). He is also remembered as a photographer and draftsman of the voyage.
- **What they left behind:** The *Belgica* zoological collections; the speleological institute at Cluj, which carries his name; Romania's Antarctic station (below); caves, stamps and a 2018 silver coin in his honor.
- **Death:** November 19, 1947, in Cluj, Romania, aged 79; buried in Hajongard Cemetery, Cluj. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Note: the Romanian station named for him, Law-Racoviță-Negoiță (the former Australian Law Base, named for Phillip Law), stands in the Larsemann Hills about 2 km from Zhongshan Station, the site of the Tepenian city Zhongshan (Glossary `Z.md`), and near Progress, the site of Sinheung. Wikipedia dates its inauguration under Romania to January 13, 2006; the Australian Antarctic Division gives February 20, 2006, as the first day of shared use. It took its present name after the Romanian scientist Teodor Negoiță died in 2011.

  - [Law-Racoviță-Negoiță Station (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law-Racovi%C8%9B%C4%83-Negoi%C8%9B%C4%83_Station)

### Émile Danco (1869-1898)
- **Life and origin:** Born November 29, 1869, at Mechelen (Malines), Belgium. A lieutenant of Belgian artillery, trained at the Royal Military Academy in Brussels, and a personal friend of Adrien de Gerlache.
- **Antarctic role:** De Gerlache's first recruit and the *Belgica*'s geophysicist, in charge of magnetic observations. In early February 1898 he took part in the landings in the Gerlache Strait, including the first camping trip on Brabant Island. He had a chronic heart condition, which the cold and the polar night made worse.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He died aboard the ship on which St. Roald was first mate, the first death of the first Antarctic winter.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as a well-liked officer; Cool Antarctica records that he had foreseen trouble from the ship's mixed international crew. His observations were carried on by Lecointe. Arctowski described the burial: "In the obscurity of the midday twilight we carried Lieutenant Danco's body to a hole which had been cut in the ice, and committed it to the deep." Julian Sancton's *Madhouse at the End of the Earth* (2021) describes how the death shook the crew.
- **What they left behind:** The Danco Coast of the Antarctic Peninsula (named by de Gerlache), Danco Island in the Errera Channel off the Gerlache Strait, and the asteroid 9812 Danco.
- **Death:** June 5, 1898, aboard the *Belgica* in the Bellingshausen Sea, of his heart condition, aged 28. Buried at sea through a hole cut in the ice.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. The real Danco Coast faces Anvers Island, the site of Palmer City, across the Gerlache Strait; it does not appear in the glossary.

### Carl August Wiencke (c. 1877-1898)
- **Life and origin:** A Norwegian able seaman, born about 1877 in Kristiania (Oslo) according to the Amundsen biography site of Mia (Museene i Akershus). Sources give his age at death as 18 or 20. Little else is recorded.
- **Antarctic role:** Seaman on the *Belgica*. On January 22, 1898, in a gale on the run toward the South Shetland Islands, he was sent to clear clogged scuppers on the flooded deck, without a safety line, and a wave washed him overboard. Lecointe tied a rope around his waist, went into the sea and caught him, but the pitching ship plunged them in and out of the water until Wiencke lost his hold and drowned.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** The first death of the *Belgica* expedition, a young Norwegian seaman on the voyage where St. Roald served as first mate.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered for the manner of his death and for Lecointe's attempt to save him; nothing more of his character is recorded in the sources found.
- **What they left behind:** Wiencke Island in the Palmer Archipelago, named for him by de Gerlache. Port Lockroy is a sheltered harbor off its coast, and the Port Lockroy base (1944) stands on Goudier Island inside that harbor.
- **Death:** January 22, 1898, drowned at sea near the South Shetland Islands. His body was not recovered.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him. Puerto Abrigo (formerly Port Lockroy; Glossary `P.md`) stands on Goudier Island in the harbor off Wiencke Island; the island itself does not appear in the glossary.

### Olav Bjaaland (1873-1961)
- **Life and origin:** Olav Olavsen Bjaaland, born March 5, 1873, on the farm Søndre Bjaaland in Morgedal, Kviteseid, Telemark, Norway. A farmer, logger and livestock trader, and one of Norway's best skiers: he won the King's Cup at Holmenkollen in 1902 and an international race at Chamonix in 1909. He was a self-taught ski maker.
- **Antarctic role:** Member of Amundsen's *Fram* expedition (1910-12). With Jørgen Stubberud he put up the prefabricated hut Framheim at the Bay of Whales in January 1911. Over the winter of 1911 the two worked in a snow workshop the men called "the United Carpenters," planing the sledges down to the lightest weight that would still hold. He drove a dog sledge on the polar journey and reached the South Pole with Amundsen on December 14, 1911.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** One of St. Roald's four companions at the Pole. He was recruited for his skiing, and he took most of the usable photographs of the sledge journey, including the Fram Museum's pictures of the party at the Pole tent with the Norwegian flag.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as the champion skier and craftsman of the polar party. His diary, written in his Telemark dialect, has been transcribed and published by the Fram Museum and is a frank, often wry, source on the expedition. In old age he told younger athletes to "stop the chase for records" (Fram Museum, translated).
- **What they left behind:** His photographs of the polar journey; his published diary; Telemark Skifabrikk, the ski factory he founded in Kviteseid; Mount Bjaaland in the Queen Maud Mountains; and the Olav Bjaaland Museum in Morgedal, to which he left his belongings. He lit the torch for the 1952 Oslo Winter Olympics relay at Morgedal. With Stubberud he was the last survivor of the South Pole expedition.
- **Death:** June 8, 1961, in Kviteseid (Morgedal), Telemark, aged 88. No cause found in the sources. He had a state honorary pension from 1958 (Fram Museum) or 1959 (Store norske leksikon) until his death.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. He stood at the site of Amundsen Station (Glossary `A.md`); Framheim and the Bay of Whales do not appear in the glossary.
- **Sources:**
  - [Olav Olavsen Bjaaland (1873-1961), Fram Museum](https://frammuseum.no/polar-history/explorers/olav-olavsen-bjaaland-1873-1961/)
  - [Olav Bjaaland, Store norske leksikon](https://snl.no/Olav_Bjaaland)
  - [Olav Bjaaland (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olav_Bjaaland)

### Helmer Hanssen (1870-1956)
- **Life and origin:** Helmer Julius Hanssen, born September 24, 1870, at Bjørnskinn (now Andøy), Vesterålen, Nordland, Norway, the son of a farmer and fisherman. He fished in Lofoten and Finnmark from age 11, hunted small whales and seals in the Arctic Ocean from 1894 to 1897, and took his mate's certificate in 1897.
- **Antarctic role:** Ice pilot and dog driver on the *Fram* expedition (1910-12). He left Framheim with the polar party on October 20, 1911 (four sledges, 52 dogs), drove the leading sledge almost the whole way, and reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911. The party was back at Framheim on January 25, 1912, after about 3,000 km in 99 days.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** St. Roald's most constant companion: mate on *Gjøa* through the Northwest Passage (1903-06), where he learned dog driving from the Inuit, then the *Fram* and *Maud* expeditions. His sledge carried the main compass. Store norske leksikon records that on the last stretch to the Pole he stood aside so that Amundsen arrived first.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A tough, practical Arctic seaman whose dog driving was central to the Norwegian method. As captain on *Maud* he made a sledge round trip of about 4,000 km from the ship near Ayon Island to Anadyr in six and a half months, likely a record at the time. The partnership ended badly: Amundsen discharged him at Nome, Alaska, in 1920, believing the captaincy had gone to his head (Fram Museum).
- **What they left behind:** His memoir *Gjennom isbaksen* (1941); a memorial stone behind Bjørnskinn church in Andøy; the University of Tromsø's research vessel *Helmer Hanssen* (renamed for him in 2011). He was made a knight of the Order of St. Olav in 1906 and received the South Pole Medal in 1912.
- **Death:** August 2, 1956, in Tromsø, Norway, aged 85; buried from Tromsø Cathedral at state expense. No cause found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. He stood at the site of Amundsen Station (Glossary `A.md`).

### Sverre Hassel (1876-1928)
- **Life and origin:** Sverre Helge Hassel, born July 30, 1876, in the Grønland district of Kristiania (Oslo), Norway, the son of a restaurant and dance-hall owner. He trained on the school ship *Christiania*, took mate's and master's certificates, and served as a customs officer and naval officer.
- **Antarctic role:** Dog handler on the *Fram* expedition (1910-12). He drove one of the four sledges on the polar journey and reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** One of St. Roald's four companions at the Pole. He had learned dog driving on Otto Sverdrup's Second *Fram* Expedition (1898-1902) in the Canadian Arctic, which mapped about 150,000 km² of new land. He later declined Amundsen's invitation to stay on for the planned Arctic drift. He died in Amundsen's own garden, twelve days before Amundsen disappeared.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A quiet, capable dog man. He gave more than 100 lectures on the expedition in the winter of 1913. He told audiences that dog meat "is not the worst kind of food" and called Amundsen "a fantastic man" without whom they could not have succeeded (Fram Museum, translated).
- **What they left behind:** Mount Hassel in the Queen Maud Mountains and Hassel Sound in the Canadian Arctic are named for him. He became chief of customs at Grimstad by 1922.
- **Death:** June 6, 1928, of a heart attack in the garden of Uranienborg, Amundsen's home at Svartskog near Oslo, while visiting after a hospital stay for heart trouble; aged 51. Amundsen's journal for that day reads "Hassel died at 5:15 pm" (*Science Norway*). Amundsen himself vanished on June 18, 1928.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. He stood at the site of Amundsen Station (Glossary `A.md`).

### Oscar Wisting (1871-1936)
- **Life and origin:** Oscar Adolf Wisting, born June 6, 1871, at Langestrand, Larvik, Vestfold, Norway. After seven years at sea he joined the Norwegian Navy at 22.
- **Antarctic role:** Member of the *Fram* expedition (1910-12). He drove a dog sledge on the polar journey and reached the South Pole with Amundsen on December 14, 1911.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** One of St. Roald's four companions at the Pole, and the companion who followed him furthest afterward. He was chief officer on *Maud* (1918-25) and in effect led that expedition from 1923, after Amundsen left for his flying attempts. On the airship *Norge* he reached the North Pole on May 12, 1926; he and Amundsen were the first two people to have stood at both Poles, and they shook hands there (Fram Museum).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as steady and loyal, the man Amundsen trusted with his ships. In retirement he gave himself to saving *Fram*: he commanded her last voyage from Horten to Bygdøy, Oslo, and worked for her preservation and for the building of the Fram Museum.
- **What they left behind:** Mount Wisting, at the head of the Amundsen Glacier in the Queen Maud Mountains; and, in large part, the preserved *Fram* itself in the Fram Museum, Oslo.
- **Death:** Found dead of a heart attack in his old bunk aboard *Fram* at Bygdøy, Oslo, days before the 25th anniversary of the Pole. The Fram Museum gives December 4, 1936; Wikipedia gives December 5, 1936.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. He stood at the site of Amundsen Station (Glossary `A.md`).
- **Sources:**

### Hjalmar Johansen (1867-1913)
- **Life and origin:** Fredrik Hjalmar Johansen, born May 15, 1867, in Skien, Telemark, Norway. He studied law at the Royal Frederick University (Oslo) but left after his father's death; a national gymnastics champion at 20 and a junior army officer.
- **Antarctic role:** Member of the *Fram* expedition (1910-12) and of the 1911 depot-laying journeys. On September 8, 1911, Amundsen started for the Pole too early in the season; in temperatures near -50°C the party turned back. On the scramble home Amundsen and others went ahead, and Johansen brought the exhausted Kristian Prestrud in to Framheim without tent or cooker. He then criticized Amundsen in front of the men. Amundsen dropped him from the polar party and sent him with Prestrud (now his superior) and Stubberud to explore King Edward VII Land (November-December 1911).
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** The record treats his quarrel with St. Roald and his dismissal as the bitterest episode of the South Pole expedition. At Hobart in March 1912 Amundsen paid him off and ordered him home separately.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** As Fridtjof Nansen's sole companion on the 1895 dash from *Fram*, he reached 86°14′N, then the closest approach to the North Pole, and wintered with Nansen in a stone-and-earth hut on Franz Josef Land; the Fram Museum calls his energy and help decisive for their survival. He was immensely strong and admired for endurance, but he struggled with drink, debt and depression; his marriage ended in 1907, and Nansen helped him onto Amundsen's crew. Sources judge Amundsen's treatment of him harshly.
- **What they left behind:** The Prestrud rescue, the King Edward VII Land journey, and his part in Nansen's farthest north. The Hjalmar Johansen Seamount in the Arctic Ocean was named for him in 2005.
- **Death:** January 3, 1913, in Kristiania (Oslo); he shot himself at Solli plass, about six months after returning home (Store norske leksikon; some secondary accounts give January 4).
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. King Edward VII Land does not appear in the glossary.

### Thorvald Nilsen (1881-1940)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 6, 1881, in Kristiansand, Norway. He passed the navigation examination in 1900, graduated from the Norwegian Naval Academy at Horten in 1903, took his master's certificate in 1906, and served in both naval and merchant ships, including command of a South America liner.
- **Antarctic role:** Captain of *Fram* on Amundsen's expedition, chosen after the death of the first choice, Ole Engelstad. *Fram* sailed from Kristiansand on August 9, 1910, and reached the Bay of Whales on January 13, 1911 (some accounts say January 14). There she went to 78°41′S, the farthest south any ship had reached; since she held the farthest north as well, she became the ship that had been furthest both north and south. Nilsen left on February 14, 1911, ran an oceanographic cruise in the South Atlantic with the Russian oceanographer Aleksandr Kuchin, came back for the shore party in January 1912, and brought *Fram* into Hobart on March 7, 1912.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He commanded the ship that carried St. Roald south and brought him and the polar party back. The Fram Museum records that he was the first of the crew told that the goal was the South Pole, not the Arctic. *Fram* was also at the Bay of Whales on February 4, 1911, when Scott's *Terra Nova* put in there and found the Norwegians (a tie to St. Robert).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A trained naval officer who handled the ship's long independent cruise, about 54,400 nautical miles in all, without notable mishap. He was made a knight first class of the Order of St. Olav in 1913 and received the French Legion of Honor.
- **What they left behind:** The *Fram*'s South Atlantic oceanographic record and the ship's farthest-south mark. (The Nilsen Plateau in the Queen Maud Mountains bears his name; not confirmed in the sources used here.)
- **Death:** April 19, 1940, in Buenos Aires, Argentina, where he had lived since leaving the navy in 1920 and worked as a marine insurance agent; aged 58. No cause found. His ashes were returned to Norway.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.
- **Sources:**

**St. Douglas: Mawson's companions**

### Xavier Mertz (1882-1913)
- **Life and origin:** Xavier Guillaume Mertz, born October 6, 1882, in Basel, Switzerland, son of Emil Mertz, a textile-machinery manufacturer. He studied law at Leipzig and Bern (doctor of laws, Bern) and took a science doctorate at Lausanne on glacier and mountain formation. He was a leading Swiss mountaineer and a champion skier (Swiss ski-running title; the AAD adds Swiss and world ski-jumping titles, 1908 and 1909).
- **Antarctic role:** He met Mawson in London in early 1911 and was taken on, as an exception to Mawson's preference for British subjects, as ski instructor for the Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911-1914). He soon took charge of the expedition's Greenland dogs, with Belgrave Ninnis, at the Main Base at Cape Denison, Commonwealth Bay. In November 1912 he set out with Mawson and Ninnis on the Far Eastern Party. After Ninnis fell into a crevasse on December 14, 1912, with the best dog team and most of the food, Mertz and Mawson turned back, eating the remaining dogs on the way.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** One of the two companions of St. Douglas on the Far Eastern journey, and the second to die on it. His death left Mawson to make the last 100 miles (160 km) to Cape Denison alone, the walk that defines Mawson's reputation. Mawson's diary record of Mertz's symptoms later became the main evidence for the vitamin A theory of his death.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as an expert skier and mountaineer, the natural choice for the dogs. His application letter said he was "as good as any one on skys." Charles Laseron wrote that Mertz had "practically adopted Ninnis." Mawson called him "one of Nature's gentlemen" on his return (quoted in Wikipedia). He kept a diary in German to January 1, 1913.
- **What they left behind:** Mertz Glacier and the Mertz Glacier Tongue in George V Land, and the undersea Mertz-Ninnis Valley, all named by Mawson. The memorial cross to Ninnis and Mertz on Azimuth Hill, northwest of the Main Hut at Cape Denison, built in November 1913 by Francis Bickerton from remnant radio-mast timber. Its plaque reads: "Erected to commemorate the supreme sacrifice made by Lieut. BES Ninnis, RF and Dr X Mertz in the cause of science AAE 1913." The original plaque is in the Australian Antarctic Division library at Kingston, Tasmania; a replica is on the cross, which was re-fixed in 1931, 1978 and 1997-1998.
- **Death:** Died in the tent in George V Land, about 100 miles from Cape Denison. Mawson's diary has him delirious on the evening of January 7, 1913, and dying peacefully about 2 a.m. on January 8 (the date most sources give; the AAD Mawson's Huts page says January 7). Mawson left him in his sleeping bag under snow blocks with a cross made from sledge runners; the grave was never recovered. Cause: vitamin A poisoning from eating husky livers is the most widely accepted view (Cleland and Southcott, 1969). Phillip Law suggested cold exposure, and Denise Carrington-Smith (*Medical Journal of Australia*, 2005) proposed general malnutrition, the sudden all-meat diet and stress.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. The Azimuth Hill cross stands at Cape Denison, the site of the city Denison.

### Belgrave Ninnis (1887-1912)
- **Life and origin:** Belgrave Edward Sutton Ninnis, born June 22, 1887, at Streatham, Surrey, England, son of Belgrave Ninnis senior, a Royal Navy surgeon (later inspector-general) who had sailed on George Nares's British Arctic Expedition of 1875-1876. He was educated at Dulwich College, Shackleton's old school, and was a lieutenant in the Royal Fusiliers, aged 23, when he joined.
- **Antarctic role:** He joined the Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911-1914) in London to care for the 48 Greenland dogs and sailed out on *Aurora* under John King Davis, forming a close friendship with Xavier Mertz on the voyage. At the Main Base at Cape Denison he ran the dogs with Mertz. He left Cape Denison on November 10, 1912, with Mawson and Mertz on the Far Eastern Party to survey George V Land.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** One of the two companions of St. Douglas on the Far Eastern journey, and its first death. His fall took the strongest dog team, the tent and most of the food, and so set up the starvation march that killed Mertz and nearly killed Mawson. Mawson read the burial service over the crevasse and wrote, nine hours after the accident, "May God help us."
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered mainly for his death, the expedition's first, and for his friendship with Mertz (Laseron wrote that Mertz had "practically adopted" him). The AAD quotes him on the hut's place in the landscape: "From the creation the silence here has been unbroken by man, and now we, a very prosaic group of fellows, are here for an infinitely small space of time." Mertz wrote of standing "helplessly, next to a friend's grave, my best friend" (as quoted in Wikipedia).
- **What they left behind:** The Ninnis Glacier (where he fell) and Ninnis Glacier Tongue, named by Mawson, and the shared Mertz-Ninnis undersea valley. His initials are still carved into the side of his bunk in the Main Hut at Cape Denison, in the corner the men called "Hyde Park Corner." He is named on the November 1913 memorial cross on Azimuth Hill (see Mertz). A memorial to him and Mertz at Mawson's Place, Hobart, was funded by the British and Swiss governments and the Mawson's Huts Foundation (Wikipedia dates it 2021).
- **Death:** December 14, 1912, about 480-500 km east of Cape Denison, aged 25. Walking beside his sledge rather than riding or skiing, he broke through the snow lid of a crevasse that Mertz had skied across and Mawson had crossed on his sledge. He fell with six dogs, the sledge and most of the supplies. He was never seen again, and his body was never recovered.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. His bunk and the memorial cross are at Cape Denison, the site of the city Denison.

### Frank Hurley (1885-1962)
- **Life and origin:** James Francis Hurley, born October 15, 1885, at Glebe, Sydney, New South Wales, son of Edward and Margaret Hurley. He left Glebe Public School and ran away at 14 to work at the Lithgow ironworks, where he picked up metalwork and electrical skills. He bought his first camera for 15 shillings and was a commercial postcard photographer by about 20. Henri Mallard recommended him to Mawson in 1911.
- **Antarctic role:** Official photographer of Mawson's Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911-1914) at Cape Denison. From November 10, 1912, to January 10, 1913, he sledged with Robert Bage (leader) and Eric Webb on the Southern Party toward the South Magnetic Pole; they turned back about 50 miles short when rations ran low. He went south again on *Aurora* in late 1913 to bring Mawson home. He was photographer and filmmaker of Shackleton's *Endurance* expedition (1914-1917) and one of the 22 men who waited on Elephant Island under Frank Wild in 1916. He returned south with Mawson on BANZARE (1929-1931).
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He served under both St. Douglas and St. Ernest, and his photographs and films are the main visual record of both men's expeditions. When *Endurance* was abandoned in 1915, he went into the flooded hold for his glass-plate negatives; on Shackleton's order to cut weight he kept about 120 at Ocean Camp and smashed the rest.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A daring, inventive photographer, known for risks taken "in the pursuit of a good shot" (Wikipedia) and praised by SPRI as tireless and many-skilled. Some contemporaries found him self-promoting and his storytelling sensational. As official Australian war photographer (honorary captain, 1917) at Ypres and Passchendaele, his composite photographs were called "fake" by the official historian Charles Bean; Hurley replied that a single negative could not show the war's scale. He headed the Australian photographic unit in the Middle East in World War II.
- **What they left behind:** The photographs of Cape Denison and of *Endurance* beset and sinking, and the films *Home of the Blizzard* (1913), *South* (1919, the *Endurance* film, also released as *In the Grip of the Polar Pack Ice*), *Southward Ho with Mawson* (1930) and *Siege of the South* (1931). He wrote *Argonauts of the South* (1925) and *Shackleton's Argonauts*, and his diaries were published in 2011. He received three Polar Medals.
- **Death:** Died January 16, 1962, at Collaroy Plateau, Sydney, aged 76, of a heart attack (cardiac infarction, per Wikipedia; SPRI says he died in his armchair).
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. His Antarctic base was Cape Denison, the site of the city Denison.

  - [Australian Antarctic Division, Mawson's Huts, "Trudging to nowhere" (the Southern Party)](https://mawsonshuts.antarctica.gov.au/cape-denison/journeys-of-discovery/trudging-to-nowhere/)

### Edgeworth David (1858-1934)
- **Life and origin:** Tannatt William Edgeworth David, born January 28, 1858, at St Fagans near Cardiff, Wales, son of the rector of St Fagans. He was educated at Magdalen College School and New College, Oxford, and briefly at the Royal School of Mines, London, and studied glacial deposits in south Wales. After a health voyage in 1878, he moved to New South Wales in 1882 as a geological surveyor and was professor of geology at the University of Sydney from 1891 to 1924, where Mawson was his student.
- **Antarctic role:** Chief scientist of Shackleton's *Nimrod* expedition (British Antarctic Expedition, 1907-1909), joining at 49. He led the first ascent of Mount Erebus from Cape Royds: the party (David, Mawson, Alistair Mackay, with Jameson Adams, Eric Marshall and Philip Brocklehurst in support) reached the crater rim on March 10, 1908; all but Brocklehurst, who was frostbitten, made the top. He then led the Northern Party with Mawson and Mackay, which left Cape Royds on October 5, 1908, man-hauled up the Victoria Land coast and onto the plateau, and reached the calculated position of the South Magnetic Pole on January 16, 1909, where they took possession of the region for the British Crown. The journey of about 1,260 miles (2,028 km) in 122 days stood as the longest unsupported sledge journey until the 1980s. *Nimrod* picked them up on the coast in early February 1909.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** Mawson's teacher at Sydney, who brought him onto Shackleton's expedition, so he links St. Douglas and St. Ernest directly. He climbed Erebus and reached the Magnetic Pole with Mawson. Near the end of the return, with David exhausted, the expedition doctor Mackay intervened and David handed leadership of the party to Mawson in writing (January 31, 1909, per Wikipedia). He later backed Mawson's own Australasian Antarctic Expedition.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** National Museum Wales calls him "one of Australia's most famous scientists," and he led Erebus at 50. By the last weeks of the pole journey he was failing physically, which is why Mackay forced the change of leader. In World War I, aged 57, he was commissioned a major (October 25, 1915) and served as a geologist with the Australian tunneling companies on the Western Front; he fell down a well in September 1916, was injured, and returned to duty. He received the DSO (1918) and was knighted KBE (1920).
- **What they left behind:** The first ascent of Erebus and the first party at the South Magnetic Pole. The David Glacier in Victoria Land, whose floating seaward end is the Drygalski Ice Tongue, is named for him, as is the mineral davidite. He wrote the Erebus chapter of *Aurora Australis* (1908), the first book printed in Antarctica. The Royal Society of New South Wales awards the Edgeworth David Medal. He received the Polar Medal in 1910.
- **Death:** Died August 28, 1934, in Sydney, aged 76, of lobar pneumonia. He was given a state funeral, reported as the first in New South Wales for someone other than a politician.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Mount Erebus, which he first climbed, stands over the city Fort McMurdo (canon has an informal custom there, "The Watching," of gathering to watch the summit glow). The Drygalski Ice Tongue, at the end of the glacier named for him, is part of what keeps Janbogo's port on Terra Nova Bay open.
- **Sources:**
  - [Australian Dictionary of Biography, "David, Sir Tannatt William Edgeworth"](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/david-sir-tannatt-william-edgeworth-5894) (the standard biography; the site blocked fetching in this run, so the facts above were checked against the other sources)
  - [National Museum Wales, "The Welsh in Antarctica"](https://museum.wales/articles/1118/The-Welsh-in-Antarctica/)
  - [*Evening Post* (Wellington), "Honour of State Funeral," August 29, 1934 (Papers Past)](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/EP19340829.2.63.2)
  - [Wikipedia, "Edgeworth David"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgeworth_David)

### Alistair Mackay (1878-1914)
- **Life and origin:** Alistair (or Alister) Forbes Mackay, born February 22, 1878, at Carskey, Argyllshire, Scotland, son of Lieutenant Colonel A. Forbes Mackay and Margaret Isabella Innes Mackay. He was educated at George Watson's College and studied medicine at Edinburgh, interrupted by biological research at Dundee and service as a trooper in the Boer War and in Baden-Powell's South African Constabulary. He qualified in 1901 and served as a Royal Navy surgeon (1902-1906).
- **Antarctic role:** Assistant surgeon on Shackleton's *Nimrod* expedition (1907-1909). He reached the summit of Mount Erebus on March 10, 1908, in the first ascent. With Edgeworth David and Mawson he formed the three-man Northern Party, which left Cape Royds on October 5, 1908, surveyed the Victoria Land coast and reached the vicinity of the South Magnetic Pole on January 16, 1909.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** Shackleton's surgeon, and Mawson's companion on Erebus and on the Magnetic Pole journey. He is the man who made Mawson leader: on January 31, 1909, with Mawson out of earshot, he used his authority as the party's doctor and threatened to declare David insane unless David gave Mawson written authority to lead (Wikipedia, from the party's diaries).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as a forceful, decisive man in hardship; the David episode is the best-known instance. After Antarctica he joined Vilhjalmur Stefansson's Canadian Arctic Expedition (1913-1918) as surgeon of the *Karluk*. When the ship sank in the Chukchi Sea on January 11, 1914, he disagreed with Captain Bob Bartlett's plan, and on February 5 he left Shipwreck Camp with the oceanographer James Murray, the anthropologist Henri Beuchat and the sailor Stanley Morris to walk for land. The four gave Bartlett a letter absolving him of responsibility for their choice; Bartlett was later criticized for letting them go.
- **What they left behind:** The Mackay Glacier in Victoria Land (76°58′S, 162°00′E), named for him by the Magnetic Pole party. The first ascent of Erebus and the first party at the South Magnetic Pole. He received the silver Polar Medal (1909). His papers are held in UK archives (Archives Hub, "Alister Forbes Mackay collection").
- **Death:** Died probably in February 1914, aged 35 or 36, on the sea ice of the Chukchi Sea, Arctic. His party was last seen approaching Herald Island, on the way toward Wrangel Island; none of the four was seen again, and their bodies were never found. Presumed cause: exposure and the dangers of the sea ice.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Mount Erebus, which he helped make the first ascent of, stands over the city Fort McMurdo.
- **Sources:**
  - [Scott Polar Research Institute, Shackleton biographies, "Mackay, Alistair Forbes"](https://www.spri.cam.ac.uk/museum/shackleton/biographies/Mackay,_Alistair_Forbes/)
  - [Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador, "The Karluk Disaster"](https://www.heritage.nf.ca/articles/exploration/karluk-disaster.php)

**St. Richard: the South Pole flight**

### Bernt Balchen (1899-1973), with Harold June and Ashley McKinley
- **Life and origin:** Born October 23, 1899, at Tveit, Kristiansand, Norway, the son of a country doctor. He served in the Norwegian Army's artillery, fought with the Finnish White Guards in the Finnish Civil War (1918), boxed and shot competitively, and became a pilot in the Royal Norwegian Navy Air Service in 1921.
- **Antarctic role:** Chief pilot of Byrd's first Antarctic expedition (1928-30), based at Little America on the Ross Ice Shelf. In March 1929 a gale wrecked his Fokker in the Rockefeller Mountains, stranding him with Harold June and the geologist Laurence Gould until Byrd flew in to fetch them. On November 28-29, 1929, he flew the Ford Trimotor *Floyd Bennett* from Little America to the South Pole and back in 18 hours 41 minutes, with Byrd navigating. To climb over the Queen Maud Mountains through the pass between Mount Fridtjof Nansen and Mount Fisher, the crew threw out weight: two 150-pound food sacks, by Balchen's account as given by *Air & Space Forces Magazine*; "both their rations and their fuel reserves," per the Norwegian Polar Institute.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He piloted St. Richard's defining flight; the Norwegian Polar Institute's summary is that "a Norwegian was at the controls." He also links St. Richard to St. Roald: his polar career began with the 1925 Amundsen-Ellsworth relief expedition to Spitsbergen and the ground crew of the 1926 *Norge* flight at Spitsbergen, where he also helped Byrd's team repair their aircraft. The two men later fell out. After Byrd's death in 1957 Balchen said openly that Byrd could not have reached the North Pole in 1926, in his 1958 autobiography *Come North with Me*, a 1960 letter and later technical calculations (1971, per the Norwegian Polar Institute). Byrd's son and supporters called this slander, and each man believed the other was working against him.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered as one of the great polar and cold-weather pilots. He did most of the flying on the Fokker *America*'s 1927 transatlantic flight and ditched it safely off the French coast. He gave his $10,000 fee for the 1928 *Bremen* relief flight to the widow of Floyd Bennett. He became a U.S. citizen by a special act of Congress in 1931, helped found the Norwegian airline DNL in 1933, set up the "Little Norway" training camp in Canada for Norwegian airmen in World War II, and ran covert air operations from Sweden that brought out more than 2,000 Norwegians and interned Americans. After the war he led the building of Thule Air Base in Greenland and commanded the 10th Rescue Squadron in Alaska (1948-51).
- **Harold June (1895-1962):** Harold Irving June, born February 12, 1895, at Stamford, Connecticut; a Navy chief machinist's mate in World War I and one of the few enlisted Navy pilots of his time (first in his 1923 pilot class; test pilot from 1925). Co-pilot and radio operator on the Pole flight; he sent the radio message that they had reached the vicinity of the Pole. He survived the Rockefeller Mountains wreck with Balchen, and on Byrd's second expedition (1933-35) he was chief pilot and led a tractor journey into the Edsel Ford Range. Commissioned lieutenant in 1943, he retired in 1947 after 30 years in the Navy and died in Windsor, Connecticut, in 1962. June Nunatak on the Liv Glacier is named for him.
- **Ashley McKinley (1896-1970):** Ashley Chadbourne McKinley, born June 23, 1896, at Marshall, Texas; Missouri National Guard (1916), Army Signal Corps (1917), then an Army Air Corps aerial photographer and co-author of *Applied Aerial Photography* (1929). He took the mapping photographs on the Pole flight and received the Distinguished Flying Cross (1930). In World War II, at Ladd Field, Alaska, he was the first to propose that U.S. aircraft be built to work in subzero cold, and he pushed for cold-weather test facilities; the McKinley Climatic Laboratory at Eglin, Florida, was named for him in June 1971. He died in Florida on February 11, 1970, and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery.
- **What they left behind:** The first flight over the South Pole and its aerial photographs; Thule Air Base; the McKinley Climatic Laboratory; mountains in Alaska and Antarctica named for Balchen and June Nunatak for June.
- **Death:** Balchen died October 17, 1973, at Mount Kisco, New York, of bone cancer, aged 73. He is buried at Arlington National Cemetery, where Byrd is also buried; Wikipedia places his grave next to Byrd's, which the other sources checked do not confirm.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. The Tepenian city Byrd stands on the Byrd Station site in Marie Byrd Land (Glossary `B.md`); Little America, the base the flight left from, does not appear in the glossary.

**St. Carsten: the *Southern Cross* expedition, 1898-1900**

### Nicolai Hanson (1870-1899)
- **Life and origin:** Born August 24, 1870, at Kristiansund (then Christiansund), Møre og Romsdal, Norway. He studied zoology at the University of Christiania (now Oslo) under Professor Robert Collett and was an experienced skier. He married shortly before leaving; his daughter, Johanne, was born after he sailed, and he never met her.
- **Antarctic role:** Zoologist of Borchgrevink's British Antarctic (*Southern Cross*) Expedition (1898-1900), collecting for the British Museum and the Zoological Museum in Christiania. He was seriously ill on the voyage out from England but recovered enough to work. He was one of the ten men who wintered at Cape Adare in 1899, the first party to winter on the Antarctic continent, and studied its marine life.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He died during St. Carsten's wintering, the act Cape Adare's canon honors, and was buried on the cape itself. Every later account of Borchgrevink's wintering carries his grave with it.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Remembered almost entirely as the first person to die and be buried on the Antarctic continent. Little else of his character survives in the sources checked; his collections went into the expedition's natural-history report published by the British Museum (1902).
- **What they left behind:** His grave on the ridge above the expedition's huts at Cape Adare, the first grave on the continent. The frozen ground had to be blasted with dynamite to dig it. It is outlined in white quartz stones, with a large boulder at its head; when *Southern Cross* returned in 1900, a memorial service was held and a brass plaque and an iron cross were fixed to the boulder. It is protected under the Antarctic Treaty as Historic Site and Monument No. 23, separately from Borchgrevink's huts (HSM 22). Named for him are Mount Hanson, "a small peak south of Cape Adare" (Wikipedia), and Hanson Peak (1,255 m) on the Adare Peninsula (Antarctic Heritage Trust), probably the same feature, and the fish *Trematomus nicolai*.
- **Death:** Died October 14, 1899, at Cape Adare, aged 29, after about three months of illness. The cause is usually given as "apparently an intestinal disorder"; it was never firmly diagnosed. At his own request he was buried on the high ground above Cape Adare.
- **Tepenian place:** Cape Adare (St. Carsten's city). The Glossary records Borchgrevink's hut there but not the grave; none named for him in canon.

### Louis Bernacchi (1876-1942)
- **Life and origin:** Louis Charles Bernacchi, born November 8, 1876, at Schaerbeek, Brussels, Belgium, to an Italian father, Diego Bernacchi, and a Flemish mother. The family came to Tasmania when he was 7, and his father set up a vineyard and farm on Maria Island in 1884. He attended the Hutchins School in Hobart (1889-1891) and from 1895 trained for about three years at the Melbourne Observatory in astronomy, magnetism, meteorology and physics. His childhood hero was James Clark Ross.
- **Antarctic role:** Physicist of Borchgrevink's *Southern Cross* expedition (1898-1900), one of the ten who wintered at Cape Adare in 1899, the first wintering on the continent, where he kept the magnetic and meteorological records. He then served as physicist on Scott's *Discovery* expedition (1901-1904), which wintered at Hut Point; he was the only man on it who had been to the Antarctic before. He made extensive magnetic observations and succeeded Shackleton as editor of the expedition's *South Polar Times*.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** He wintered with St. Carsten at Cape Adare, and his diaries are a main source for that winter (published as *That First Antarctic Winter*). On *Discovery* he served beside St. Robert and St. Ernest, taking over the *South Polar Times* from Shackleton. Scott was best man at his wedding in 1906.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The AAD credits him with holding up morale in Borchgrevink's "fractious" ten-man party and getting the science done despite the tensions; he found the *Discovery* years "more enjoyable." His summing-up of polar life is often quoted: "Life in the Antarctic is one of hardship, privation, monotony and isolation, but it has a subtle charm which is indefinable." He stood unsuccessfully as a Liberal for Parliament (Widnes, 1910), served in the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve's anti-submarine division in World War I (OBE; U.S. Navy Cross, 1919), sat on the Royal Geographical Society council (1928-1932) and organized the British Polar Exhibition (1930).
- **What they left behind:** The magnetic and weather records of the first winter on the continent (Cape Adare, 1899). Books: *To the South Polar Regions* (1901), *The Polar Book* (1930), *A Very Gallant Gentleman* (1933, a life of Lawrence Oates) and *Saga of the "Discovery"* (1938). Cape Bernacchi, Bernacchi Bay (Victoria Land) and Bernacchi Head (Franklin Island), and the fish *Trematomus bernacchii*, are named for him.
- **Death:** Died April 24, 1942, at his home in Paddington, London, aged 65, after rejoining the naval reserve in World War II. Cause not given in the sources checked.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. He wintered at Cape Adare (St. Carsten's city) and at Hut Point, which canon makes the city of Scott's most sacred civic site.
- **Sources:**
  - [Australian Antarctic Division, "Louis Bernacchi"](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/history/people/louis-bernacchi/)
  - [Antarctic Heritage Trust (New Zealand), "Borchgrevink's Hut, Cape Adare: crew"](https://nzaht.org/conserve/explorer-bases/borchgrevinks-hut-cape-adare/crew/)
  - [Wikipedia, "Louis Bernacchi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Bernacchi)

**St. Jules: the *Astrolabe* and *Zélée* voyage**

### Charles Hector Jacquinot (1796-1879)
- **Life and origin:** Born March 4, 1796, at Nevers, France. A French naval officer, he served with Jules Dumont d'Urville in the Mediterranean and sailed as an ensign on Louis Isidore Duperrey's scientific circumnavigation in the *Coquille* (1822-25).
- **Antarctic role:** Commander of the corvette *Zélée* (380 tons, about 70-79 men), the consort of d'Urville's *Astrolabe*, through the whole 1837-40 expedition, which left Toulon on September 7, 1837. In early 1838 the two ships met the pack ice of the Weddell Sea and charted the northern tip of the Antarctic Peninsula. In January 1840, sailing south from Hobart, they found the coast that d'Urville named Adélie Land. A party landed on a small offshore islet on January 21, 1840, raised the French flag and took rock samples. (Sources differ: Cool Antarctica dates the event January 19 and calls it the first landing on continental Antarctica, but the landing was on an islet; the first-round survey's St. Jules entry gives the sighting as January 20 and the landing as January 21.) The sources checked do not say whether Jacquinot himself went ashore. The expedition was back in Toulon on November 7, 1840.
- **Tie to an existing Saint:** St. Jules's second ship captain and, by the accounts in Wikipedia, his closest friend. He had already sailed with d'Urville in the *Astrolabe* (the renamed *Coquille*) on the 1826-29 Pacific voyage, which recovered relics of the lost La Pérouse expedition. After d'Urville was killed in the Meudon rail disaster in 1842, Jacquinot, with the hydrographer Clément Adrien Vincendon-Dumoulin, finished editing the expedition's 24-volume account, *Voyage au pôle sud et dans l'Océanie*.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He kept command of the *Zélée* for the whole three-year voyage, which lost men to scurvy and desertion. He received the Cross of Honor for the 1826-29 voyage. His younger brother Honoré Jacquinot sailed as surgeon and naturalist, and a cousin, Charles Thanaron, as a lieutenant. He rose to vice admiral, commanded the French naval force at Piraeus, Greece, in 1854-55 during the Crimean War, and asked to be buried without military honors.
- **What they left behind:** The *Zélée*'s share of the 1837-40 charts and collections, and the completed published account of the voyage. Mount Jacquinot in the Antarctic was named for him by d'Urville.
- **Death:** November 17, 1879, after retiring from the naval general staff, aged 83. Place and cause not found in the sources checked.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him. The Tepenian city Dumont d'Urville (Glossary `D.md`) stands on Petrel Island off Adélie Land, the coast his ship helped discover; St. Jules's memorial site there marks the January 21, 1840, landing (Glossary `S.md`, "St. Jules").
- **Sources:**
  - [Charles Jacquinot (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jacquinot)
  - [L'Astrolabe and Zélée, Cool Antarctica](https://www.coolantarctica.com/Antarctica%20fact%20file/History/antarctic_ships/L'Astrolabe_Zelee.php)
  - [Dumont d'Urville, Jules Sébastien César, Te Ara: Dictionary of New Zealand Biography](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/1d19/dumont-durville-jules-sebastien-cesar)

## Founders and firsts of the station era

Second round, 2026-11-01. Station founders and expedition leaders of the mid-20th century, early Argentine Antarctic figures, the first women, the first head of state to visit, and the International Geophysical Year. None of the 17 is named in the Glossary. The "Tepenian place" line names a canon city where the figure's real station or landing site is that city's site, and gives looser ties (the same island group, coast or region) as notes. Real stations give Tepenian cities their coordinates, not their founders.

**Station founders and expedition leaders**

### Mikhail Somov (1908-1973)
- **Life and origin:** Mikhail Mikhailovich Somov, born April 7, 1908 (March 25 Old Style), in Moscow, into a family of the nobility; his father was a fisheries scientist. The family moved to Vladivostok in 1928, where Somov worked as a lathe operator and studied shipbuilding at the Far Eastern Polytechnic Institute (1929-1931) until his father's arrest by the OGPU in 1931 ended his studies (the father was cleared in 1932). He then graduated as an engineer-oceanographer from the Moscow Hydrometeorological Institute in 1937, worked briefly at the Central Weather Institute in Moscow, and moved to the Arctic Institute in Leningrad as a senior hydrologist forecasting sea ice for the Northern Sea Route.
- **Antarctic role:** Commander of the first Soviet Antarctic Expedition (1955-1957). The diesel-electric ship *Ob* left Kaliningrad on November 30, 1955. On the Davis Sea coast of Queen Mary Land he opened Mirny, the first Soviet Antarctic station, on February 13, 1956, and led its first winter of 86 people. His expedition also set up Pionerskaya, an inland station 375 km south of the coast at 2,741 m (May 27, 1956), and Oazis in the Bunger Hills oasis (October 15, 1956), and scouted the inland sites later used for Vostok and Sovetskaya. He handed Mirny to his successor, Aleksey Treshnikov, on January 15, 1957. From late 1957 he was the Arctic Institute's deputy director for Antarctic research, directing all later Soviet expeditions; he was on the Soviet delegation at the Washington conference that adopted the Antarctic Treaty (1959) and at the first treaty consultative meeting in Canberra (1961). He returned south as leader of the 8th Soviet Antarctic Expedition's summer season (1962-1963), aboard *Ob*.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Before Antarctica he was a leading Soviet Arctic ice scientist. In World War II he took part in the defense of Dikson against the German pocket battleship *Admiral Scheer* (August 1942); in 1945 he flew over the North Pole as ice observer; on the secret high-latitude air expeditions of 1948-1949 (whose purpose was to test drifting-ice airfields for fighter aircraft) his teams helped find the undersea rise later named the Lomonosov Ridge. He led the drifting station North Pole-2 (1950-1951), the first such station since Papanin's of 1937, and was made a Hero of the Soviet Union in 1952. He also won the Vega Medal (1959) and the Royal Geographical Society's gold medal (1961). An Arctic Institute anniversary tribute (V. V. Lukin, 2018) remembers a skilled scientist and organizer and a "kind, responsive and cultured colleague" (translated from Russian). He left no memoirs; his second wife, the Leningrad writer Elena Serebrovskaya, published books about him (1978-1980).
- **What they left behind:** Mirny Station, still in operation; the first Soviet station network in Antarctica; the Arctic Institute's Antarctic research department, which he founded. The Somov Sea (off the Pacific sector, between 150°E and 170°E), a glacier in Queen Maud Land and the research ship *Mikhail Somov* (1975) are named for him; that ship was trapped in the Ross Sea ice for 133 days in 1985.
- **Death:** Died December 30, 1973, aged 65, after years of declining health; he had stepped down from his post in 1964 and retired in 1967 to a dacha at Komarovo on the Gulf of Finland. Buried in the Komarovo village cemetery. A specific cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Mirny. The Tepenian city Mirny stands on the site of the station he founded. Not named for him (the name comes from Lazarev's sloop *Mirny*). His expedition's Oazis station was in the Bunger Hills, where the city Relung Panen stands, though canon says Relung Panen did not grow from a station; his expedition began the scouting for Vostok, which Treshnikov's expedition founded on the site of Ariun Nuur.
- **Sources:**
  - [V. V. Lukin (AARI), "Life and feats in the ice: for the 110th anniversary of M. M. Somov," *Russian Polar Research* no. 31 (2018), in Russian](https://data.aari.ru/publicat/rpr/sources/31/42-46_Somov.pdf)
  - [Arctic and Antarctic Research Institute, "Mirny station"](https://aari.ru/expeditions/russian-antarctic-expedition/mirnyy-eng)
  - [Wikipedia, "Mikhail Somov"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Somov)

### Aleksey Treshnikov (1914-1991)
- **Life and origin:** Aleksey Fyodorovich Treshnikov (also spelled Tryoshnikov), born April 14, 1914, in the village of Pavlovka, Simbirsk Governorate, Russia, into a peasant family. He left home at 15, trained as a village schoolteacher and became a primary-school head, then went to Leningrad in 1932. He studied geography at Leningrad State University from 1934, unloading barges at night to pay his way, and chose polar oceanography under the cartographer Yuly Shokalsky. He graduated in 1939 and joined the Arctic Institute, where he worked for over 40 years. (The St. Petersburg city encyclopedia gives St. Petersburg as his birthplace; the Arctic Institute's centenary article and other sources give Pavlovka.)
- **Antarctic role:** Leader of the second Soviet Antarctic Expedition (1956-1958), which sailed on November 7, 1956, with 196 members, twice the size of the first, and ran the Soviet program for the International Geophysical Year. He took over Mirny from Somov on January 15, 1957. In 1957 he led the first Soviet tractor-sledge traverse inland to the South Geomagnetic Pole, and on December 16, 1957, under his direct leadership, Vostok Station was opened there. He returned as leader of the 13th Soviet Antarctic Expedition (1967-1969), led an Il-18 air expedition to Antarctica (1963) and a winter voyage on the research ship *Professor Zubov* (1973).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** In the Arctic he wintered on the New Siberian Islands (1940), ran ice patrols on the Northern Sea Route in World War II, was among the discoverers of the Lomonosov Ridge (his team sounded 1,005 m about 280 km from the North Pole on April 30, 1949) and led the drifting station North Pole-3 (1954-1955). He was made a Hero of Socialist Labor (1949 per the St. Petersburg encyclopedia; 1950 per Wikipedia). He directed the Arctic and Antarctic Research Institute from 1960 to 1981, became a full member of the USSR Academy of Sciences and shared a USSR State Prize for the *Atlas of the Antarctic*; he was president of the Geographical Society of the USSR until his death (from 1977 per the Institute; from 1980 per the city encyclopedia). Colleagues called him a demanding but fair chief and a kind, cheerful man. One story from the Vostok traverse: when Yevgeny Tolstikov, leader of the next expedition, worried that the tractor train would not get back to Mirny before *Ob* sailed and radioed him to stop short and found the station early, he had his radio operator stay silent, blaming poor reception, until the station was opened at the geomagnetic pole. Asked later whom he envied, he answered: "Nansen, because he had no radio and no one could stop him doing what he had planned" (translated; quoted in the Institute's centenary article).
- **What they left behind:** Vostok Station, where the world's lowest natural air temperature (−89.2 °C, July 1983) was later recorded and above which Lake Vostok was later found; a monument to him as Vostok's founder stands at its main building. He wrote over 200 works, among them a history of the discovery and exploration of Antarctica (awarded the Litke Gold Medal). The research ship *Akademik Tryoshnikov* (2012) and minor planet 3339 Treshnikov carry his name.
- **Death:** Died November 18, 1991, in Leningrad (St. Petersburg), aged 77; buried in the Komarovo cemetery, where his friend Somov also lies. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Ariun Nuur (formerly Vostok). The Tepenian city stands on the Vostok Station site he founded, and its central scientific district keeps the name Vostok "to honor the scientists of the past." Not named for him.

  - [Encyclopedia of St. Petersburg, "Treshnikov A. F."](https://www.encspb.ru/object/2804014800?lc=en)
  - [Wikipedia, "Alexey Tryoshnikov"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexey_Tryoshnikov)

### Hernán Pujato (1904-2003)
- **Life and origin:** Born June 5, 1904, at Diamante, Entre Ríos, Argentina, son of José Diego Pujato and Martina García. He was schooled in Diamante and Buenos Aires and trained as an army officer at the Colegio Militar de la Nación. As a colonel serving as military attaché in Bolivia, he presented President Juan Perón with a plan (in 1948 per *BAE Negocios*; 1949 per Wikipedia) for permanent Argentine settlement and science in Antarctica, and he visited the United States and Greenland to study survival in extreme cold.
- **Antarctic role:** He led the Army's first Antarctic expedition and founded San Martín Base in Marguerite Bay on March 21, 1951 (English Wikipedia gives March 8), the first base south of the Antarctic Circle and the world's southernmost station at the time; he commanded its first winter. He was the founding director of the Instituto Antártico Argentino, created on April 17, 1951, and described by Argentina's foreign ministry as the first institution in the world devoted solely to Antarctic study. As director he pressed for an Argentine icebreaker; the *General San Martín*, delivered in December 1954, carried him into the Weddell Sea, where he founded General Belgrano Base on the Filchner Ice Shelf on January 18, 1955, about 1,300 km from the South Pole, and made exploratory flights from it. Esperanza at Hope Bay (1952) was another Army base of the same program.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Argentine accounts treat him as the founder of the country's permanent continental presence and of its Antarctic science, and the Institute bears his name. He spent his 47th birthday at San Martín in a blizzard of over 200 km/h. His career was tied to Perón: after the 1955 coup (the Revolución Libertadora) he was removed from Antarctic work and forced to retire, and he held no major post afterward. In 1982, aged 78, he volunteered for a suicide mission against HMS *Invincible* in the Falklands War and was refused. He left no children.
- **What they left behind:** The Instituto Antártico Argentino; San Martín Base, still in use; General Belgrano Base (Belgrano I), which operated until 1980; the name passed to Belgrano II (1979).
- **Death:** Died September 7, 2003, aged 99, at the Army's Hospital Militar at Campo de Mayo, Buenos Aires province. A cause was not given. By his wish his ashes were taken to San Martín Base in 2004 and placed in a mausoleum on a small islet beside it; a great-nephew made the first family visit to the tomb in 2023.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: the Tepenian city Esperanza stands on the Esperanza Base site, set up by the Army in 1952 within his program; the city Belgrano stands on the Belgrano II site in Coats Land, which carries the name of his 1955 base but not its Filchner Ice Shelf site. San Martín Base does not appear in the Glossary.

### George J. Dufek (1903-1977)
- **Life and origin:** George John Dufek, born February 10, 1903, at Rockford, Illinois, United States. He graduated from the U.S. Naval Academy in 1925 and qualified as a naval aviator at Pensacola in 1933. In World War II he commanded the escort carrier USS *Bogue* from September 1944; its hunter-killer group sank 13 submarines in the course of the war.
- **Antarctic role:** Navigator of the barkentine USS *Bear* in Byrd's United States Antarctic Service Expedition (1939-1940); seaplane flights from the ship first sighted Thurston Island. In Operation Highjump (1946-1947), as a captain, he commanded the Eastern Task Group (the seaplane tender *Pine Island*, a tanker and a destroyer), which photographed the coast from the Getz Ice Shelf to Thurston Island; one of its flying boats crashed on Thurston Island in a whiteout on December 30, 1946, killing three of its nine crew, and the survivors were rescued on January 12, 1947. From September 1954 he planned and then commanded Operation Deep Freeze (Task Force 43), the U.S. Navy's support of American science for the International Geophysical Year, until 1959. Deep Freeze I (1955-1956) built the air facility at McMurdo Sound and made the first flights from New Zealand to Antarctica (December 20, 1955). On October 31, 1956, he flew in the ski-equipped R4D *Que Sera Sera* (pilot Conrad "Gus" Shinn, seven men aboard) to make the first aircraft landing at the South Pole; he was the first to step out, the first person to stand at the pole since Scott's party in 1912. The crew stayed about 49 minutes at roughly −60 °F with the engines running. Deep Freeze II (1956-1957) then built the South Pole station by airdrop, Byrd Station by tractor train, and stations at Cape Hallett, on the Filchner Ice Shelf (Ellsworth) and in the Windmill Islands (Wilkes).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** His Antarctican Society notice sums up his command as operations "on an unprecedented scale," with 12 ships and some 3,000 men, and says the surprise was not that there were fatal accidents but that there were not more. He wrote *Operation Deepfreeze* (1957), *Through the Frozen Frontier* (1959) and a biography of Byrd (1969). After leaving the Navy in 1959-1960 he directed the Mariners' Museum at Newport News, Virginia, until 1973.
- **What they left behind:** The permanent U.S. logistics system of McMurdo Station and the air route from Christchurch; the first South Pole station (opened 1957). The *Que Sera Sera* survives and is displayed at the National Naval Aviation Museum, Pensacola, Florida. The Dufek Coast, Dufek Massif, Dufek Head and Dufek Mountain are named for him.
- **Death:** Died of cancer on February 10, 1977, his 74th birthday, at the Bethesda Naval Hospital, Maryland. He was survived by his wife Muriel, two sons and a daughter.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: his command built the stations on the sites of three Tepenian places: Fort McMurdo (McMurdo Station), Amundsen Station (the South Pole, where he made the first landing) and Byrd (Byrd Station).

### Paul-Émile Victor (1907-1995)
- **Life and origin:** Born Paul Eugène Victor on June 28, 1907, in Geneva, Switzerland, to parents of Central European Jewish origin who had settled in France; his father, the industrialist Erich Steinschneider, of Bohemian origin, changed the family name to Victor in 1907. He left engineering school at Lyon, qualified at the merchant-marine navigation school in Marseille (1928) and took a diploma in ethnography at the Trocadéro ethnographic institute in Paris (1933).
- **Antarctic role:** Founder and director of the Expéditions polaires françaises (EPF, founded February 28, 1947; directed until 1976), which ran France's postwar return to Adélie Land, the coast Dumont d'Urville had claimed in 1840. He secured government backing for the plan of three young men (Yves Vallette, Robert Pommier and Jacques-André Martin); after a first attempt in 1948-1949 failed in the ice, the EPF landed on January 20, 1950, and an eleven-man party under André-Frank Liotard built and wintered at Port-Martin, named for Martin. Port-Martin burned down on the night of January 23, 1952, with no one hurt, and Mario Marret's party wintered instead in a small hut on Petrel Island in the Pointe Géologie archipelago (1952-1953). On the same island the EPF built Dumont d'Urville Station, opened January 12, 1956, as France's base for the International Geophysical Year, and then the inland station Charcot (about 320 km inland). French Wikipedia dates his own first visit to Adélie Land to 1956.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Before Antarctica he was a Greenland ethnographer: he went to Ammassalik in 1934 on Jean-Baptiste Charcot's *Pourquoi-Pas?*; in 1936 he crossed the Greenland ice cap from west to east by dog sled with Robert Gessain, Michel Perez and Eigil Knuth, and then lived 14 months with an Inuit family. In World War II he served with the U.S. Army Air Forces (1942-1946) as a pilot and Arctic search-and-rescue commander. The EPF under him ran some 150 expeditions to Greenland and Antarctica. A gifted popularizer through books, lectures and broadcasts, he was one of France's best-known explorers; in 1974 he cofounded, with Jacques-Yves Cousteau, Alain Bombard and others, a group for the defense of people and their environment. A saying often attributed to him in France ("What keeps us from realizing our dreams is not who we are, but who we believe we are not") circulates widely; no original source was found.
- **What they left behind:** Dumont d'Urville Station, France's permanent Antarctic base; the ruins of Port-Martin; the French polar program itself, now the Institut polaire français Paul-Émile Victor (IPEV), which runs Dumont d'Urville and shares Concordia Station with Italy.
- **Death:** Died March 7, 1995, aged 87, on Bora Bora, French Polynesia, where he had lived on a small islet since 1977. By his wish he was buried at sea from the French Navy ship *Dumont d'Urville*. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Dumont d'Urville. The Tepenian city stands on the Dumont d'Urville Station site on Petrel Island, Pointe Géologie, which his expeditions founded; the city is named for Jules Dumont d'Urville (St. Jules), not for him, and canon gives it Japanese founders.

### Takesi Nagata (1913-1991)
- **Life and origin:** Takesi (Takeshi) Nagata, born June 24, 1913, at Okazaki, Aichi Prefecture, Japan. He graduated from Tokyo Imperial University in March 1936 and joined its Earthquake Research Institute, where he began experimental work on the magnetism of rocks; his doctoral thesis (1944) was on the natural remanent magnetization of volcanic rocks. He became a professor of geophysics at the University of Tokyo and is regarded as a founder of the field of rock magnetism.
- **Antarctic role:** As secretary of Japan's national committee for the International Geophysical Year from June 1952, he handled its national and international negotiations; Japan's Antarctic expedition became its main IGY field project. He was leader of the first Japanese Antarctic Research Expedition (1956-1957), which sailed in the ship *Sōya* and set up Syowa Station on East Ongul Island in Lützow-Holm Bay on January 29, 1957; he led the first three expeditions in all (1956-1957 to 1958-1959). On the second (1957-1958) *Sōya* was trapped in the ice; the U.S. icebreaker *Burton Island* came to help (the ships met on February 7, 1958), and the first wintering party was flown out of Syowa in an emergency evacuation, leaving 15 Sakhalin sled dogs chained at the base because a relief party was expected. No new wintering party could be landed. When the third expedition reached Syowa in January 1959, two of the dogs, Taro and Jiro, were alive; seven were found dead and six were never found. When the National Institute of Polar Research was founded in 1973, he became its first director-general (until 1984 per Japanese Wikipedia).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** A geophysicist of international standing: Japan Academy Prize (1951), Order of Culture (1974), Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society (1987) and foreign membership of the U.S. National Academy of Sciences. The expedition's cat on *Sōya* was named Takeshi after him.
- **What they left behind:** Syowa Station, Japan's main Antarctic station ever since; the National Institute of Polar Research and Japan's continuing Antarctic program; the story of Taro and Jiro, retold in the film *Nankyoku Monogatari* (*Antarctica*, 1983) and commemorated by statues in Japan. Mount Nagata in Antarctica is named for him.
- **Death:** Died June 3, 1991, aged 77. The place and cause of death were not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Temirötkel (formerly Sayowa). The Tepenian city stands on the mainland Sôya Coast of Lützow-Holm Bay with East Ongul Island, the Syowa Station site he founded, as its port; the old name came from Syowa (Shōwa), an era name, not from him.

### Gaston de Gerlache (1919-2006)
- **Life and origin:** Baron Gaston de Gerlache de Gomery, born November 17, 1919, in Brussels, Belgium, the second son of Adrien de Gerlache, commander of the *Belgica* (1897-1899). At 12 he went on an Arctic cruise with his parents and sister; his father, "a modest and taciturn man," told him little about his own polar work. He meant to follow the family's military tradition, but World War II interrupted his studies: he was taken prisoner, released, joined the resistance, escaped to England and flew as a Royal Air Force pilot at the end of the war. He flew on in the Belgian Air Force (later a reserve lieutenant colonel), then qualified as a lawyer, became legal adviser in the textile industry and ended his career as president of a large insurance group; he was also mayor of his community for many years.
- **Antarctic role:** After a first field season as an observer with the French expedition in Adélie Land, he organized and led the Belgian Antarctic Expedition of 1957-1958, Belgium's contribution to the International Geophysical Year. It built King Baudouin Base (Base Roi Baudouin) on the ice shelf of the Princess Ragnhild Coast, Queen Maud Land (70°26′S, 24°18′E), in 1958, exactly 60 years after his father's voyage, and he raised the old flag of the *Belgica* at the new station. The expedition discovered a new range and named it the Belgica Mountains. In December 1958 four of the expedition's men came down in their small Auster plane on a reconnaissance flight about 250 km from the base (Wikipedia gives December 11, in the Crystal Mountains); with no radio contact they set out to walk back, and on December 16 a Soviet pilot, V. M. Perov, flew across from Mirny on the far side of the continent and rescued them. He was a Belgian delegate at the 1959 Washington conference, where Belgium became one of the 12 original signatories of the Antarctic Treaty. The base closed for lack of government money in 1961; it was reopened in 1964 through his efforts for three Belgian-Dutch expeditions, which he oversaw as chairman of the joint committee, and he led a large Belgian summer party himself in 1964-1965.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** In his obituary (Claude De Broyer, *Polish Polar Research*, 2008) he is "the most prominent 'Antarctician' in Belgium," with a "great presence," who for a long time personified Belgium's involvement in Antarctic affairs; not a scientist himself, he was keenly aware of the science. He liked to say that "a well prepared expedition is already half successful." He chaired the Belgian Royal Academies' National Committee on Antarctic Research for eight years and hosted the 1998 centenary of the *Belgica* expedition. He wrote *Retour dans l'Antarctique* (1960) and made the documentary film *Plein sud* (1961). Awards included the U.S. Antarctic Service Medal and the Bellingshausen Medal of the Soviet Academy of Sciences.
- **What they left behind:** King Baudouin Base (1958-1961 and 1964-1967, since abandoned); Belgium's seat among the original treaty states; the Belgica Mountains on the map. Mount Gaston de Gerlache in the Queen Fabiola Mountains is named for him. His children and grandchildren continued the family's polar work; Belgium's present station, Princess Elisabeth Antarctica (opened 2009), followed in the same region of Queen Maud Land.
- **Death:** Died July 13, 2006, at Oudenaarde, East Flanders, Belgium, aged 86 (his home was at Mullem, part of Oudenaarde). A cause of death was not found. He was survived by his wife Lily and five children.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Note: the Tepenian city Utstein stands on the site of Belgium's later Princess Elisabeth station in the Sør Rondane Mountains, not on the King Baudouin Base site.

  - [Wikipedia, "Gaston de Gerlache"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_de_Gerlache)

### John Rymill (1905-1968)
- **Life and origin:** John Riddoch Rymill, born March 13, 1905, at Penola, South Australia, into the family that owned Penola Station, a pastoral property. He was schooled at Melbourne Grammar School and in 1927 went to London, where he trained in surveying and navigation through the Royal Geographical Society and earned a pilot's license.
- **Antarctic role:** Organizer and leader of the British Graham Land Expedition (1934-1937), a small private party of 16 men that sailed in the three-masted schooner *Penola* to the west coast of the Antarctic Peninsula. It wintered first in the Argentine Islands (1935) and then in the Debenham Islands in Marguerite Bay (1936), using dog sledges and a single light aircraft. Its sledge and air surveys found a long, permanently frozen channel, later named George VI Sound, and showed that Graham Land is a peninsula of the continent, not an archipelago, disproving the channels Hubert Wilkins had reported from the air in 1928. The party came home after almost three years with no deaths or serious accidents.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Before Antarctica he was surveyor and pilot on Gino Watkins's British Arctic Air Route Expedition to Greenland (1930-1931) and second-in-command of Watkins's East Greenland expedition (1932-1933), which he led after Watkins's death in Tuttilik Fjord (he was lost while hunting alone in his kayak). The Encyclopedia of Australian Science counts the BGLE among the most successful and best-organized polar expeditions of its time. He received the Polar Medal with Arctic and Antarctic bars, the Royal Geographical Society's Founder's Medal (1938) and the American Geographical Society's David Livingstone Centenary Medal (1939). He wrote the expedition account *Southern Lights* (1938).
- **What they left behind:** The first accurate map of the southwest Antarctic Peninsula and George VI Sound. The Rymill Coast in Palmer Land and Rymill Bay in Graham Land are named for him. Argentina's San Martín Base (founded 1951 by Hernán Pujato) stands in the same Debenham Islands as his second winter base.
- **Death:** After the expedition he married the geographer Eleanor Mary Francis, returned to manage the family estate at Penola, served on the district council and, in World War II, held a commission in the Royal Australian Naval Volunteer Reserve. He died on September 7, 1968, at Penola, aged 63, after a car accident; he was survived by his wife and two sons.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

**Early Argentine Antarctic figures**

### José María Sobral (1880-1961)
- **Life and origin:** Born in 1880 at Gualeguaychú, Entre Ríos, Argentina; the day is given as April 14 (Wikipedia) or April 1 (*El Heraldo*, Concordia). He trained at the Argentine naval school and was an *alférez* (ensign) of 21 when he went south.
- **Antarctic role:** When Otto Nordenskjöld invited Argentina to take part in his Swedish Antarctic Expedition, the Navy named Sobral, on the suggestion of the explorer Francisco P. Moreno, to join it as meteorological observer. He embarked on the expedition ship *Antarctic* (captain Carl Anton Larsen) in Buenos Aires and in February 1902 was landed with Nordenskjöld's six-man wintering party at Snow Hill Island, off the east side of the Antarctic Peninsula, where he made meteorological, magnetic, geodetic and other observations. The *Antarctic* was crushed in the ice and sank in February 1903, so the Snow Hill party had to winter a second time, unplanned. They were rescued on November 8, 1903, by the Argentine corvette *Uruguay* under Julián Irízar and reached Buenos Aires on December 2, 1903. He was the first Argentine to winter in Antarctica and to do science there.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Argentine sources call him the father of Argentine Antarctica. Drawn to geology by the expedition, he asked the Navy for leave to study it; when that was refused he resigned his commission and went to Uppsala University, Sweden, where he took a doctorate in 1913, the first Argentine geologist with that degree. Swedish scientists named a mineral, "sobralite" (later found to be pyroxmangite), for him. He married a Swede, Elna Klingström, in 1906, and they had nine children. He was national director of mines and hydrology (Wikipedia gives 1914-1924), consul in Norway (1930) and later worked for the state oil company YPF; he retired in 1935 but went on lecturing. He wrote an account of the expedition, *Dos años entre los hielos* (1904).
- **What they left behind:** Argentina's claim to have been present in Antarctic science from 1902; his observation records from Snow Hill. Base Alférez de Navío Sobral (1965, on the Filchner Ice Shelf) and the patrol ship ARA *Alférez Sobral* are named for him. Nordenskjöld's Snow Hill hut, where he lived for two winters, survives as a historic site.
- **Death:** Died April 14, 1961, in Buenos Aires, on his 81st birthday by the Wikipedia date. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: Snow Hill Island, where he wintered, lies on Highway 1's causeway chain from Marambio across Picnic Passage, in the Glossary.

### Julián Irízar (1869-1935)
- **Life and origin:** Born January 7, 1869, at Capilla del Señor, Buenos Aires Province, Argentina. He entered the Argentine Naval Academy on March 11, 1884. He became an explosives specialist; in 1898 he oversaw the building of the training frigate ARA *Presidente Sarmiento* in England and served as a staff officer on her first voyage around the world (1899). Before 1903 he was naval attaché in Britain and Germany.
- **Antarctic role:** When nothing was heard from the Swedish Antarctic Expedition, Argentina adapted the old corvette ARA *Uruguay* (built 1874) for the ice and gave Irízar, then a *capitán de corbeta* (lieutenant commander), its command. The *Uruguay* left Buenos Aires on October 8, 1903. Near Seymour Island, close to the site of today's Marambio Base, its men sighted a tent; Irízar went ashore and met two men of the Swedish expedition, and on November 8, 1903, the rescuers reached Nordenskjöld's wintering party at Snow Hill Island, among them the Argentine José María Sobral. The *Uruguay* then went on to Paulet Island to take off the rest of the castaways from the sunken expedition ship *Antarctic*, and was back in Buenos Aires on December 2, 1903, to a celebrated welcome.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** The voyage is treated in Argentina as a national feat and the start of the country's continuous Antarctic activity; it brought Argentina international notice for a humanitarian rescue. He was promoted to *capitán de fragata* (commander) for it. Later he commanded the Navy's First Division, supervised the modernization of Argentine battleships in the United States (1923), became a vice admiral (1926), presided over the Centro Naval from 1931, and was named National Maritime Prefect in 1932; he retired on January 8, 1932, after nearly 50 years of service.
- **What they left behind:** The *Uruguay* itself survives as a museum ship in Buenos Aires, the oldest ship of the Argentine Navy afloat. The icebreaker ARA *Almirante Irízar* (in service from 1978-1979), the main supply ship of Argentina's Antarctic bases, is named for him, as are Irízar Island and Cape Irízar in Antarctica.
- **Death:** Died March 17, 1935, in Buenos Aires, aged 66; buried in the Recoleta Cemetery. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: the rescue began on Seymour Island, the site of the Tepenian city Marambio, and ended at Snow Hill Island, on the Highway 1 causeway chain from Marambio in the Glossary.

### Hugo Alberto Acuña (1885-1953)
- **Life and origin:** Born May 26, 1885, in Buenos Aires, Argentina. At 18 he was a junior employee of the Ministry of Agriculture, which ran Argentina's weather service, and he volunteered for the South Orkney posting; the foreign ministry's history calls him "the young volunteer Hugo Acuña, postal agent."
- **Antarctic role:** One of the three Argentines in the first party to take over the weather and magnetic observatory on Laurie Island, South Orkney Islands, which William Speirs Bruce's Scottish expedition had built in 1903 and handed to Argentina. He sailed south on Bruce's *Scotia* in January 1904 with Edgar Szmula (meteorological office) and Luciano Valette (zoology office); the year's party was led by the Scot Robert Mossman, the *Scotia*'s meteorologist. On February 22, 1904, Acuña raised the Argentine flag at the observatory, the act Argentina dates its permanent Antarctic presence from. He wrote that day: "We are in our own house" (translated; quoted by Meteored). He served as meteorological assistant and as postmaster of the first post office in Antarctica, which he opened that year. The party was relieved and he left on January 1, 1905, aboard the corvette *Uruguay*.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He kept a diary in a black notebook through the year; published much later (most recently as *Diario del estafeta Hugo A. Acuña, pionero de la soberanía argentina en la Antártida*, 2015), it is counted among the first works of Argentine Antarctic writing and records the hard daily work and the storms (on March 8, 1904, giant waves broke down a snow wall built against the hut). Argentine accounts made him a national hero. Details of his later life and career were not found.
- **What they left behind:** Orcadas Base, which Argentina has run without a break since 1904, the oldest continuously operating station in Antarctica, with its post office still open. February 22 is kept in Argentina as Antarctic Day (Día de la Antártida Argentina) in memory of the flag raising. A bust of him was unveiled on February 22, 2016, on the Antarctic Pioneers Walk in Ushuaia.
- **Death:** Died May 13, 1953, at Vicente López, Buenos Aires Province, aged 67. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. Note: Orcadas Base is on Laurie Island in the South Orkneys, the archipelago of the Tepenian city Signy (on Signy Island); Orcadas does not appear in the Glossary.
- **Sources:**

**Firsts**

### Caroline Mikkelsen (1906-1998), with Klarius Mikkelsen
- **Life and origin:** Born November 20, 1906, in Denmark. She married the Norwegian whaling captain Klarius Mikkelsen and moved to Norway. Klarius commanded the tanker *Thorshavn*, which supplied the Antarctic whaling fleets of the Sandefjord shipowner Lars Christensen and carried out Christensen's exploring and claim-making program; his own birth date and origin were not found.
- **Antarctic role:** She sailed with her husband on the *Thorshavn*'s 1934-1935 voyage. On February 20, 1935, a boat party of the captain, his wife and seven crew went ashore off the coast of East Antarctica; they landed supplies as a depot, built a rock cairn and raised the Norwegian flag on it, and Caroline helped with the flag and the cairn. For 60 years she was counted as the first woman to set foot on Antarctica and on the mainland. In 1995 personnel from Australia's Davis Station relocated the cairn and found it on the Tryne Islands, about 5 km off the mainland; Australian researchers set this out in articles in *Polar Record*, and the first landing by a woman on the mainland passed to Ingrid Christensen's party (1937). Guinness World Records still lists Mikkelsen as the first woman to set foot on Antarctica. Klarius's 1935 voyage is credited with discovering and naming the Vestfold Hills that same day, after Vestfold, the Norwegian county of the whaling town Sandefjord.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** She did not speak publicly about the voyage until 1995, sixty years after it, when she told her story to the Norwegian newspaper *Aftenposten*. She is remembered for the single landing rather than for any further polar career. Klarius Mikkelsen is remembered as one of Lars Christensen's captains; sources differ on his death (Earth Magazine gives 1937; Wikipedia implies 1941, when Caroline's first husband died).
- **What they left behind:** The 1935 cairn on the Tryne Islands; the name Vestfold Hills. Mount Caroline Mikkelsen in Antarctica is named for her.
- **Death:** Caroline Mikkelsen died in 1998 in Norway, aged 91 or 92 (she had remarried, to Johan Mandel, in 1944). The exact date, place and cause were not found. Klarius Mikkelsen died in 1937 or 1941 (see above); place and cause not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Davis (indirectly). The Tepenian city Davis stands on the Davis Station site in the Vestfold Hills, the oasis Klarius named; the Tryne Islands landing site lies about 5 km offshore. The city is named for John King Davis, not for either Mikkelsen.

  - [Earth Magazine, "Benchmarks: February 5, 1931, and February 20, 1935: Antarctic firsts for women"](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-february-5-1931-and-february-20-1935-antarctic-firsts-women/)
  - [Norwegian Polar Institute, "Did you know that the first woman in Antarctica was Norwegian?"](https://sorpolen2011.npolar.no/en/did-you-know/2011-11-02-the-first-woman-in-antarctica-was-norwegian.html)
  - [*Polar Record*, "Klarius Mikkelsen's 1935 landing in the Vestfold Hills, East Antarctica: some fiction and some facts" (abstract page; not readable on November 1, 2026)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/polar-record/article/abs/klarius-mikkelsens-1935-landing-in-the-vestfold-hills-east-antarctica-some-fiction-and-some-facts/634BD34AE0FB97414945D943E80B05D6)

### Ingrid Christensen (1891-1976)
- **Life and origin:** Born Ingrid Dahl on October 10, 1891, at Sandefjord, Norway, daughter of the merchant and shipowner Thor Dahl and Alfhild Freng Dahl. In 1910 she married the whaling shipowner Lars Christensen (surveyed in the first round); they had six children.
- **Antarctic role:** She made four voyages to Antarctic waters with her husband on the tanker *Thorshavn*. On February 5, 1931, with her companion Mathilde Wegger, she sighted and named Bjerkö Head, which makes them the first women on record to see Antarctica. In 1933 (with Lillemor Rachlew) and 1933-1934 (with Ingebjørg Dedichen, a voyage that went around the continent) bad conditions kept her from landing. On the 1936-1937 voyage, with her daughter Augusta Sofie Christensen, Lillemor Rachlew and Solveig Widerøe, she flew over part of the continent in the expedition's seaplane and dropped a Norwegian flag, the first woman to see Antarctica from the air, and on January 30, 1937, the four women landed at Scullin Monolith in Mac.Robertson Land. Since the 1995 relocation of the Mikkelsen cairn on an offshore island, she is credited as the first woman to set foot on the Antarctic mainland, and the four as the first group of women to land there.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** In her time the landing drew little notice, partly because Caroline Mikkelsen's 1935 landing was then believed to be the first; her standing as a "first" is largely a late-20th-century revision. She received Norway's Order of St. Olav, Knight First Class, in 1946. The four women's voyages are the subject of Jesse Blackadder's novel *Chasing the Light* (2013).
- **What they left behind:** The Ingrid Christensen Coast of East Antarctica, named for her in 1935, between Jennings Promontory and the western end of the West Ice Shelf; it takes in the Vestfold Hills and the Larsemann Hills.
- **Death:** Died June 18, 1976, aged 84. The place and cause were not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for her in canon. Note: the coast named for her holds the sites of three Tepenian cities: Davis (Vestfold Hills) and Sinheung and Zhongshan (Larsemann Hills). Her landing site, Scullin Monolith, is not a canon place.

### Edith "Jackie" Ronne (1919-2009)
- **Life and origin:** Born Edith Ann Maslin on October 13, 1919, in Baltimore, Maryland, United States. She began college at the College of Wooster, Ohio, and studied history at George Washington University, then worked in several posts at the U.S. State Department. She married the Norwegian-born explorer Finn Ronne (surveyed in the first round) on March 18, 1941, per Wikipedia.
- **Antarctic role:** She went south with her husband's privately funded Ronne Antarctic Research Expedition (1947-1948), based at the old U.S. East Base on Stonington Island, Marguerite Bay. Her husband needed her to write the dispatches for the North American Newspaper Alliance, one of the expedition's sponsors, and she stayed as the expedition's recorder and historian. She thus became the first American woman to set foot on Antarctica and the first woman to work as an official member of an Antarctic expedition. She and Jennie Darlington, the chief pilot's wife, were the first women to winter in Antarctica, spending about 15 months on Stonington Island with 21 men. Finn Ronne named the newly mapped land at the head of the Weddell Sea "Edith Ronne Land" for her; it is now the Ronne Ice Shelf.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Her daily records underlay her husband's book *Antarctic Conquest* (1949). In her own account she defended his leadership and put complaints about his "disciplined Norwegian regimen" down to the grievances any such winter breeds (Mystic Seaport's summary). She returned to Antarctica some 15 times, including a 1971 Navy-sponsored flight to the South Pole marking the 60th anniversary of Amundsen's arrival, and in 1995 went back to Stonington Island as a cruise-ship lecturer. She was president of the Society of Woman Geographers (1978-1981) and a fellow of The Explorers Club, and published her own account, *Antarctica's First Lady*, in 2004.
- **What they left behind:** The Ronne Ice Shelf, one of the largest ice shelves in Antarctica; the expedition's records and her writing.
- **Death:** Died June 14, 2009, aged 89, in a nursing home at Bethesda, Maryland, of cancer and Alzheimer's disease (the *Philadelphia Inquirer*; Wikipedia gives Alzheimer's disease).
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Neither Stonington Island nor the Ronne Ice Shelf appears in the Glossary.)

### Jennie Darlington (1924-2017)
- **Life and origin:** Born Jennie Orvalla Zobrist on January 25, 1924, in Baltimore, Maryland, United States; her father was a Swiss immigrant who built roads and tunnels. In 1946 she married Harry Darlington III, who became chief pilot of the Ronne expedition.
- **Antarctic role:** She meant to go only as far as Valparaíso, Chile, the expedition's last port, but joined the Ronne Antarctic Research Expedition (1947-1948) there, at Jackie Ronne's request. From March 1947 to February 1948 she lived with Ronne and 21 men in a one-room prewar bunkhouse at East Base, Stonington Island, Marguerite Bay. She had no official role; her obituary calls her the expedition's unofficial morale officer. With Jackie Ronne she was one of the first two women to winter in Antarctica, and she is recorded as the first woman to become pregnant there (her daughter was born July 22, 1948, after the return).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Asked why she went, she answered: "because it was the most beautiful, dangerous place in the world and because I was glad to be with my husband" (quoted in her obituary). The winter was marked by a feud between Finn Ronne and her husband over aircraft safety, which ended in Harry Darlington's removal as chief pilot; the two wives "did not get on," which Wikipedia suggests may have reflected their husbands' quarrel. Her account, *My Antarctic Honeymoon: A Year at the Bottom of the World* (with Jane McIlvaine; Doubleday, 1956 per her obituary, 1957 per Mystic Seaport), gives "a rather unflattering portrayal" of both Ronnes (Mystic Seaport) and, in Wikipedia's summary, concludes that "women do not belong in Antarctica"; she later wrote an unpublished sequel. Afterward she ran Chilly Bleak Farm near Marshall, Virginia, raising over 200,000 chickens as well as cattle, turkeys and sheep; she was an amateur pilot and sailboat racer, kept her bedrooms cold all her life and took a penguin as her personal emblem.
- **What they left behind:** *My Antarctic Honeymoon*, a firsthand account of the first winter spent by women in Antarctica. The East Base buildings on Stonington Island survive as a protected historic site.
- **Death:** Died August 30, 2017, aged 93, at her home, Chilly Bleak Farm, near Marshall, Virginia, of heart ailments. She was survived by a son and a daughter; Harry Darlington had died in 1996.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Gabriel González Videla (1898-1980)
- **Life and origin:** Born November 22, 1898, at La Serena, Chile, the eldest of 18 children of Gabriel González Castillo and Teresa Videla Zepeda. He took his law degree at the University of Chile (1922). A Radical Party politician, he was a deputy (1930-1941), president of the Chamber of Deputies (1933), party leader (1931-1937), ambassador to France and Brazil among other posts, and senator (1945-1946). He married Rosa Markmann Reijer; they had three children.
- **Antarctic role:** As President of Chile (1946-1952) he made the first visit by a head of state to Antarctica, in February 1948, to assert the Chilean Antarctic Territory that his predecessor's government had declared by decree in 1940, against British, Argentine and other claims. Sailing with the frigate *Covadonga* and the oil transport *Rancagua*, and accompanied by his wife, daughters, politicians and officers, he reached Soberanía Base (now Arturo Prat) on Greenwich Island, South Shetlands, on February 17, 1948, and decorated its men for serving "with valor, abnegation and sacrifice" (translated, as reported by *El Mostrador*). On February 18, 1948, he inaugurated Base General Bernardo O'Higgins, Chile's second permanent station, at Cape Legoupil on the Trinity Peninsula.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** In Chile the visit is remembered as a landmark of national sovereignty; the ceremonies were broadcast nationwide, and the party was served a banquet of Antarctic foods (krill, penguin soup, seal fillet). His wider record is contested. Having governed at first with Communist ministers, he turned against them in 1948 with the Law for the Permanent Defense of Democracy (known to opponents as the *Ley Maldita*, "the accursed law"), which outlawed the Communist Party and struck its members from the electoral rolls. In 1952 he signed the Santiago Declaration claiming a 200-nautical-mile maritime zone. Later he opposed Salvador Allende's government and supported the 1973 military coup.
- **What they left behind:** O'Higgins Base, still in use; an early claim to the 200-mile maritime zone. Chile's Antarctic base on the Danco Coast, Base Presidente Gabriel González Videla (opened 1951), is named for him.
- **Death:** Died August 22, 1980, in Santiago, aged 81. A cause of death was not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Neither O'Higgins Base nor Arturo Prat appears in the Glossary.)

### Lloyd Berkner (1905-1967)
- **Life and origin:** Lloyd Viel Berkner, born February 1, 1905, in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, United States, and raised in Sleepy Eye, Minnesota. By 14 he ran the amateur radio station 9AWM and set relay records. He graduated in electrical engineering from the University of Minnesota in 1927; he never took a doctorate, though he later received 12 honorary ones.
- **Antarctic role:** Radio engineer on Richard Byrd's first Antarctic expedition (1928-1930): he set up the radio links between Little America and the outside world and flew on the expedition's first reconnaissance flight, but did not winter. His lasting Antarctic role came later. At a dinner at James Van Allen's home in Silver Spring, Maryland, on April 5, 1950, he proposed to Sydney Chapman and others a third International Polar Year, 25 years after the second instead of 50, on the model of the first (1882-1883) and second (1932-1933). The idea went to the International Council of Scientific Unions, which set up the Special Committee for the International Geophysical Year (CSAGI) on May 16, 1952; Berkner was its vice president under Chapman. The IGY ran from July 1, 1957, to December 31, 1958, and grew from 23 to 67 participating nations. Antarctica was one of the IGY's main fields: twelve nations set up research stations there, and the same twelve signed the Antarctic Treaty in 1959.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** He studied the ionosphere at the Bureau of Standards (from 1928) and at the Carnegie Institution's Department of Terrestrial Magnetism (1933-1939), and in World War II he organized the Navy's radar section and led naval aviation electronics engineering. He was president of Associated Universities, Inc., which ran Brookhaven National Laboratory (1951-1960), president of the International Council of Scientific Unions (from 1955 per his memoir), first chairman of the U.S. Space Science Board (1958) and president of the American Geophysical Union (1959). In 1961 he founded the Graduate Research Center of the Southwest in Dallas, which became the University of Texas at Dallas in 1969. His National Academy of Sciences memoir calls him "a man of vision," large in body and in thought, and quotes a colleague: "more than anyone else he brought new life and vigor into international cooperation." His *Arctic* obituary (J. C. Reed, 1967) records the epithet "Father of the International Geophysical Year."
- **What they left behind:** The IGY itself, the scientific basis of the station era and of the Antarctic Treaty; the University of Texas at Dallas. Berkner Island, the large ice-covered island in the Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf, is named for him.
- **Death:** He had a severe heart attack in June 1964 and kept working. He died of a heart attack on June 4, 1967, aged 62, in Washington, D.C., while attending a National Academy of Sciences Council meeting; he was buried in Arlington National Cemetery on June 7, 1967.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note: the IGY he proposed produced or expanded the real stations on many Tepenian city sites, among them Mirny, Vostok (Ariun Nuur), Syowa (Temirötkel's port), Dumont d'Urville, McMurdo (Fort McMurdo), the South Pole (Amundsen Station), Byrd, Halley and Davis.
- **Sources:**
  - [National Academy of Sciences, *Biographical Memoirs* vol. 61 (1992), "Lloyd Viel Berkner"](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/2037/chapter/3)
  - [NASA History, "70 years ago: scientists establish the International Geophysical Year"](https://www.nasa.gov/history/70-years-ago-scientists-establish-the-international-geophysical-year)
  - [J. C. Reed, "Lloyd V. Berkner (1905-1967)," obituary, *Arctic* (1967)](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/view/66341)

### Francis Crozier (1796-1848?)
- **Life and origin:** Francis Rawdon Moira Crozier, born in Banbridge, County Down, Ireland, the son of the attorney George Crozier and Jane Graham, and named for his father's friend the Earl of Moira. Sources differ on the date: October 17, 1796 (Wikipedia), or September 1796 (Dictionary of Falklands Biography); a *Polar Record* note is devoted to the question. He joined the Royal Navy in June 1810, aged 13. He served on William Edward Parry's Arctic voyages in search of the Northwest Passage (1821-1825) and on Parry's 1827 attempt on the North Pole from Spitsbergen, and became a close friend of James Clark Ross. Promoted commander in 1837.
- **Antarctic role:** Captain of HMS *Terror* and second-in-command of James Clark Ross's Antarctic expedition with HMS *Erebus* (1839-1843), sent for magnetic research and geographical discovery. The expedition found the Ross Sea, the Victoria Land coast, Ross Island with Mount Erebus and Mount Terror (named for the two ships), McMurdo Sound and the Ross Ice Shelf. Ross named Cape Crozier, the eastern cape of Ross Island, for him. He was promoted post captain while the ships wintered in the Falkland Islands (1842), on Ross's recommendation. He also collected about 150 bird specimens, later given to the Belfast Museum.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** An experienced and capable polar seaman, in Ross's shadow by rank; History Ireland writes that he "captained *Terror* and never lost a man." Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1843 for his magnetic work. He proposed more than once to Sophia Cracroft, Sir John Franklin's niece, and was refused; History Ireland's line is that "she liked the man, but not the sailor." He went north again as captain of *Terror* on Franklin's 1845 Northwest Passage expedition and took command when Franklin died in June 1847.
- **What they left behind:** Cape Crozier, where Edward Wilson, Henry Bowers and Apsley Cherry-Garrard made the 1911 "worst journey in the world" to the emperor penguin colony; Crozier Strait in the Canadian Arctic; a lunar crater; Crozier Place in Stanley, Falkland Islands; a monument opposite his family home in Banbridge. The Franklin crews' retreat completed the last unknown link of the Northwest Passage.
- **Death:** unknown. The last written record is the Victory Point note of April 25, 1848, found on King William Island in 1859: the ships had been abandoned in the ice, 9 officers (Franklin among them) and 15 men were dead, and the survivors under Crozier would set out on April 26 for the Canadian mainland. None survived; History Ireland describes a retreat marked by starvation and scurvy and believes he was "among the last to die." Inuit accounts gathered in 1852-1858 suggested that Crozier and one other man might have been seen near Baker Lake; this is unconfirmed.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found. (Cape Crozier is on Ross Island, the island of the cities Fort McMurdo and Scott, but the Glossary does not name it.)
- **Sources:**

  - [Remembering "the Captain", History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/?p=7369)
  - [Francis Crozier (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crozier)

### Samuel R. Knox (1811-1883)
- **Life and origin:** Born in 1811 in Charlestown, Massachusetts, son of Robert and Ann (Richardson) Knox; schooled in Boston. He entered the U.S. Navy on April 1, 1828, served in the frigate *Constitution*, then in the Pacific, including the frigate *Guerriere*, until 1833.
- **Antarctic role:** an officer of the U.S. Exploring Expedition under Charles Wilkes (1838-1842). When the schooner *Flying Fish* joined the squadron at Hampton Roads on August 12, 1838, Passed Midshipman Knox was put in command of her. During the 1840 Antarctic cruise he served as acting master of the flagship *Vincennes*; by October 1840, at Honolulu, he was acting master of the *Porpoise*. The coast he is named for was discovered by the expedition in February 1840, and Wilkes named it the Knox Coast. It runs from Cape Hordern (100°31′E) to the Hatch Islands (109°16′E) in Wilkes Land.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** a career naval officer known mainly through this naming. Commissioned lieutenant in 1841. After the expedition he served at the Boston Navy Yard and in the Mediterranean in the *Plymouth*. In the Mexican War (1846-1848) he served in the *Albany* off Veracruz and commanded smaller vessels; a letter of his to his brother Robert about the naval war survives. The record holds no judgment of his character.
- **What they left behind:** the Knox Coast; a Mexican War letter (Briscoe Center for American History, University of Texas) and papers listed under his name.
- **Death:** November 21, 1883. He had retired to Everett, Massachusetts, living with his brother and sisters; he never married. Cause of death not found.
- **Tepenian place:** Relung Panen (formerly "Bunger Hills City", `DR-39`) stands in the Bunger Hills oasis on the Knox Coast, in the Mirny subnet. The city is not named for him.

### William Smith (1790-1847)
- **Life and origin:** English merchant captain from the Blyth area of Northumberland, eldest son of William Smith, a joiner from Seaton Sluice, and Mary Smith. Sources give October 11, 1790, at Seaton Sluice (British Antarctic Territory material and the ship-stamp record) or a baptism on October 10, 1790, at St. Cuthbert's, Earsdon parish (Wikipedia, from the parish register). He grew up and was schooled in Blyth. From about age 14 he served a seven-year apprenticeship, by one early account "in the Greenland whale-fishery." By 1811 he was part owner of the brig *Williams* (Wikipedia spells it *William*), then being built at Blyth, and later traded in her between the Río de la Plata and Chile.
- **Antarctic role:** On February 19, 1819, carrying cargo from Buenos Aires to Valparaíso, he swung far south around Cape Horn for better winds and sighted land at about 62°S, 60°W, at Livingston Island (Williams Point is named for the sighting). He called it New South Britain. The naval authorities at Valparaíso did not believe him; one account says they "ridiculed the poor man for his fanciful credulity." On a later voyage he landed on October 16, 1819, at Esther Harbour on King George Island, the first recorded landing in today's British Antarctic Territory. His party "planted a board with the Union Jack... and an appropriate inscription," taking possession for the King. He named the island King George Island and the group the South Shetland Islands. In January 1820 the Royal Navy chartered his ship to survey the islands under Edward Bransfield, with Smith aboard, and that voyage sighted the Antarctic Peninsula (see Bransfield in the first round). He reported "a great abundance of whales and seals," and he went back sealing there, reaching London in September 1821 with a large cargo of fur seal pelts.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** remembered as the discoverer of the South Shetlands, whose report set off the sealing rush of 1820-1822 that brought Palmer and others south. Little personal detail survives.
- **What they left behind:** the names King George Island and South Shetland Islands; Smith Island and Cape Smith in the South Shetlands and Williams Point on Livingston Island are named for him and his ship. In December 2019, for the bicentenary, a sculpture to him was unveiled in Blyth by the Duchess of Northumberland, and a plaque outside the Blyth Tall Ships workshop tells his story; British Antarctic Territory issued a 2019 stamp set.
- **Death:** 1847, aged about 56, in poverty in London. On his return in 1821 he found his co-owners in financial trouble and was bankrupted with them. He settled in London as a pilot on the Thames and was later given alms-house accommodation, where he died about seven years after. Exact date and cause not found.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. The Palmer subnet covers the South Shetlands, where the cities Pergamino (Livingston Island, the island he first sighted) and Contrapunto (King George Island, the island he landed on and named) stand, along with Machu Picchu Airport on King George Island.

### John Davis (1784-?), the American sealer of 1821
- **Life and origin:** Born in 1784 in Surrey, England; an American sailor and sealer based in Connecticut. Nothing more about his origin or training was found. (Not to be confused with John King Davis, the namesake of the city Davis, or with John Edward Davis, second master of HMS *Terror* under Crozier, whose drawings illustrated Ross's account.)
- **Antarctic role:** Captain of the New Haven sealing ship *Huron* on a sealing voyage to the South Shetlands (1820-1822), sailing from New Haven in 1820, in company with Christopher Burdick's *Huntress* of Nantucket. The *Huron* carried the shallop *Cecilia* as a tender, and Davis took the *Cecilia* south from the South Shetlands to look for new sealing grounds. On February 7, 1821, some of his crew went ashore at Hughes Bay (about 64°01′S) on the west coast of the Antarctic Peninsula, "on Shore to look for Seal," for less than an hour. That is the earliest recorded claim of a landing on the Antarctic continent. They found no seals worth taking. The log entry ends: "I think this Southern Land to be a Continent." The *Huron* and *Cecilia* wintered in the Falklands in 1821 and returned to the South Shetlands for the 1821-1822 summer.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** known almost wholly from the *Cecilia*'s logbook, which Edouard A. Stackpole published and discussed in *The Voyage of the Huron and the Huntress* (1955). The landing is a claim, not proven: sources call it "thought" or "debated," and the first undisputed landing on the mainland came 74 years later, at Cape Adare on January 24, 1895 (see St. Carsten in the first round).
- **What they left behind:** the *Cecilia* logbook; the Davis Coast, the west coast of the Antarctic Peninsula between Cape Kjellman and Cape Sterneck, named for him by the U.S. Advisory Committee on Antarctic Names.
- **Death:** not found. Wikipedia notes that little is known of his later life.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for him in canon. Note for the author: the Glossary's Mirny entry places Mirny "on the Davis Coast." The Davis Coast named for this John Davis is on the Antarctic Peninsula. Mirny's real coast faces the Davis Sea, which is named for John King Davis; the Glossary wording may be a slip.

### Clements Markham (1830-1916)
- **Life and origin:** Sir Clements Robert Markham, born July 20, 1830, at Stillingfleet, Yorkshire, England, second son of the Reverend David Frederick Markham, of a family that included an Archbishop of York. Schooled at Cheam and Westminster. He joined the Royal Navy as a cadet in 1844 and served in HMS *Assistance* on the 1850-1851 search for Franklin's lost expedition (see Crozier above), where he took part in sledging and was there when the first Franklin graves were found on Beechey Island. He left the navy, traveled in Peru (1852-1853) and led a cinchona-collecting expedition in the Andes (1859-1861). Geographer, historian and translator.
- **Antarctic role:** never went south. He was the chief organizer of Britain's return to Antarctic exploration. He was secretary of the Royal Geographical Society (1863-1888) and its president (May 29, 1893, to May 22, 1905). As president he drove the British National Antarctic Expedition (*Discovery*, 1901-1904) and chose its leader. He had first noticed the young midshipman Robert Falcon Scott in 1887, when Scott won a cutter race in the West Indies, and he secured Scott's appointment in 1900 over opposition from the scientific societies, insisting on naval rather than civilian scientific command. He was godfather to Scott's son, Peter Scott.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** a forceful, tireless promoter and a prolific writer: he translated and edited twenty-two books for the Hakluyt Society, which he led as president (from 1889 or 1890, sources differ, to 1909), and wrote *The Lands of Silence*, a history of polar exploration, published after his death (1921). Judgments are sharply mixed. King George V spoke of "the debt the country owed" him. The geologist Frank Debenham called him "a dangerous old man," and the RGS librarian Hugh Robert Mill called him "an enthusiast rather than a scholar." He backed man-hauling over dogs and skis, a preference later blamed for weaknesses in British polar method. He denounced Carsten Borchgrevink (St. Carsten) as "evasive, a liar and a fraud," accused William Speirs Bruce of "mischievous rivalry," and turned against Shackleton (St. Ernest) after the *Nimrod* expedition.
- **What they left behind:** the *Discovery* expedition and, through it, the careers of Scott and Shackleton; Mount Markham (82°51′S, in the Transantarctic Mountains, named by Scott in 1902), Markham Island, and Minna Bluff (named for his wife, Minna); a large body of geographical history and translation.
- **Death:** January 30, 1916, in London, aged 85. While reading in bed by candlelight he set fire to the bedclothes and was overcome by the smoke.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Fridtjof Nansen (1861-1930)
- **Life and origin:** Born October 10, 1861, at Store Frøen near Christiania (Oslo), Norway, and raised at the edge of the Nordmarka forest, where he became a skier and outdoorsman. He studied zoology at the university in Christiania from 1881 and earned a doctorate on the nervous system of marine animals, work that made him known in neuroscience. In 1888, aged 27, he led a party of six across the Greenland ice sheet on skis, east to west, the first crossing. With the shipbuilder Colin Archer he designed the *Fram*, whose rounded hull was meant to rise out of the ice's grip, and let her drift across the Arctic Ocean (1893-1896). He and Hjalmar Johansen left the ship with dogs and sledges and reached a farthest north in April 1895 (86°14′N in the usual figure; the Fram Museum's page gives 86°4′N), then wintered on Franz Josef Land.
- **Antarctic role:** never went south. His tie to Antarctica is through St. Roald and St. Robert. He had hoped to take *Fram* to Antarctica after 1896, but by 1907 that hope had faded; late in September 1907 he called Roald Amundsen to his home and told him he could have the ship. Amundsen took *Fram* south in 1910 and reached the South Pole in December 1911, announcing his change of goal from the North Pole only once at sea. In 1910 Nansen also advised Robert Falcon Scott on skis and introduced him to the young skier Tryggve Gran, whom Scott then took to Antarctica as ski expert. His sledge designs, the Nansen cooker and the ski-and-dog method of his Greenland and *Fram* journeys shaped the equipment of the heroic-age expeditions.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** scientist, explorer, diplomat and humanitarian, remembered in Norway as a national figure of the first rank. During the 1905 separation from Sweden he acted as a secret envoy to persuade Prince Carl of Denmark to become King Haakon VII, then served as Norway's first minister in London (1906-1908). As the League of Nations' first High Commissioner for Refugees from 1920, he organized the return of about 450,000 prisoners of war, ran famine relief in Soviet Russia (1921-1922) and created the "Nansen passport" for stateless refugees. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1922. From his Greenland book: "It is this strange desire to accomplish something, to fill life with more than a daily journey from home to office and back again" (as quoted by the Fram Museum).
- **What they left behind:** the *Fram*, now in the Fram Museum, Oslo, the ship that carried Amundsen to the Bay of Whales; the Nansen passport; the Fridtjof Nansen Institute at his home, Polhøgda; Mount Nansen in Antarctica and many other features north and south.
- **Death:** May 13, 1930, of a heart attack at his home, Polhøgda, at Lysaker near Oslo, aged 68. He is buried in the garden at Polhøgda.
- **Tepenian place:** none in canon found.

### Macpherson Robertson (1859-1945)
- **Life and origin:** Born September 6, 1859, at Ballarat, Victoria, Australia, eldest child of Macpherson David Robertson, a Scottish carpenter, and Margaret (née Brown). His father left the family in Fiji; in 1869 his mother took the children to Leith, Scotland, where he started work at nine. The family came back to Australia in 1874, and he was apprenticed to a confectioner in Fitzroy, Melbourne. At 19 he began making sweets in the bathroom of the family home, making them Monday to Thursday and selling them on weekends.
- **Antarctic role:** never went south. He was the chief private backer of Douglas Mawson's British Australian and New Zealand Antarctic Research Expedition (BANZARE, 1929-1931), giving £16,500 in 1929. Mawson named Mac.Robertson Land, the coast and hinterland of East Antarctica where Australia's Mawson Station was later built (1954), in his honor. He was knighted (Knight Bachelor) in 1932 "for services to Antarctic expeditions," and made KBE in 1935 for his giving.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** a self-made industrialist and showman. His company, MacRobertson's, made Freddo Frog, Cherry Ripe and Old Gold chocolate and brought fairy floss (cotton candy) to Australia; at its peak it was the largest chocolate maker in the southern hemisphere, with about 3,000 workers, until Cadbury took it over in 1967. His Fitzroy works were known as the "White City" for their white buildings, white uniforms and his own white clothes and white ponies. A heavy giver: for Victoria's centenary in 1934 he gave about £100,000, including £40,000 for the Mac.Robertson Girls' High School, the MacRobertson Bridge, a fountain near the Shrine of Remembrance and the herbarium at the Royal Botanic Gardens, and he sponsored the MacRobertson Air Race from England to Melbourne (prize money £15,000). By 1933 he estimated he had given away more than £360,000.
- **What they left behind:** Mac.Robertson Land; the school, bridge, fountain and herbarium in Melbourne; the confectionery brands, still sold today.
- **Death:** August 20, 1945, at his home in Kew, Melbourne, aged 85. Cause of death not found in the sources checked.
- **Tepenian place:** Mawson (Mawson subnet hub city) stands on the Mawson Station site at Horseshoe Harbor, Holme Bay, in Mac.Robertson Land. The city is named for St. Douglas, not for him.

### Adèle Dumont d'Urville (1798-1842)
- **Life and origin:** Adèle Dorothée Pépin, born in 1798, probably at Toulon, France, daughter of the watchmaker Joseph Marie Pépin. She met the young naval officer Jules Dumont d'Urville (St. Jules) when he visited her father's shop, and married him in Toulon on May 1, 1815, at about 17, in a quiet ceremony "with a few guests from both sides." Sources differ on their children: at least four, none of whom reached adulthood (Wikipedia), or at least two with burial records (Linda Hall Library). Their son Jules died with them in 1842. She later bought a country house (bastide) with a garden outside Toulon.
- **Antarctic role:** never went south. Her husband named Adélie Land (Terre Adélie) for her when his ships *Astrolabe* and *Zélée* reached that coast of East Antarctica in January 1840. In his words, the name was meant to "perpetuate the memory of my deep gratitude" for "the devoted companion who three times has consented to a long and painful separation" (as quoted in the sources below, translated from the French; the three separations presumably being his long voyages of 1822-1825, 1826-1829 and 1837-1840). He also named Cape Pépin for her family. The Adélie penguin takes its name from the land, where the species was first collected.
- **Deeds and character in the record:** known mainly as the wife who stayed home through his long voyages. Her letters survive in the French National Archives, and an archival collection is held under both their names; the sources checked do not describe their content.
- **What they left behind:** Adélie Land, Cape Pépin, Adele Island (New Zealand), and through the land, the Adélie penguin. A French Southern and Antarctic Lands airmail stamp showed her in 1981.
- **Death:** May 8, 1842, at Meudon, with her husband and son, in the derailment and fire of a Versailles-Paris train, described by the Linda Hall Library as the worst railway accident since public railroads began. Buried in Montparnasse Cemetery, Paris.
- **Tepenian place:** the city of Dumont d'Urville (Janbogo subnet) stands on Petrel Island off Adélie Land, beside Adélie penguin colonies; the Glossary also records a Dumont d'Urville community moment when the Adélie penguins return each breeding season. The city is named for St. Jules, and `DR-58` gives it a new name, not yet chosen.
- **Sources:**

### Queen Maud of Norway (1869-1938) and Crown Princess Märtha of Norway, born Princess Märtha of Sweden (1901-1954)
- **Life and origin:** **Maud**, born November 26, 1869, at Marlborough House, London, daughter of the Prince of Wales (later King Edward VII) and Alexandra. She married her cousin Prince Carl of Denmark on July 22, 1896. When Norway left its union with Sweden in 1905 (the change in which Fridtjof Nansen acted as envoy, see above), Carl was elected King Haakon VII, and the two were crowned in Nidaros Cathedral, Trondheim, on June 22, 1906. **Märtha**, born March 28, 1901, in Stockholm, daughter of Prince Carl, Duke of Västergötland, and Princess Ingeborg of Denmark. She married Crown Prince Olav of Norway (later King Olav V), Maud's son, on March 21, 1929, in Oslo Cathedral; she was the mother of Princesses Ragnhild and Astrid and of the future King Harald V.
- **Antarctic role:** neither went south. Both names come from Norwegian whaling-era exploration and the Norwegian claim.
  - Queen Maud: Roald Amundsen (St. Roald) named the Queen Maud Mountains for her on his South Pole journey (1911), and christened his Arctic ship *Maud* (launched 1916) in her honor. In January 1930 Hjalmar Riiser-Larsen and Finn Lützow-Holm, flying from Lars Christensen's *Norvegia* expedition, gave the name Queen Maud Land to the coast they found between 37°E and 49°30′E. Norway annexed Queen Maud Land by royal decree on January 14, 1939, moving quickly after the geologist Adolf Hoel warned of German intentions: the German *Schwabenland* expedition was then on its way to the same coast, and Germany later declared a sector there named Neu-Schwabenland.
  - Crown Princess Märtha: in February 1930 Riiser-Larsen discovered from the *Norvegia* and roughly charted from the air the coast near Cape Norvegia and named it Crown Princess Martha Land, now the Princess Martha Coast, between 05°E and the Stancomb-Wills Glacier at 20°W. It is the coast where Bellingshausen sighted the ice shelf on January 28, 1820 (see the first round).
- **Deeds and character in the record:** Maud is described as kind, cheerful, athletic and fashionable; she learned to ski and took to Norwegian winter sports, though she kept regarding England as home. Märtha is described as confident and outspoken for a royal woman of her time. Invited to the United States by President Franklin D. Roosevelt after the German invasion of Norway in 1940, she lived for a time at the White House and campaigned and raised funds for Norway through the war, earning the name "Norway's Ambassador Number 1."
- **What they left behind:** Queen Maud Land (Dronning Maud Land), Norway's Antarctic claim; the Queen Maud Mountains on Amundsen's route to the Pole; the Princess Martha Coast.
- **Death:** Maud died November 20, 1938, in London, of heart failure after an abdominal operation that found advanced cancer; buried in the Royal Mausoleum, Akershus Castle, Oslo. Märtha died April 5, 1954, at the National Hospital in Oslo, aged 53; the cause is given as cancer or hepatitis and is disputed. She is buried in the same mausoleum.
- **Tepenian place:** none named for them, but much of the Halley subnet lies in Queen Maud Land: the cities Troll (Norway's founding, where St. Roald is called the region's patron), Sanay and Santa Luce, and the Queen Maud Land coastal spine from Belgrano to Lazar; DLC 5's region covers the same coast. Neumayer, on the Ekström Ice Shelf (Neumayer Station III at about 8°W), lies within the Princess Martha Coast's span, and Troll stands inland of its eastern part.
