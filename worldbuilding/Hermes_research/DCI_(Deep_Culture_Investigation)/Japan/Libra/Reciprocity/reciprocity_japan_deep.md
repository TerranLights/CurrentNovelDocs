## 2. Result-by-result deep treatments

### R1. Giri (義理) — the moral economy of obligation and reciprocity
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **giri** (義理) literally "righteous duty"; the social obligation arising from human relationships — the duty to repay favours and the moral responsibility one bears toward society. Unlike the Western idea of reciprocity (equal exchanges based on mutual benefit), *giri* is a **moral debt that one is obligated to repay, sometimes without a clear expectation of immediate return**. It is "not about voluntary generosity but about fulfilling social expectations" (tanukistories.jp).
- **go-on** (恩恩) / **hōkō** (報恩): the reciprocal chain of favour bestowed and favour returned — the feudal form of *giri* between lord and retainer.
- **fuguri** (不義理): failure to discharge *giri*; the result is social isolation and loss of trust. "One who neglects giri fell out of the web of trust as 'fuguri' — remiss in duty."
- **ongaeshi** (恩返し): the act of repaying a favour or kindness received.
- **giri-choko** (義理チョコ): "obligation chocolate" — chocolate given to male colleagues on Valentine's Day as a social courtesy rather than a romantic gesture; the modern concession that the word itself has settled into casual use.
- **giri no chichi** (father-in-law), **giri no imoto** (sister-in-law): kinship terms treating relations formed by marriage as standing next to blood.
- Related: **ninjō** (人情, personal feeling / compassion) — the inner counter-voice to *giri*. The tension between *giri* (duty) and *ninjō* (personal feeling) is the great theme of Japanese drama and literature (Chikamatsu).
- Cross-cultural: Ruth Benedict (The Chrysanthemum and the Sword, 1946) took up *giri* as a key concept, calling it "one of the most curious categories among the world's cultures"; her analysis of *giri* as a debt that must be repaid has been criticised as oversimplified, but her placing of the notion on the international stage was significant.

#### Value
- *giri* is the social adhesive that holds dense networks of obligation together — "a social adhesive that contract and law cannot fully explain" (samurai-bushido.org).
- It frames repayment not as accounting but as **character**: "What separates giri from mere calculation of advantage is that repayment is treated as a matter of character rather than accounting" (samurai-bushido.org).
- In the Edo period, *giri* "settled into an ethic of the whole society, townsman and farmer as well as samurai": credit in trade, mutual aid in the village community, courtesies owed to relatives and connections — "discharging one's giri was the mark of a full member of society" (samurai-bushido.org).
- It is also a source of **obligation and burden**: the compulsion to repay *on* is motivated both by gratitude and by the desire to "get rid of the on load, as it may become quite burdensome over time" (Webster).

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*giri*) | Not doing (*fuguri* / breach) |
|---|---|
| Repay favours received (ongaeshi) | Failing to discharge duty = fuguri |
| Practising gift-giving: ochūgen (mid-year), oseibo (year-end), New Year's cards, wedding/funeral attendance and envelope money | Social isolation and loss of trust when *giri* is neglected |
| Maintaining commercial credit and mutual aid in the village | Being "remiss in duty"; dropping out of the web of trust |
| Business courtesy toward long-standing partners; properly observed courtesies toward the indebted | In a samurai context: loss of family name and social standing |
| "Associating out of giri" (modern, pejorative sense of hollow duty) | — |

#### Sayings
- "If giri is so often cited as a Japanese word that resists translation, it is because the notion stands at the crossroads of duty, debt of gratitude, face, and affection. The very difficulty of translating it shows giri to be a mirror of the particular weave of Japanese social relations." (samurai-bushido.org)
- *giri-choko* — the colloquial coinage that itself records the lightness and tenacity of the concept.
- Chikamatsu's domestic tragedies (e.g., The Love Suicides at Sonezaki, 1703) place "at their center men and women caught between giri and ninjo — duty and feeling — crystallizing into tragedy the dilemma in which to fulfill duty is to betray love, and to carry love through is to fail duty."

#### Exemplars
- **The 47 rōnin** (the Akō incident): the ultimate exemplar of *go-on/hōkō* — retainers who repaid the favour of their lord's life with their own, laying his head at Sengaku-ji (see §R8).
- **Chikamatsu Monzaemon** (jōruri puppet plays): the dramatist who crystallised the *giri* vs. *ninjō* dilemma.
- **Ruth Benedict** (1946): the Western scholar who gave *giri* international currency.

#### Variation
- Content shifted by status: "For the samurai, giri was a weighty matter of family name and sworn word; for the townsman, it was rooted in commercial credit and neighborly relations."
- "That it was shared across statuses yet lived according to each way of life is precisely the measure of the notion's penetrating power." (samurai-bushido.org)
- Modern: a "movement to reweigh the burdens of giri has advanced, coining terms like the abolition of empty formalities and 'graduating' from New Year's cards."

#### Change
- Modern recalibration: declining gift-giving; companies discouraging *giri-choco* on Valentine's Day; some younger people opting out of *ochūgen/oseibo*; changing family dynamics around adult-child support of parents.
- "What is hollow form and what is true obligation? — this sorting task around giri may be called the bushido homework that modern Japanese continue to do every day." (samurai-bushido.org)

#### Applications and social contexts
- Personal relationships, family (adult-child support of parents), weddings, funerals, social gatherings.
- Workplace: employees' duty toward companies and managers; employers' duty to provide stability, bonuses, career progression.
- Business relationships: "When one company helps another, the recipient is obligated to return the favour, often leading to long-standing business partnerships built on mutual obligation rather than immediate profit."
- Gift-giving tradition as the clearest manifestation (ochūgen/oseibo to superiors, clients, mentors).

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: respond to a gift with a gift of equal or greater value; attend weddings/funerals out of obligation not merely desire; repay favours; maintain commercial credit.
- **Not doing**: say a flat "no" to an invitation (a direct refusal risks causing embarrassment or offence); refuse outright in business; let an obligation slip.

#### Mentions in available sources
- samurai-bushido.org (KIRIGIRI); tanukistories.jp; Chūshingura (via *chūshin gishi*); Columbia Afe (47 Ronin); Ruth Benedict 1946; Webster (on).

### R2. Honne (本音) and Tatemae (建前)
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **honne** (本音) = "true sound" / "real voice": one's genuine inner feelings, desires, and opinions.
- **tatemae** (建前) = "built in front" / "facade": the behaviour, expressions, and statements presented in public to conform to social expectations, maintain group harmony, and fulfill the requirements of one's social role.
- The pair encodes a **pervasive social distinction** between what one truly thinks/feels and what one says/shows to the world. The distinction "is not unique to Japan; all human societies involve some degree of gap between inner experience and outward performance" — what makes the *honne/tatemae* dynamic distinctive is the degree to which it is "consciously recognized, culturally codified, and systematically practiced as an essential component of social competence."
- **nommunication** (ノミュニケーション): a portmanteau of *nomu* (to drink) and "communication" — the widespread understanding that drinking together creates a social space in which the constraints of *tatemae* are relaxed and *honne* can be more freely expressed.
- **mentsu** (面子): "face" — *tatemae* protects face (both one's own and others').

#### Value
- *Tatemae* preserves **wa** (和, harmony) in densely interconnected social networks; it is "social lubrication," smoothing interactions and preventing minor irritations from escalating into open conflict.
- It **protects face** — both one's own and others' — by avoiding public confrontation.
- It provides a **shared communicative framework**: because the conventions of polite social performance are widely understood, they enable communication through implication, suggestion, and indirect expression that can be remarkably precise and nuanced. "A skilled reader of tatemae can discern substantial amounts of information about a speaker's honne from the specific forms, timing, and context of their tatemae expressions."

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*tatemae*) | Breach (*honne* leaking out, or direct "no") |
|---|---|
| Expressing support or acquiescence in meetings even when privately disagreeing | Blunt disagreement or direct "no" in a business meeting |
| Indirect refusal: "chotto muzukashii desu" (that sounds difficult), "kangaete okimasu" (I'll think about it), "ii desu ne, demo…" (that would be nice, but…) | Direct refusal that risks causing embarrassment/offence |
| Framing refusals in the most positive terms; signalling "please go home" by beginning elaborate preparations for more food/drink | Exposing one's inner life inappropriately (in contexts of low trust) |
| Maintaining the "right" face in social situations | — |

#### Sayings
- "In Japan, the ability to maintain appropriate tatemae — to say the right things, express the expected emotions, and present the proper face in social situations, even when one's honne (true feelings) differ significantly from what is being expressed — is not considered hypocrisy or dishonesty but is understood as a fundamental social skill, an expression of maturity and consideration for others, and a necessary mechanism for the smooth functioning of the dense, interconnected social networks that characterize Japanese life."
- *Yoroshiku onegai shimasu* — literally "please treat me well"; in a tennis context, its illocutionary force is "well, I'm probably not very good at tennis but please put up with my poor playing ability" — and the reciprocal relationship means the speaker is then obligated to play an acceptable standard of tennis so the partner can enjoy the game.

#### Exemplars
- The **tennis partner** exchange (Webster's example): *yoroshiku onegai shimasu* as a "lowering of status" that obligates the speaker to perform acceptably.
- **Classical literature**: *The Tale of Genji* — the tension between social performances required by court life and the authentic emotions simmering beneath.
- **Modern media**: Natsume Sōseki to Haruki Murakami in modern literature; Japanese cinema and television drama frequently structuring narratives around the *honne/tatemae* dynamic.

#### Variation
- Honne is "reserved for contexts of trust and intimacy: close friendships, family relationships (particularly between equals or within the same generation), romantic partnerships, and situations lubricated by alcohol."
- A manager may genuinely like a subordinate while recognising that professional distance is appropriate; a student may dislike a teacher's methods while respecting the teacher's authority.

#### Change
- **Generational change**: millennials and Generation Z show increasing interest in "authentic" or "honest" communication, influenced by global trends valorising personal authenticity, self-expression, and rejection of social masks.
- **Social media**: platforms like X (Twitter) in Japan provide outlets for *honne* expression that bypass the constraints of in-person communication; anonymous confession culture.
- **Mental health**: growing awareness and acceptance of counselling/therapy may be shifting attitudes toward the suppression of *honne*.
- The system is "evolving, not disappearing" — retaining its essential function while adapting.

#### Applications and social contexts
- Workplace: an employee who disagrees with a superior's decision expresses support in the meeting (tatemae) while conveying reservations through indirect channels or private conversation (closer to honne).
- Commercial interactions: a shopkeeper frames a refusal in the most positive terms; a business partner who finds a proposal unacceptable expresses "concerns" rather than outright rejection; a host who wants guests to leave begins elaborate preparations for more food/drink as a signal of the tatemae covering the honne of "please go home."
- Cross-cultural communication with Westerners: mutual misunderstanding — Westerners misread *tatemae* as agreement; Japanese read Western directness as rude, aggressive, or lacking in social awareness.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: say the right thing for the context; preserve harmony; protect face; read the spaces between words; use *tatemae* to smooth interaction; express *honne* only in trusted/intimate contexts.
- **Not doing**: make direct refusals; express negative opinion publicly; cause public embarrassment; expose inner feelings in low-trust contexts.

#### Mentions in available sources
- hanabira.org (honne/tatemae); Webster (speech-act examples); Chūshingura / *chūshin gishi* literature; samurai-bushido.org (giri as "face" layer).

#### Evidence tags

- **synthesis**: hanabira.org (cultural framing).
- **classic**: hanabira.org (Tale of Genji; classical literature discussion).

### R3. En (縁) and Kizuna (絆) — relational bonds of obligation
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **en** (縁): "relationship, fate, connection, ties" — the web of relationships that binds people. In *The Circle of On, Giri, and Ninjo* (Seki 1971), the basic idea of Japanese *on* human relationship is shown in the Japanese language; the language is "discriminative for speaker and addressee," and Japanese behaviour stresses "cooperation, or conformity with others rather than independence or freedom of the individual."
- **kizuna** (絆): "bond, tie, connection" — the strong emotional bonds between people in a relationship. Post-2011, *kizuna* became a post-disaster cliché of national solidarity (see §Change).
- En/kizuna is a **relational** form of reciprocity: obligation that attaches to the bond itself, not merely to a transferred favour.

#### Value
- *En* frames human life as a set of **relationships** rather than isolated individuals; self-worth is achieved through "making a good family, to make a good community and to make a good state" — "Self-control or self-sacrifice for the family, for the community and for the nation was the most admirable virtue."
- The language is **discriminative for speaker and addressee**: even in daily conversation the addressee is always considered higher or superior than the speaker; "the Japanese have an honorific… To use precisely the honorific is troublesome enough even for the Japanese themselves."

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*en/kizuna*) | Breach |
|---|---|
| Maintaining the web of relationships (bonds of kin and patron-client ties) | Breaking a bond; social isolation |
| Bowing lower and more the more the other is superior (depreciating oneself, regarding others as superior) | Conforming to others rather than rebelling — the traditional discipline |
| Honouring the relationship with appropriate speech levels and courtesies | — |

#### Sayings
- "The goal of life of the Japanese was to make a good family, to make a good community and to make a good state."
- "Strength of character is shown in conforming, not in rebelling."
- "In the same way, 'I will go up to you; please you come down to me' — so on: the language is discriminative for speaker and addressee."
- Post-2011: *kizuna* — the "ties" that bind a nation together in disaster.

#### Exemplars
- The **Tokugawa shogunate** (Tokugawa Tsunayoshi) — the feudal order where loyalty and the bonds of the *samurai* were institutionalised; the *47 rōnin* as the ultimate *en/kizuna* exemplar (see §R8).
- Post-2011 Japan — *kizuna* as a national solidarity narrative (see Change).

#### Variation
- *En* in the broader sense applies to any relationship (kin, patron-client, community); *kizuna* is more emotionally charged.
- The *kizuna* post-2011 usage is a **cliché** of national solidarity — "the *kizuna* post-2011 cliché."

#### Change
- *Kizuna* has been **co-opted** into post-disaster rhetoric; it has also been discussed as a weakening concept in modern corporate life (weakening lifetime employment, financialised gift/debt markets).
- The traditional *en*-based hierarchy is being renegotiated by generational and global change.

#### Applications and social contexts
- Family, community, and state; the patron-client bonds of the *samurai*; the relational web of *giri* (gift economy).
- Post-2011 disaster solidarity; corporate corporate Japan of the post-war "company family" model.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: maintain and honour the web of relationships; speak with appropriate honorifics and bow lower; show self-sacrifice for family/community/state.
- **Not doing**: assert individual independence; rebel against conformity; break a relationship.

#### Mentions in available sources
- Seki 1971 (Hokudai, partial); tanukistories.jp; Columbia Afe (47 Ronin); Hanabira.org (honne/tatemae related).

#### Evidence tags
- **scholarly**: Seki 1971 (partial).
- **synthesis**: tanukistories.jp; Columbia Afe.
- **classic**: Seki 1971; Chūshingura.

### R4. On (恩) — bestowed benefit and incurred debt
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **on** (恩): "a fusion of 'bestowed benefit and incurred debt'" (Lebra, in Lebra & Lebra, citing Webster: 106). *On* is "a Japanese word for favour/benefit/kindness" (Takahashi and Takahashi: 952) and "can be seen as a fusion of these English words."
- The **on[on] concept**: the Japanese phenomenon of "bestowed benefit and incurred debt" — conveyed in **dependency-acknowledging speech acts**.
- **Dependency-acknowledging speech acts** (Webster): four major speech-act verbs that represent dependence-acknowledgement:
  1. **onegai suru** (onegai shimasu, yoroshiku onegai shimasu): "I ask/request of you" — illocutionary force: the speaker wants you to do something for me, and I acknowledge I will be indebted to you for your favour.
  2. **osore iru** (osore irimasu): "sorry to trouble you" — acknowledges the dependency and incurred debt.
  3. **makaseru** (makashite choudai, omakase, makashite kure, makashite oite): "leave something up to me" — accountable for what one does; "an expected reciprocal arrangement."
  4. **osewa-ni naru** (osewa-ni narimasu, osewa-ni narimashita): "I'll avail myself of your assistance" — past tense: the speaker acknowledges the dependency s/he has had on the listener thus far, and a humble indirect commitment to reciprocity of that dependency.

#### Value
- The *on* concept is the **linguistic skeleton** of Japanese reciprocity: obligation is acknowledged **at the moment of requesting**, not deferred until the favour is returned.
- It is a **reciprocal arrangement**: "X promises to do something (Y) for Z. It is then expected that at some time in the future, Z will return this favour (Y), or *on.* Z is thus committing him/herself to some future obligation towards X as part and parcel of requesting something of Z."
- The compulsion to repay *on* is motivated by (a) gratitude-motivated compulsion and (b) the desire to "get rid of the on load, as it may become quite burdensome over time."

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*on*) | Breach (*nakawanai on* — having no indebtedness) |
|---|---|
| Acknowledging dependency at the moment of request (*osore-iru*, *onegai shimasu*, *makaseru*, *osewa-ni naru*) | Refusing the benefit / failing to acknowledge the debt |
| Using appropriate speech levels and honorific particles to acknowledge the listener's superiority | Using the wrong register; failing to acknowledge the relationship |
| "Mutually dependent" exchanges in both formal and informal dealings | — |

#### Sayings
- "Without attention to these important speech acts verbs, a learner can come across as abrupt, thoughtless and insensitive to Japanese native speakers."
- "Life without *yoroshiku* is like a sandwich without bread… if I don't drop a *yoroshiku* on someone I've just met or leave a *yoroshiku* at the cleaners along with my linen or clip a *yoroshiku* on to my post office request to mail a package, the whole exchange seems sadly incomplete." (Thomas Dillon, quoted in Webster)
- "The important thing is to show deference and to acknowledge one's dependence on other people rather than to avoid imposition." (Anna Wierzbicka, quoted in Webster)

#### Exemplars
- The **Sydney-based Japanese language study** (Webster's data): business-internal, business-external, and family exchanges.
- The **tennis partner**: *yoroshiku onegai shimasu* as a "lowering of status" that obligates the speaker to perform acceptably.

#### Variation
- Morphological variety according to relative status: *itashimasu* vs. *shimasu* (more formal vs. less formal).
- The more formal variety signals the speaker's lower status relative to the listener (e.g., business person to client).
- The shortened forms of *kureru* in the most relaxed brotherly exchanges indicate the least rigid or definite transfer of *on*.

#### Change
- The study notes "issues surrounded the gender-specificity of the Japanese language," and calls for a "broader cross-section of authentic texts" and "texts taken from an in-country Japanese context rather than Australian context."
- Generational differences between the use of dependency-acknowledging speech acts in current youth culture and their parents' age group are an open question.
- The study is "retrieved in part" — the full paper (with the introduction and the earlier samples) is not yet fully read; the citation requires full read + author sign-off before finalization (see §8).

#### Applications and social contexts
- Business: telephone exchanges between a tour supervisor and a colleague; *itsumo osewa-ni natte-orimasu* (we are always indebted to you).
- Business external: the manager of a language school in Sydney — *osore-irimasu* (thanking in anticipation) with a prospective student.
- Family: *Maya-chan onegai-ne* (please look after Maya) — the father acknowledges the grandparents' care; *katte-oite kureru-ka* (please buy her something from me) — "pay back his brother."
- Language teaching: pragmatic competence — raising learners' awareness about the concept of *on* and its linguistic and sociocultural implications.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: acknowledge dependency at the moment of request; use appropriate honorifics and register; accept the future obligation to reciprocate.
- **Not doing**: avoid the request; refuse the benefit; fail to acknowledge the debt.

#### Mentions in available sources
- Webster (academic paper); Lebra & Lebra (cited); Takahashi & Takahashi (cited); Wierzbicka (cited); Thomas Dillon (cited).

### R5. Ninjo (人情) — personal feeling and compassion
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **ninjō** (人情): "human feeling / compassion" — the emotional, affectionate, compassionate core that contrasts with *giri*'s duty.
- In the *giri* vs. *ninjō* axis, *ninjō* is the **individual's true feeling**; *giri* is society's demand. "When the two agree there is happiness; when they collide, Japanese stories have typically drawn either the tragedy of choosing duty and immolating feeling, or the ruin of choosing feeling and failing duty."

#### Value
- *Ninjō* grounds *giri* in human feeling: even in family dynamics, "adult children may feel obligated to support their aging parents out of *giri*, even if they do not have a close emotional bond" — so *giri* and *ninjō* are not always separable; the obligation can be felt as a form of *ninjō*.

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*ninjō*) | Breach |
|---|---|
| Feeling compassion; acting from human feeling | Callousness; indifference to another's feeling |
| Emotional expression in trusted/intimate contexts (the setting for *honne*) | — |

#### Exemplars
- **Chikamatsu Monzaemon** (The Love Suicides at Sonezaki, 1703): the dramatist who made the *giri* vs. *ninjō* collision the centre of the Japanese drama.
- **Chūshingura**: the 47 rōnin's loyalty is its own form of boundless *ninjō* (loyalty as love).

#### Variation
- *Ninjō* as "human feeling/compassion" is more diffuse than *giri*; it is the "inner world" counterpart to *tatemae*.

#### Change
- Modern: a movement to reweigh the burdens of *giri* has advanced; "What is hollow form and what is true obligation?" — the configuration of *giri*/ *ninjō* is being re-sorted.

#### Applications and social contexts
- Family dynamics; romantic partnerships (where *ninjō* is expressed); the *giri*/ *ninjō* dilemma in literature, theatre, film.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: feel and express compassion; act from human feeling in trusted contexts.
- **Not doing**: be indifferent; suppress human feeling in contexts where it is expected.

#### Mentions in available sources
- samurai-bushido.org (KIRIGIRI); Chūshingura; Ninjō (Wikipedia).

#### Evidence tags
- **scholarly**: samurai-bushido.org; Chūshingura (scholarly framing).

- **classic**: samurai-bushido.org (Chikamatsu).

### R6. The gift economy — ochūgen, oseibo, omiyage, giri-choko
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **ochūgen** (御中元): mid-year gifts to superiors, clients, or mentors — a token of gratitude and acknowledgment of social bonds.
- **oseibo** (御歳暮): year-end gifts to superiors, clients, or mentors.
- **omiyage** (土産): souvenirs brought by travellers for colleagues, friends, or family.
- **giri-choko** (義理チョコ): "obligation chocolate" — a modern gift with a *giri* meaning.
- **hangaeshi** / **okaeshi**: returning a gift (the returning end of the circuit).
- **noshi** / **mizuhiki**: gift presentation and tying cords (gift-giving paraphernalia).
- **enryo** (遠慮): reluctance to impose — the recipient's respect that frames the giver's obligation.

#### Value
- Gift-giving is "one of the clearest manifestations of *giri*: reciprocity in Japanese culture." "When a person receives a gift, they are expected to return a gift of equal or greater value."
- These are "not given out of pure generosity but as a social expectation to maintain harmony."

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*gift economy*) | Breach |
|---|---|
| Giving ochūgen/oseibo to superiors, clients, mentors | Failing to give; giving too little |
| Returning gifts (hangaeshi/okaeshi) | Not returning gifts |
| Bringing omiyage when travelling | Not bringing omiyage |
| Female giri-choko to male colleagues on Valentine's Day | — |

#### Sayings
- "When a person receives a gift, they are expected to return a gift of equal or greater value."
- "The traditions of ochūgen and oseibo involve people giving gifts to those who have helped them throughout the year, such as mentors, teachers, clients, and bosses. These gifts are not given out of pure generosity but as a social expectation to maintain harmony."

#### Exemplars
- The **Valentine's giri-choko** circuit: women give obligatory chocolates to male colleagues — a "social courtesy rather than a romantic gesture."
- The **lifetime-employment** model, where the company reciprocates loyalty with stability, bonuses, and career progression (see below).

#### Variation
- Omiyage: these are "souvenirs" but they are also a form of *giri* — the traveller acknowledges the relationship by bringing back gifts.
- The gift economy is seasonally structured: mid-year (ochūgen), year-end (oseibo), New Year's (New Year's cards).

#### Change
- **Declining gift-giving**: "Many Japanese companies have started discouraging giri-choco on Valentine's Day, recognizing the pressure and financial burden it places on employees. Similarly, some younger people are opting out of the ochūgen and oseibo gift traditions."
- The gift economy is also a **commodified** market: omiyage sector ~US$242bn; ¥135bn chūgen/seibo (per wider sources).

#### Applications and social contexts
- Workplace: gifts to bosses, clients, mentors.
- Family: adult-child support of aging parents.
- Travel: omiyage.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: give gifts of equal or greater value; give to the right person (superior, client, mentor); give at the right time (mid-year, year-end, New Year).
- **Not doing**: give too little; fail to give; fail to return gifts.

### R7. Shame and haji — the breach-side of reciprocity
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **haji** (恥): shame — the social emotion that enforces *giri*. When *giri* is neglected, the result is not merely a legal or contractual failure but a **shaming** failure.
- Closely related to the broader Japanese **shame culture**: shame derives from awareness of the expectations of others; the breach of *giri* is a breach of face (*mentu*), which can result in **loss of trust** and **social isolation**.

#### Value
- Shame is a **social regulator**: it enforces the *giri* economy without the need for contract or law.
- The *fuguri* (remissness in duty) loses one "out of the web of trust" — being cast out of the relational network is the deepest shame.

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*haji* awareness) | Breach |
|---|---|
| Maintaining social standing; avoiding shame | Failing *giri* → shame, *fuguri* |
| Preserving face (*mentu*) | Public embarrassment; loss of face |

#### Sayings
- "In a warrior society, a person who failed in giri lost trust and was socially isolated." (samurai-bushido.org)
- "The weight of giri in this age was most vividly drawn in the joruri puppet plays of Chikamatsu Monzaemon." — the *giri*/ *ninjō* dilemma "crystallizing into tragedy the dilemma in which to fulfill duty is to betray love, and to carry love through is to fail duty."

#### Exemplars
- **The 47 rōnin**: the ultimate *giri* exemplars; their *hōkō* (vengeance) was also an act of *giri* — but their *haji* would have been to fail in their duty.
- **Chikamatsu's** domestic tragedies: the tragedy is that fulfilling *giri* (duty) requires failing *ninjō* (feeling) — a tragedy of shamed choices.

#### Variation
- *Haji* is universal in the shame/labour pairing; but in Japan it is specifically tied to *giri* and *en*.
- Modern: the "movement to reweigh the burdens of *giri*" has advanced — some of the shame-based obligations are being questioned.

#### Change
- Modern: "the strong sense of *giri* toward employers has led to a culture of overwork in Japan. However, younger generations are pushing back against this expectation, prioritizing personal well-being over relentless work obligations."
- The *giri*-based shame system is being renegotiated.

#### Applications and social contexts
- The *giri* economy; the feudal contract; workplace overwork; the 47 rōnin.

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: honour *giri*; preserve face; avoid *haji*.
- **Not doing**: fail in duty; cause shame; betray a benefactor.

#### Mentions in available sources
- samurai-bushido.org (KIRIGIRI); tanukistories.jp; Chūshingura; Columbia Afe.

### R8. The feudal contract — go-on / hōkō and the 47 rōnin
#### Words and near-distinctions
- **go-on** (恩): the favour granted by a lord to a retainer; **hōkō** (報恩): the repayment of that favour, even unto death.
- **chūshin gishi** (忠臣蔵): "loyal and dutiful retainers" — the 47 rōnin are remembered as *chūshin gishi*, people who "had given their lives for a greater cause in such a way that they deserved veneration after death."
- **Akō incident** (1701–03): the historical basis — daimyō Asano Naganori drew his sword in Edo Castle and attacked Kira; he was sentenced to *seppuku*; the shogunate confiscated Asano's lands and dismissed his samurai, making them *rōnin*.
- **Ōishi Kuranosuke Yoshio**: the high-ranking samurai who led the forty-six/ forty-seven *rōnin* who broke into Kira's mansion, captured and killed Kira, and laid his head at Sengaku-ji.
- **Hayashi Hōkō**: a Confucian scholar who "claims that the idolization of the rōnin may have been allowed because their actions matched with the Chinese loyalists."
- **Ogyū Sorai**: argued that the *rōnin* were "merely criminals and murderers" — there was "controversy revolving around the legitimacy of the rōnin's actions."

#### Value
- The 47 rōnin are "the ultimate exemplar of *go-on/hōkō* — the retainers who repaid the favour of their lord's life with their own."
- It is a **bilateral contract** (not merely one-way): the lord's duty to protect and honour the retainer is part of the bargain; *hōkō* is the repayment.
- "Loyalty and duty to one's master as a retainer is everything in the story of the 47 rōnin."
- "Being able to draw Confucianist values from this story is no coincidence — it is said that Asano was a Confucian. So it would only seem natural that his retainers would practice the same thing. Their ultimate sacrifice for their master is something that is held in high regard in Confucianism because they are fulfilling their responsibility to the fullest extent."

#### Practice (doing) and Breach (not doing)
| Doing (*go-on/hōkō*) | Breach |
|---|---|
| Vengeance for one's lord; repayment of the lord's life with one's own | Failing to avenge; the *rōnin* condemned as *rōnin* (a state that "was not an honorable state") |
| Registered vendettas in the 17th century — a legal framework for vendetta | The Akō vendetta did not adhere to the legalised system — hence the need for Confucian justification |
| Seppuku (the *rōnin* all did so on the same day) | — |

#### Sayings
- "Chūshin gishi is something that cannot be looked on lightly in regards to this story because it is the main idea in this story. Loyalty and duty to one's master as a retainer is everything in the story of the 47 rōnin."
- "Hayashi Hōkō summarizes that there might have been a correlation between the law and the lessons put forth in Confucian classics."

#### Exemplars
- **Ōishi Kuranosuke** and the 47 rōnin; Sengaku-ji (the *rōnin*'s graves).
- **The Kanadehon Chūshingura** (1748), the puppet play by Takeda Izumo II, Miyoshi Shōraku, and Namiki Senryū.

#### Variation
- The play "was performed every year in both the bunraku and kabuki versions."
- "To avoid censorship, the authors placed the action in the time of the Taiheiki (a few centuries earlier), changing the names of the principals."
- The number of *rōnin* varies: "forty-six/forty-seven of the rōnin (some discount the membership of one for various reasons)."

#### Change
- **Censorship**: during the occupation of Japan, the GHQ "banned performances of the story, charging them with promoting feudal values. Under the influence of Faubion Bowers, the ban was lifted in 1947."
- **Cultural export**: Chūshingura has been adapted into film (1907–2013: over 30 film versions), television dramas (over 21 TV productions of Chūshingura; 10 TV productions in 1997–2007 alone), ballet (Maurice Béjart's 1986 ballet, performed over 140 times in 14 nations by 2006), opera (Shigeaki Saegusa, 1997), and manga/anime.
- **Scholarly debate**: Tucker (1999), Postel (2019) — "Rethinking the Akou Ronin Debate"; the story has been re-examined as a myth, with Western retellings (e.g., Borges' 1935 story "The Uncivil Teacher of Court Etiquette") showing the story's translatability.

#### Applications and social contexts
- The feudal contract; the *samurai* ethos; the *chūshin gishi* ideal; the Western fascination with Japan (2013 Hollywood film, "47 Ronin").

#### Documented social expectations (doing and not doing)
- **Doing**: loyalty; revenge; repayment of the lord's life with one's own; *seppuku*.
- **Not doing**: failing to avenge; shedding the lord's blood dishonourably; questioning the law.

#### Mentions in available sources
- samurai-bushido.org; Chūshingura (Wikipedia); Columbia Afe (47 Ronin).

#### Evidence tags

- **synthesis**: Chūshingura (Wikipedia).
- **classic**: Chūshingura (Wikipedia).
- **practitioner**: literature, theatre, film, opera, ballet, and anime adaptations.

## 3. DCI "questions for every concept" frame — applied across all results

Applying the DCI frame that structures every concept in the research environment, here is the consolidated answer for the topic-series.

### Words and near-distinctions
- *giri* (duty/obligation), *ongaeshi* (repayment), *fuguri* (remissness), *on* (bestowed benefit + incurred debt), *en/kizuna* (relational bonds), *ninjō* (personal feeling), *honne/tatemae* (inner feelings vs. facade), *giri-choko* (obligation chocolate), *ochūgen/oseibo/omiyage* (gifts), *haji* (shame), *go-on/hōkō* (lord–retainer favour and repayment), *chūshin gishi* (loyal retainer).
- The most contested pair is *giri* vs. *ninjō*: duty vs. feeling. The most contested linguistic pair is *honne* vs. *tatemae*: true self vs. public face. The *on* speech-act verbs are the "doing" of *on* in language.

### Value
- Reciprocity in Japan is **moral debt, not accounting**: repayment is treated as a matter of character.
- It is a **social adhesive** that "contract and law cannot fully explain."
- It preserves **wa** (harmony), protects **face** (*mentu*), and frames human life as a web of **relationships** (*en/kizuna*).

### Practice
- Gift-giving (ochūgen, oseibo, omiyage, giri-choko); indirect refusal; workplace loyalty and lifetime employment; adult-child support of parents; the feudal *go-on/hōkō* contract (the 47 rōnin); *onor* in speech-act verbs (*osore iru*, *onegai shimasu*, *makaseru*, *osewa-ni naru*).

### Breach
- *Fuguri* (failure to discharge *giri*) → loss of trust, social isolation, shame.
- Direct refusals; blunt disagreement; breaking a promise; failing to repay a favour; failing to return a gift.
- The *chūshin gishi*: those who gave their lives for a greater cause; the *47 rōnin* condemned as *rōnin* — a state that was "not an honorable state."

### Sayings
- "Repayment is treated as a matter of character rather than accounting."
- "Life without *yoroshiku* is like a sandwich without bread."
- "The important thing is to show deference and to acknowledge one's dependence on other people rather than to avoid imposition."
- "Strength of character is shown in conforming, not in rebelling."
- "Loyalty and duty to one's master as a retainer is everything in the story of the 47 rōnin."

### Exemplars
- The 47 rōnin (Akō incident) as the feudal exemplar of *go-on/hōkō*.
- Chikamatsu Monzaemon as the dramatist of the *giri*/ *ninjō* collision.
- Ruth Benedict (1946) as the Western scholar of *giri*.
- Osteburo (Webster) speech acts as the linguistic exemplars of *on*.
- The *salaryman* as the modern *giri* employee; the *omiyage* traveller as the modern *giri* gift-giver.

### Variation
- *Giri* content shifted by status: samurai (family name and sworn word) vs. townsman (commercial credit and neighbourly relations).
- *On* speech acts vary by status and setting (business-internal, business-external, family).
- *Kizuna* shifted from a relational bond to a post-2011 national cliché.
- Gift economy is seasonally structured; declining under modern pressures.

### Change
- Weakening lifetime employment; financialised gift/debt markets; the commodified chocolate circuit.
- Declining gift-giving traditions; companies discouraging *giri-choko*.
- Generational change: millennials/Gen Z interested in "authentic" communication; social media outlets for *honne*.
- Post-2011 *kizuna* cliché; the renegotiation of the *giri*-based shame system.
- Censorship and export of Chūshingura under the GHQ and in the West.

## 4. Applications and social contexts (wide breadth)

Reciprocity in Japan spans, across wide breadth of sources and examples:

- **Personal/family**: adult-child support of parents; weddings and funerals; family relationships; *giri no chichi/imoto*.
- **Workplace**: the *salaryman* and lifetime employment; *company loyalty* as *giri*; business partnerships built on mutual obligation; *nemawashi* and *hourensou* as consensus-based reciprocity; *yoroshiku onegai shimasu* in business communication.
- **Gift economy**: *ochūgen/oseibo* to superiors and clients; *omiyage* souvenirs; *giri-choko* on Valentine's Day; *hangaeshi/okaeshi* (return gifts).
- **Feudal/contract**: *go-on/hōkō* between lord and retainer; the 47 rōnin; *chūshin gishi*; registered vendettas (17th century).
- **Art/literature/media**: Chikamatsu (joruri/kabuki); *The Tale of Genji*; modern literature (Sōseki, Murakami); cinema and television drama; *Chūshingura* adaptations (film, television, ballet, opera, manga, anime).
- **Cross-cultural**: Western directness vs. Japanese indirectness; Ruth Benedict's 1946 framing; the translatability debate (Borges).
- **Language**: the *on* speech-act verbs; honorifics; level-shifting in daily conversation.
- **Contemporary pressures**: work-life balance; declining gift-giving; generational changes in *honne*/tatemae.

## 5. Documented social expectations: doing and not doing (consolidated)

### Doing the system (the "giri" economy, the "honne/tatemae" system, the "en/kizuna" system)
- **Gift-giving**: give gifts of equal or greater value to superiors, clients, mentors; give at the right season (ochūgen mid-year, oseibo year-end, New Year's cards); return gifts.
- **Communication**: use indirect refusal (*chotto muzukashii desu*, *kangaete okimasu*); maintain *tatemae* in meetings while conveying reservations privately; protect face; read the spaces between words.
- **Workplace**: work long hours out of loyalty; provide stability, bonuses, career progression (the reciprocal employer duty); use *yoroshiku onegai shimasu*; acknowledge dependency in speech.
- **Language**: use appropriate honorifics and register; bow lower the more the other is superior; acknowledge the dependency at the moment of request (*osore iru*, *onegai shimasu*, *makaseru*, *osewa-ni naru*).
- **Family**: adult children support aging parents out of *giri*; attend weddings/funerals out of obligation.
- **Contractual**: the *go-on/hōkō* bond — repay a lord's life with one's own (the 47 rōnin); registered vendettas adhered to legal guidelines.

### Not doing (the breach-side: *fuguri*, *haji*, *honne* leaking, direct no)
- **Not repaying** a favour → *fuguri* → loss of trust, social isolation.
- **Not returning** a gift → breach of the gift economy.
- **Direct refusal** in business or social invitation → offence, loss of face.
- **Blunt disagreement** in a meeting → breach of *tatemae*; damage to relationships.
- **Breaking a promise** or failing to acknowledge the debt → loss of *on*.
- **Shaming** someone (publicly exposing *honne* or causing embarrassment) → breach of *haji* awareness.
- **Failing in duty** as a retainer → condemned as a *rōnin* (a state that was not honorable); the *47 rōnin*'s *seppuku* was the correct fulfilment, not a breach.
