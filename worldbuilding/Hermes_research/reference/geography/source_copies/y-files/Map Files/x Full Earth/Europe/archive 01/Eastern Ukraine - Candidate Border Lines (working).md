# Eastern Ukraine — Candidate Border Lines (working)

**Written 2026-09-30. Working note, for consideration only.** The author's premise: part of eastern Ukraine becomes Russian in the setting, and the people in the far-eastern slices consider themselves Russian; the author asked for help finding where the line falls. This note turns the evidence base in `research/Europe - 5 ...` §7 into three whole-oblast candidate packages (map: `eastern-ukraine-candidate-lines.png`, script `render_ukraine_east.py`), gives a recommendation, and lists the questions where the author's own knowledge of the ground is worth more than the data. **Nothing here is settled; the author draws the line.** ⚠️ marks a recalled or unverified fact.

## 1. What the data can and cannot say

The only oblast-level baseline is the 2001 census (the last Ukraine held), plus one 2014 poll. Raion-level tables were **not obtainable** with the tools available this session (the census site's regional tables did not open), so every package below is whole oblasts.

|---|---|---|---|

| Crimea | 77.0% | 58.3% | not polled |
| **Donetsk** | **74.9%** | 38.2% | 27.5% |
| **Luhansk** | **68.8%** | 39.0% | 30.3% |
| Zaporizhzhia | 48.2% | 24.7% | 6.2% |
| Kharkiv | 44.3% | 25.6% | 16.1% |
| Odesa | 41.9% | 20.7% | 7.2% |
| Dnipropetrovsk | 32.0% | 17.6% | 6.9% |
| Mykolaiv | 29.3% | 14.1% | 6.2% |
| Kherson | about 25% (⚠️) | 14.1% | 3.5% |
| Sumy, Poltava, Chernihiv | under about 20% (⚠️) | 5 to 9% | not in the note |

**Three things to take from it.**
1. **There is a real break in the data, and it sits between Donetsk and Luhansk and everything else.** Crimea, Sevastopol, Donetsk and Luhansk are all at 69% or more native-Russian; the next unit down is 48% (Zaporizhzhia). No other oblast is close to the four.
2. **"Consider themselves Russian" is layered, and the census separates the layers.** Language is where Russian is the clear majority (Donbas 69 to 75%). Ancestry is not: ethnic Russians were 38 to 39% in Donetsk and Luhansk, with Ukrainians the majority by ancestry (57 to 58%); only Crimea and Sevastopol have a Russian majority by ancestry. Political preference in 2014 was lower again (about three in ten in the two Donbas oblasts). What the author describes from years of living there is the language-and-culture layer: Russian-speaking, Soviet-industrial, urban. That is what the native-language column measures best, and it is why the data points to Donbas and Crimea first.
3. **The baseline is old.** It predates 2014 and 2022 and the displacement since; the 2022 shift runs toward Ukrainian self-identification in unoccupied areas, while occupation-era passportization and resettlement have changed the occupied ones in ways not measured (§7.8 of the note). Nothing here is projected past the 2083 war.

## 2. The three packages (whole oblasts)

| | Members | What it gets right | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A · Core** | Crimea, Sevastopol, Donetsk, Luhansk | The natural break in the data; existing oblast borders, so no new line; the four units where Russian is the majority language | Crimea is not joined to Donbas by land; Donetsk and Luhansk are still Ukrainian by ancestry (57 to 58%) |
| **B · Core and the land bridge** | A plus Zaporizhzhia and Kherson | Joins Crimea to Donbas by land along the Azov coast | Takes two Ukrainian-majority oblasts (70.8% and 82.0% Ukrainian by ancestry; 2014 support 6.2% and 3.5%); Kherson is only about 25% native-Russian |
| **C · The language belt** | B plus Kharkiv, Mykolaiv and Odesa | Takes every oblast at 40% or more native-Russian (Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia, Odesa); Kherson and Mykolaiv come along to keep Odesa connected | The most Ukrainian-by-ancestry territory; Mykolaiv and Kherson are well under 40%; Odesa's Budjak is a separate Bulgarian-Gagauz-Moldovan pocket (note §7.7) |

Not offered as packages: the Dnipro river line (note option 8) and the Novorossiya historical line (option 7), because both take oblasts that are 79 to 93% Ukrainian by ancestry and under 7% in 2014 support; and the line of contact (option 1), which follows arms rather than culture and moves.

## 3. Recommendation

**Start from A, and treat B's land bridge as a separate decision about geography rather than culture.**
- **A is what the evidence supports most strongly.** It is also the shape the real-world negotiation currently turns on (Russia demands the whole of the Donbas; Crimea is already held), so it would read as continuous with the world the setting grows from. ⚠️ That is my reading of September 2026 news, not a finding.
- **The land bridge is a choice about contiguity, not identity.** The data do not show Zaporizhzhia or Kherson as Russian by any measure except language in Zaporizhzhia's cities. If the author wants Crimea and the Donbas joined, the cleaner way to do it inside the project's rules is a **narrow Azov-coast corridor drawn at raion level** (the southern raions of Zaporizhzhia and Kherson that run from Mariupol to Crimea), not whole oblasts; the project has already accepted district-level exceptions for Iraq, Lebanon and the Northeast. B is the whole-oblast fallback if the author prefers the simpler rule.
- **I would not recommend C.** Kharkiv, Odesa, Mykolaiv and Kherson were 3.5 to 16% for joining Russia in 2014 and 63 to 82% Ukrainian by ancestry; adding them moves the border from where Russian culture is the majority to where it is one strong minority. If the author wants the Russian-speaking cities (Kharkiv, Odesa) represented, a **sphere** (hatching on the cultural map, as Silesia and Szeklerland are) carries that without moving the border.

The lines inside these units are the open questions. I have no raion-level numbers, and the notes' sub-oblast statements are recalled (⚠️).
1. **Crimea and Sevastopol:** in, as in A? Is the Tatar minority (about 12%) a status issue?

6. **Dnipropetrovsk:** Dnipro city is Russian-speaking inside a 79%-Ukrainian oblast; out, unless the author says otherwise.
7. **What becomes of the region:** part of Russia outright, as the author says, or a Russian-speaking unit with its own status. And the name of the unit on the map.

## 6. The author's proposed line (2026-09-30) — traced and measured

**The trace.** From the Black Sea up the Dnieper to just past the city of Dnipro, then north at Shulhivka and Prydniprianske (the author also named Molodizhne, which I could not place near Dnipro), then through Natalyne, Sydorenkove, Kolontaiv, Kaplunivka, Korbyni Ivany and Tymofiivka to the Russian border. The author's intent: the villages trace the border; the territory east of it is whole provinces. **Crimea goes to Donska-Kubania** (the Russia project's nation: Krasnodar, Rostov, Stavropol, Adygea, Kalmykia, Volgograd, Astrakhan; capital Rostov-on-Don). Map: `eastern-ukraine-proposed-line.png`; line as `eastern-ukraine-proposed-line.geojson`; script `render_ukraine_east_line.py`. Village coordinates are OpenStreetMap (Nominatim); where a name occurs more than once, the occurrence that makes a continuous path was used (Shulhivka and Prydniprianske near the Dnieper north of Dnipro; Natalyne in Berestyn Raion; Kaplunivka and Kolontaiv in Krasnokutsk hromada; Tymofiivka in Bohodukhiv hromada).

**What falls east of it (by area; Natural Earth oblast polygons against the Natural Earth Dnieper centerline, good to a few percent):**
| Unit | East of the line |
|---|---|
| Luhansk, Donetsk | all |
| Kharkiv | 97% (a thin western strip stays) |
| Zaporizhzhia | 96% (the oblast's own border is the river, so in practice whole) |
| Kherson | 75% (the whole left bank, including the isthmus to Crimea) |
| Dnipropetrovsk | 45% (the left bank and the east; Dnipro city, Kamianske, Kryvyi Rih and Nikopol stay west) |
| Poltava | 0.3% (a sliver at the Prydniprianske corner) |
| Crimea, Sevastopol | all (to Donska-Kubania) |

**Scale.** Whole-oblast populations from the 2001 census counts in the note (Donetsk about 4.82 M, Luhansk about 2.54 M, Kharkiv about 2.90 M, Zaporizhzhia about 1.93 M, Crimea about 2.02 M, Sevastopol about 0.38 M) come to about 14.4 million, plus the east-of-the-river parts of Kherson and Dnipropetrovsk (roughly another 1 million; ⚠️ an area-weighted guess), so **about 14 to 16 million people, roughly a third of Ukraine's 2001 population of 48.5 million** (⚠️ rough, 2001 baseline).

**Why it is a good line.**
- **It uses the Dnieper where the river is the real seam** (Left Bank and Right Bank; the Sich lands and Novorossiya east, the Right-Bank heartland west) and leaves the river exactly where the data flip: the northern leg runs along the western edge of Kharkiv Oblast, which is the Sloboda Ukraine and Hetmanate divide, and on the far side Russian-native falls below about 15% (Poltava, Sumy).
- **It solves contiguity.** Crimea's only land link to the mainland is the Kherson left bank; with Zaporizhzhia and Donetsk it joins Crimea to the Donbas, and the Donbas touches Rostov (Donska-Kubania) directly.
- **In the north it mostly follows existing oblast borders,** so the only places that need district-level units are Kherson and Dnipropetrovsk.
- **It avoids the flaw in package C:** Odesa, Mykolaiv, Kherson city, Dnipro city and Kryvyi Rih, which are the most Ukrainian-oriented parts of the belt, stay west.

**What it costs and what to decide.**
1. **It takes Ukrainian-majority-by-ancestry territory:** Zaporizhzhia (70.8%; 2014 support 6.2%), Kharkiv (70.7%; 16.1%), most of Kherson (82.0%; 3.5%). The justification is the river and contiguity, not the census.
2. **Dnipro city (about a million, Russian-speaking) falls west** because the river rule puts it on the right bank; Zaporizhzhia city falls east. Intended?
3. **Crimea's link depends on Kherson's left bank.** If the author does not need land contiguity, Kherson could stay wholly Ukrainian and Crimea would be an exclave across the isthmus.
4. **Dnipropetrovsk split or whole.** The alternative is a whole-oblast variant (Dnipropetrovsk entirely west, line on the Zaporizhzhia-Dnipropetrovsk and Kharkiv-Dnipropetrovsk oblast borders), which avoids cutting a 3.6-million, 32%-Russian-native oblast and keeps Dnipro whole.
5. **Which nation gets the mainland slice.** Crimea goes to Donska-Kubania. Luhansk, Donetsk, Zaporizhzhia and the Kherson left bank border Rostov or the Sea of Azov; Kharkiv borders Belgorod, which is in the Russia nation. Options: all to Donska-Kubania, or Donbas and the south to Donska-Kubania with Kharkiv to Russia. Either changes Donska-Kubania's weight a great deal (about 2.4 million from Crimea alone, about 15 million for the whole slice).
6. **The river's course:** the line uses the pre-2023 Dnieper and reservoirs (the Kakhovka Reservoir was drained in 2023).

**How to implement at district level** (the project has accepted district exceptions elsewhere): the 2020 raions split along roughly the same line. Kherson: Henichesk, Skadovsk and Kakhovka raions east, Kherson and Beryslav raions west (⚠️ the Kherson raion includes some left-bank settlements such as Oleshky). Dnipropetrovsk: Pavlohrad, Synelnykove and Novomoskovsk raions east, Dnipro, Kamianske, Kryvyi Rih and Nikopol raions west (⚠️ recalled, to be checked against the raion boundaries). That would make the line a set of whole raions in the two split oblasts and whole oblasts elsewhere.

## 7. Version 2 — the author's clarification (2026-09-30)

**What the author confirmed.** The villages were only a way of tracing the border (the author did not know the oblast names). The territory is: **Luhansk, Donetsk, Kharkiv, Dnipropetrovsk and Zaporizhzhia in full; Kherson Oblast only east of the Dnieper; Crimea and Sevastopol to Donska-Kubania** (the Russia project's nation, capital Rostov-on-Don). This supersedes the Dnipropetrovsk split in §6. Map: `eastern-ukraine-proposed-line-v2.png`; border as `eastern-ukraine-proposed-border-v2.geojson`; script `render_ukraine_east_line_v2.py` (version 1 is kept for comparison).

**The villages match the oblast borders.** Measured against the Natural Earth oblast borders, each village lies within 10 km of the border of the territory: Prydniprianske 0.0 km, Tymofiivka 0.4 km, Korbyni Ivany 1.8 km, Sydorenkove 2.8 km, Kaplunivka 4.2 km, Shulhivka 8.2 km, Kolontaiv 8.5 km, Natalyne 9.8 km (within the generalization of the boundary data). So the traced line and the whole-oblast border are the same line: Kherson's Dnieper left bank, then Dnipropetrovsk's western edge (with Kherson, Mykolaiv, Kirovohrad), then Poltava's and Sumy's eastern edges, then Kharkiv's western edge to the Russian frontier.
