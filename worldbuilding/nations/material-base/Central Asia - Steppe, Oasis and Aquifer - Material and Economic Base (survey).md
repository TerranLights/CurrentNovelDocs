# Central Asia — Steppe, Oasis and Aquifer — Material and Economic Base

2026-09-28. **Survey only.** What **Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan**
physically hold, and what survives a collapse.

## 0. The organizing fact — a closed basin, and the sea it used to feed

Every regional material survey in this project so far has organized around one physical fact that decides
more than any single nation's politics: East Asia's Great Wall line, Mainland Southeast Asia's Mekong,
Maritime Southeast Asia's Strait of Malacca. **Central Asia's is a closed drainage basin with only two rivers
of consequence, and a sea at the bottom of it that is now mostly gone.**

The **Syr Darya and Amu Darya** together supply roughly 90% of the region's water (frameworks survey §4). Both
rise in the glaciers and snowpack of Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan and run down onto the arid Turan lowland, where
for millennia they terminated in the **Aral Sea** — until Soviet planners diverted roughly 75% of their
combined flow starting in the 1950s to grow cotton. The lake has since fallen from the world's **fourth-largest**
(26,300 sq mi) to a pair of shrinking remnants near 8,000 sq km combined, still losing 80–110 cm of depth a
year (frameworks survey §7, not repeated in full here).

**What the material survey adds to that finding**: the basin is closed, which means every drop of water used
anywhere in it is a drop not reaching everywhere downstream of it, with no ocean outlet to renew the account
and no alternate basin to fall back on. East Asia's rivers leave Chinese territory and return; Mainland
Southeast Asia's Mekong never enters any one territory that can fully protect it; **Central Asia's water
problem is the third variant — the river's entire catchment sits inside the same five countries this survey
covers, so there is no external actor to blame and no external fix to import.** Whatever water leaves the
Pamirs and Tian Shan as snowmelt is the entire regional water budget for the year, full stop.

## 1. Food and fiber — cotton first, wheat second, and a fiber crop that is not food

### 1.1 Cotton — a crop chosen for export value, grown at a scale the basin cannot sustain

**Uzbekistan** remains the region's dominant cotton grower despite decades of reform. **MY 2026/27 lint
production is forecast at 780,000 metric tons across 891,000 hectares** — down from the Soviet-era peak that
originally drained the Aral Sea, but still a serious water draw: cotton in Uzbekistan receives an estimated
**10,000–12,000 cubic meters of irrigation water per hectare**, virtually all of it diverted, none of it
rain-fed. **The USDA's own 2026 assessment names hydrological deficit as the season's primary downside risk** —
Amu Darya flow was measured at just **66.8% of its historical norm** heading into the 2026/27 season. This is
not a hypothetical future stress; it is the current growing season's stated limiting factor.

**⚠️ A real structural pivot is underway, not yet complete.** Uzbekistan's MY 2026/27 raw lint exports are
projected at **zero** — the government is deliberately steering the sector toward higher-value finished
textiles (fabric, garments) rather than exporting raw fiber, backed by what the USDA source calls "record
government investment." Whether this reduces the sector's total water draw, or simply moves the same water
use one processing step downstream, was not established by this pass — flagged as open.

### 1.2 Wheat and grain — Kazakhstan's steppe, and the only rain-fed staple crop in this survey

**The Kazakh Steppe is one of the world's largest continuous grain-growing regions**, producing high-protein
milling wheat that supplies Central Asia, Afghanistan, and increasingly markets beyond the region. **Grain and
flour exports (grain equivalent) ran 11.1 million metric tons in MY 2025/26**, with wheat specifically at
roughly **11.7 million tons that season** (figures vary 8.6–13.4 million tons across sources depending on
whether flour-equivalent conversion is included — treat as a wide but real band, not a single precise figure).
**The 2026/27 forecast is a genuine decline — 10.5 million tons of total grain exports, of which 9 million
tons is wheat** — driven by farmers shifting acreage toward more profitable oilseed crops rather than any
water-supply crisis. **This is the one major food crop in the whole survey that is substantially rain-fed
rather than irrigation-dependent** — the steppe's continental climate supports dryland wheat farming the way
none of the oasis agriculture further south can. It is also, structurally, this region's only real food-export
surplus; every other country in the set is a net food importer or subsistence-level producer by comparison.

## 2. Minerals and hydrocarbons — extraordinarily uneven, and concentrated in exactly two countries

Restating and grounding frameworks-survey finding 6 in full figures. Central Asia's mineral wealth sits almost
entirely in Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan; Uzbekistan holds one world-class asset of its own; Kyrgyzstan and
Tajikistan hold comparatively little beyond the hydropower already covered in the companion survey.

### 2.1 Kazakhstan — uranium, oil, and a pipeline that runs through someone else's country

- **World's largest uranium producer since 2009.** Kazatomprom produced **~25,839 tU in 2025** (~39–40% of
  global output, entirely via **in-situ leach (ISL) mining** — acid pumped directly into the ore body rather
  than conventional open-pit or underground extraction). **✅ Updated 2026-09-28**: Kazatomprom is cutting 2026
  output by roughly 10%, from ~32,777 tU nominal capacity down to ~29,697 tU, explicitly a "value over volume"
  market-balancing decision, not a resource or technical constraint — **the country is choosing to produce
  less uranium than it could**, a fact worth remembering if a post-collapse setting needs a reason production
  could be ramped back up quickly rather than rebuilt from scratch. ISL mining is also unusually vulnerable to
  a specific single-point input: **sulphuric acid supply**, the key reagent in the process, has constrained
  output in prior years even though 2026 supply is currently rated stable.
- **Top-12 global proven oil reserves (~30 billion barrels)**, anchored by **Tengiz** (Kazakhstan's largest
  field, contributing **36.6 million of the 63 million tons** carried by the CPC pipeline in 2025) and
  **Kashagan** (~35 billion barrels total reserves, one of the largest global discoveries in 50 years).
- **✅ CLOSED — the export chokepoint, precisely quantified.** The **Caspian Pipeline Consortium (CPC)** line
  runs **1,511 km from western Kazakhstan through Russian territory** to a marine terminal near Novorossiysk
  on the Black Sea, and **carries more than 80% of all Kazakh oil exports** — roughly **2% of global daily
  crude supply** through one pipe. **2025 throughput: ~63 million tons; 2026 target: ~72 million tons**
  (nameplate capacity ~72.5 million tons/year from Kazakhstan, up to 83 million tons/year system-wide including
  Russian-origin barrels). **This is a real, current, single-country dependency**: Kazakhstan's single largest
  export earner physically transits Russian territory for its entire route to open water, with **no comparable
  alternate capacity** — a documented "capacity gap" that multiple 2026 sources flag as an active policy
  concern in Astana, not a settled arrangement. Combined with oil/gas revenue accounting for **40–50% of
  government revenue and 60–70% of export value** (frameworks survey §3.1, restated here as the material
  anchor for that political finding), **Kazakhstan's entire fiscal base runs through one pipeline it does not
  control the territory of.**

### 2.2 Turkmenistan — the world's second-largest gas field, converted almost entirely into one customer

- **The Galkynysh field (with neighboring Garakol and Yashlar) is independently estimated at 27.4 trillion
  cubic meters of gas** — the **world's second-largest gas field**, behind only Iran/Qatar's shared South
  Pars/North Dome. **2026 planned national output: ~76 billion cubic meters (Bcm).**
- **✅ CLOSED — the customer concentration is total, not merely dominant.** **Around 90% of all Turkmenistan's
  gas exports move via the China-Central Asia pipeline system, currently supplying 30+ Bcm/year to China
  alone**, with Turkmenistan's own stated ambition to eventually reach 65 Bcm/year to that single buyer. **A
  fourth development phase at Galkynysh, plus a new gas-processing plant (10 Bcm capacity), is under active
  construction as a joint CNPC-Turkmengaz project as of 2026** — i.e., the dependency is actively deepening,
  not stabilizing or diversifying. Combined with the frameworks survey's own finding that Turkmenistan converts
  this wealth into extreme political isolation rather than regional leverage (§3.3), the material picture
  completes that finding: **isolation and a single-customer gas trade are the same fact seen from two angles.**
  A country need not be economically integrated with its neighbors to be completely economically dependent on
  one of them.

### 2.3 Uzbekistan — the world's largest gold mine, structurally separate from the water problem

- **Muruntau, in the Kyzylkum Desert (Navoiy Region), is believed to hold reserves of over 170 million ounces
  of gold** and is **the largest gold mine in the world by production volume.** A **"Stage 5" expansion** is
  underway, intended to raise processing capacity from 38.5 to 50 million tons of ore per year by 2026.
  **This is materially significant beyond the resource itself**: unlike cotton, gold mining in the Kyzylkum
  Desert draws on groundwater and processing water at a scale that does not compete directly with the Amu
  Darya/Syr Darya irrigation system the way cotton does — **Uzbekistan holds one genuinely water-decoupled
  major export asset**, a real structural difference from its cotton-dependent agricultural economy.
- **⚠️ Karakalpakstan — this survey's own Fergana-tier finding, not previously flagged in the companion
  frameworks survey.** The **Republic of Karakalpakstan** is a constitutionally autonomous republic inside
  Uzbekistan (capital Nukus, population ~2.15 million per the 2026 census, ~166,590 km² — roughly 37% of
  Uzbekistan's total land area on a fraction of its population), ethnically distinct (Karakalpak, a Turkic
  people closer to Kazakh than to Uzbek), and **sits directly on the Uzbek shore of the dying Aral Sea** — the
  fishing port of Muynak, now stranded tens of kilometers from open water, is inside its territory. **In July
  2022, proposed constitutional amendments that would have stripped Karakalpakstan's right to secede via
  referendum (a right it has held since 1993, under Article 89 of Uzbekistan's constitution) triggered violent
  protests in Nukus** — Uzbek security forces used lethal force, over 500 people were arrested, and President
  Mirziyoyev withdrew the amendments within days. **This is a real, structurally load-bearing case, the same
  tier as Tuva in this project's own Russia survey**: an ethnically distinct, constitutionally autonomous
  region with an actual (if currently unexercised) legal secession clause, sitting on the single most visible
  ecological disaster zone in the entire region. **Flagged here for Stage 3** — the whole-first-order-
  subdivision rule already treats Karakalpakstan as one drawable unit (it is Uzbekistan's own first-order
  autonomous republic), so no enclave-style problem exists the way it does in the Fergana Valley — but the
  political-autonomy question (does it stay Uzbek territory, or become its own case) is a live one the author
  may want to weigh the same way Tuva's or Karelia's was.

### 2.4 Kyrgyzstan — one mine carrying a disproportionate share of a small economy

- **The Kumtor gold mine accounts for roughly 12% of Kyrgyzstan's entire GDP and 23% of industrial output.**
  Gold was the country's top single export in 2024 at **$6.18 billion**, and in one recent year the mine alone
  generated **17.2 billion som (over $194 million) in taxes and social payments — about one-third of the
  entire national budget.** **This is a single-asset dependency of the same shape as Timor-Leste's Bayu-Undan
  field in the Maritime Southeast Asia survey**, though still in active production rather than depleted.
- **Economic Complexity Index**: ranked **75th of 130 economies (Trade ECI)** and **101st of 138 (Research
  ECI)** per the OEC/Harvard framework, 2024 data — filling the one gap the companion frameworks survey left
  open (it could not find a Kyrgyzstan-specific ECI figure).

### 2.5 Tajikistan — minerals genuinely secondary to the hydropower already covered

No major mineral asset comparable to the above was found in this pass. Tajikistan's material profile is
almost entirely the Rogun Dam/hydropower story the companion frameworks survey already covers in depth (§4);
see §3 below for this survey's own material update on Rogun's actual construction status.

## 3. Water as infrastructure — the Rogun Dam's real, current construction status

The frameworks survey establishes the upstream/downstream political fracture in full (§4); this section adds
the physical construction facts a material survey needs and the frameworks pass did not carry.

**✅ CLOSED — Rogun's construction status, precisely dated 2026-09-28.** The dam has reached **1,155 meters
above sea level — roughly 190 of its planned 335 meters, just over half its final height.** It is **already
generating power despite being incomplete**: the first of six planned 600 MW turbines was commissioned in
2018, the second in 2019, and the completed plant's total installed capacity will be **3,600 MW — expected to
double Tajikistan's total electricity-generation capacity.** Construction is active and reported on schedule:
**~49 million cubic meters of fill material placed (more than half the total planned volume)**, over
**500,000 linear meters of foundation drilling and grouting completed.** More than 20,000 workers are involved
as of mid-2026.

**Why this matters materially, beyond the politics §4 of the companion survey already covers**: Rogun is not a
future hypothetical the way the frameworks survey's own framing might read in isolation — **it is a
half-built, partially operational piece of infrastructure today**, meaning any post-collapse scenario has to
reckon with a specific physical object at a specific height, generating a specific fraction of its eventual
output, rather than an either/or "does it get built" question. A collapse that hits mid-construction leaves a
half-height dam with an incomplete spillway and no crew to finish it — a materially different failure mode
than either "no dam" or "completed dam," and one this survey did not find existing analysis of. Flagged as
open.

## 4. Ports, pipelines and the emerging land-bridge

**Central Asia's ocean access, in its entirety, runs through the Caspian Sea — a landlocked sea with no
outlet — and two ports on opposite shores of it.**

| Port | Country | Capacity | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aktau** | Kazakhstan | ~12 million tons/year total throughput; three dry-cargo + three oil terminals | Kazakhstan's own oil exports to Russia (Makhachkala) and Azerbaijan; grain exports to Iran |
| **Turkmenbashi** | Turkmenistan | ~17 million tons/year total (9.2M tons oil/oil-products terminal + 4M tons ferry terminal) | Turkmenistan's main cargo/passenger port; ferry links to Baku (Azerbaijan) and Astrakhan (Russia) as well as Aktau |

**Both ports work as a linked pair** — a car-and-rail ferry service connects Aktau and Turkmenbashi directly
across the Caspian, and both sit on the same Europe-Caucasus-Asia trade route. **Neither port, individually or
combined, offers anything close to the throughput of the region's overland hydrocarbon pipelines** (§2.1's CPC
alone moves roughly six times Aktau's total capacity) — the Caspian ports matter for diversification and for
non-Russian-transiting trade, not as the primary export channel for either country's flagship resource.

**✅ CLOSED, added 2026-09-28 — the Middle Corridor/Trans-Caspian International Transport Route (TITR) is real,
current, and growing fast, though still small in absolute terms.** A ~4,000 km multimodal corridor linking
western China to Eastern Europe via Kazakhstan, the Caspian Sea, the South Caucasus, the Black Sea and Turkey —
explicitly the **land bridge across this entire survey's northern tier**, and the corridor's own stated purpose
is to be a **Russia-bypass route**, which places it in direct structural tension with Kazakhstan's own CPC
pipeline dependency on Russian transit territory (§2.1) — the same country simultaneously depends on Russian
soil for its oil exports and is investing in a route explicitly designed to route everything else around
Russia.

- **2026 volume: ~44,000 TEU in the first eight months, a 58% year-on-year increase**; container trains from
  China up **84% year-on-year to 419 trains** over the same period.
- **2025 volume: 63,300 TEU over the first ten months, up 37% year-on-year.**
- **Target: 600 China-origin container trains by the end of 2026, rising to 800–1,000 in 2027, with total
  cargo throughput targeted at 11 million tons/year by 2030** (against 2026's low-tens-of-thousands-of-TEU
  actual volume — a genuinely ambitious growth target, not yet close to being met).
- ⚠️ **A live, credible skeptical case exists.** A 2026 Carnegie Endowment analysis is titled outright "The
  Much-Touted Middle Corridor Transport Route Could Prove a Dead End" — this survey did not resolve the
  optimist/skeptic disagreement, and flags it as a genuinely open question rather than picking a side. The
  corridor's fundamental vulnerability (a Caspian Sea crossing with limited ferry/port capacity feeding into a
  rail network still being built out) is real and documented on both sides of the debate.

**Structural read**: the Middle Corridor is Central Asia's version of a bet on infrastructure that does not yet
exist at the scale its backers want, the same category of forward-looking, unresolved-outcome asset as
Myanmar's planned Dawei deep-sea port in the Mainland Southeast Asia survey — worth naming as a future-facing
asset, not as an already-realized one.

**⚠️ Partially closed.** No single source gave all five countries on one consistent methodology; the table

| Country | ECI rank / score | Leading exports |
|---|---|---|

## 7. Post-collapse assessment

Using the project's three-tier framework: **survives regardless / repairable with effort / gone for good.**

### Survives regardless

- **Kazakh Steppe wheat.** Substantially rain-fed dryland farming, the one staple crop in this survey not
  hostage to a shrinking irrigation budget. **This region feeds its own grain-eaters even in collapse, the
  same structural note Mainland Southeast Asia's rice made about itself.**
- **Gold mining at Muruntau and Kumtor.** Neither requires a functioning export economy to keep producing — a
  metal, unlike a cubic meter of gas, does not need a pipeline to remain valuable, and both operations are
  large-scale, established, and (per §2.3) substantially water-decoupled from the region's central irrigation
  problem.
- **Gems and small-scale extraction generally** — the same "needs eyes and hands, not a supply chain" logic
  the Mainland Southeast Asia survey applied to Myanmar's rubies applies here to any small-scale artisanal
  mining that survives a state collapse.

### Repairable with effort

- **Irrigation canal networks** feeding cotton and other oasis agriculture — earthworks, in principle
  repairable by hand the way the Mainland Southeast Asia survey judged Thailand's Chao Phraya irrigation works,
  though the scale (10,000–12,000 m³/ha for cotton alone) is a much heavier structural demand than rice.
- **The Rogun Dam, at whatever height a collapse catches it.** The concrete and fill material placed by that
  point do not disappear; finishing the remaining structure, spillway and turbine installation is a
  labor/capital problem, not a from-scratch one — directly parallel to the Laos finding in the Mainland
  Southeast Asia survey ("the debt is unenforceable and the dams are still standing").
- **The Caspian ports (Aktau, Turkmenbashi)** — physical infrastructure that predates any hypothetical
  collapse and needs crews and fuel to operate, not reconstruction.

### Gone for good

- **The Galkynysh gas export trade to China**, and by extension Turkmenistan's entire fiscal model — a
  90%-single-customer pipeline trade requires both an intact pipeline network across intact territory and an
  intact customer state on the other end; either failure alone ends the trade.
- **The CPC pipeline export route**, on the same logic, with the added fragility that it physically transits a
  second country's territory (§2.1) — a double point of failure, not a single one.
- **Uzbekistan's cotton-export scale as currently practiced.** The crop itself can be grown at a smaller,
  subsistence-fiber scale, but the export-oriented processing/milling/shipping chain the USDA's own 2026
  reporting describes (the pivot toward finished textiles specifically) is exactly the kind of
  logistics-dependent value-add the Mainland Southeast Asia survey already flagged as fragile for palm oil and
  milled rice alike.
- **The Middle Corridor's growth trajectory as currently projected** — a young, ferry-dependent, still-being-
  built-out corridor is a poor candidate to survive the kind of shock this project's collapse scenarios
  generally assume, regardless of which side of the Carnegie/optimist disagreement (§4) turns out to be right.

### ⚠️ The open question that decides the region, restated with this survey's own numbers

The companion frameworks survey already identifies the upstream/downstream water divide and the Rogun
flashpoint as the region's central structural fracture (§4 there). **This material survey adds one further
open variable the frameworks pass did not have the figures for**: Rogun is currently **~57% complete by
height** (1,155m of a planned unspecified final elevation reaching 335m total structure height) and **already
supplies a meaningful fraction of Tajikistan's power from two of its eventual six turbines.** How a collapse
scenario handles a half-finished, partially-operational dam of this scale — abandoned mid-build, seized by one
riparian state, finished by surviving institutions, or left to fail structurally — is a real open design
question this pass surfaces but does not resolve, the same "author decision, not a research gap" category the
Mainland Southeast Asia survey used for its own Mekong dam-failure-mode question.

## 8. What this means for the nations layer

Offered, not asserted, per this project's standing rule.

1. **Kazakhstan's fiscal base and Uzbekistan's export pivot point in opposite directions** — one nation's
   wealth runs through a single foreign-territory pipeline (§2.1), the other is actively trying to convert a
   water-hungry raw-commodity trade into a less resource-intensive finished-goods trade (§1.1). A post-collapse
   Kazakhstan without CPC access is materially crippled in a way a post-collapse Uzbekistan without cotton
   exports specifically is not, if the textile pivot has meaningfully progressed by the time of any collapse.
2. **Karakalpakstan (§2.3) is this survey's own Tuva-tier open case** — a constitutionally distinct, recently-
   contested autonomous region sitting on the region's most visible ecological disaster, with a real (if
   currently dormant) legal secession mechanism. Worth the same weight of consideration the author already gave
   Tuva in the Russia survey, though the political shape is different (Tuva's case is ethnic-majority
   secession; Karakalpakstan's 2022 unrest was specifically about *retaining* an existing right, not asserting
   a new one).
3. **Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan hold the physical water infrastructure but almost none of the region's other
   wealth** — meaning any post-collapse leverage they have runs entirely through §3's dam and the remittance
   dependency the frameworks survey already covers (§5 there), not through any mineral or export asset this
   material pass could find.
4. **The Middle Corridor, if it succeeds, restructures Kazakhstan's entire trade orientation away from Russia**
   — worth remembering as a live, currently-unresolved tension the same shape as this project's other
   "which direction does this country's dependency actually point" findings (Guyana/Suriname/French Guiana's
   language-and-colonial-history tri-lateral pull in the Latin America survey is the closest parallel).

10. **✅ Population, GDP and first-order subdivision counts for all five countries — closed** (§6), directly
    resolving the frameworks survey's own flagged gap on Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan population, and providing
    subdivision counts the frameworks survey did not attempt for any of the five states.
11. **⚠️ The Rogun mid-collapse failure-mode question (§7) is a forward-looking scenario question, not a fact
    gap** — an author decision, not something a further research pass resolves, the same category this
    project's other regional surveys have already used for their own single biggest open dam/infrastructure
    question.

**What remains genuinely open after this pass**: Tajikistan's own mineral base (item 6), Turkmenistan's gas
revenue in dollar terms (item 3), the Middle Corridor's ultimate viability (item 8), and the Rogun
failure-mode scenario question (item 11) — everything else in this list is closed.
