# East Asia — Material and Economic Base (survey)

**What this file is:** a factual, real-world research baseline covering **China, Japan, Korea, Mongolia and
Taiwan**, compiled 2026-09-23. It is *not* fiction and contains no invented content. It documents what the ground
actually holds — geology, soil, climate, rivers, fisheries, industry — so the author can design post-collapse
successor nations from an accurate material floor rather than from guesswork. It does not design any of them. It
supplies the ground; the nations are derived separately. The closing **Post-Collapse Assessment** is the only
interpretive section, and it reasons *from* the facts above it.

**This is a survey, not a country-by-country buildout.** The aim is to make the *shape* of the region's material
position legible. Where a province, a deposit or a port is named, it is named because it carries weight in that
shape.

**Scope, stated precisely.**

**In scope:**

- **China**, all of it, reported on both sides of the line defined below.
- **Japan**, **South Korea** and **North Korea**.
- **Mongolia** — the independent state.
- **Taiwan**.

**Explicitly out of scope, and named here so the boundary is not mistaken for an omission:**

- **Southeast Asia** — Vietnam, Laos, Burma, Thailand and the archipelago. The Mekong and Salween leave this
  survey's area and are followed no further than the Chinese border.
- **South Asia** — India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh. The Brahmaputra and Indus are noted only at their
  Tibetan sources.
- **Central Asia** — Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and Afghanistan.
- **Russia**, including the Russian Far East, Sakhalin and Kamchatka. Where the Sea of Okhotsk is described it is
  described as a fishery abutting the region, not as territory in it.

All four abut the region and several of them are materially entangled with it — Mongolian coal moves south,
Kazakh and Russian ore moves east, the Tarim's oases face west. Those linkages are noted where they bear on the
assessment, and no further.

## 0. THE LINE — and why this file is organized around it

**The author has settled that the post-war Sinian Federation is the Han core only — roughly the Qing Eighteen
Provinces, inside the Great Wall. Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia and Manchuria are separate polities.**

That decision is not a detail. It changes who owns what, and **every mineral, basin, river and port in this file is
therefore tagged inside the line or outside the line.** Where a figure is national and cannot be split, it is
stated as national and flagged.

### 0.1 What the line actually is

Three real-world lines nearly coincide, and the author should know that the border chosen is also a climate border
and a demographic border:

- **The Qing Eighteen Provinces.** The Qing kept the Ming provincial system for the former Ming lands and did not
  extend it to Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, Taiwan or Tibet. Those eighteen provinces are what early Western
  sources meant by "China proper." Taiwan was administered under Fujian until it became a separate province in
  1885 and was ceded to Japan in 1895; Xinjiang became a province in 1884; Manchuria was split into Fengtian,
  Jilin and Heilongjiang only in 1907. Since then: Beijing and Tianjin split from Zhili (now Hebei), Shanghai from
  Jiangsu, Chongqing from Sichuan, Ningxia from Gansu, and Hainan from Guangdong
  ([China proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_proper)).
- **The 400 mm annual precipitation isohyet.** The Great Wall is commonly used as the approximate boundary between
  the Han-dominated core and the frontier, and **it corresponds roughly to the 400 mm rainfall line**, which
  separates country that supports settled cereal agriculture from country that supports only pastoralism (same
  source).
- **The Hu Line** (Heihe–Tengchong Line), drawn by the demographer Hu Huanyong in 1935. East of it: **43% of the
  territory and 94% of the population.** West of it: 57% of the territory and 6% of the population. The 1935
  figures were 36%/96% and 64%/4%; the ratio has barely moved in ninety years. It also approximates that same
  400 mm isohyet ([Heihe–Tengchong Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heihe%E2%80%93Tengchong_Line)).

**So the border the author has chosen is the boundary between rain-fed agriculture and rangeland.** That is an
unusually durable thing to build a nation on, and it is the reason this line has been redrawn in roughly the same
place for two thousand years.

### 0.2 The second line, inside the first — the wheat/rice divide

Within the core there is a second and equally old division: the **Qinling–Huaihe Line**, running from the Qin
Mountains in the west to the Huai River in the east, roughly along the 33rd parallel. It coincides with three
things at once:

- **the 800 mm annual precipitation isohyet** — the division between China's humid and semi-humid zones, and
  therefore **between rice country and wheat country**;
- **the 0 °C average January isotherm**;
- **the drainage divide between the Yangtze and the Yellow River.**

It has been a political border more than once — between the Northern and Southern dynasties, and between the
Southern Song and the Jin. Subsidized district heating was installed only north of it in the 1950s. The southern
half's gross regional product was equal to the northern half's in 1960 and **83% larger by 2019**
([Qinling–Huaihe Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qinling%E2%80%93Huaihe_Line)).

**For §6 this is the load-bearing fact: south of the Huai, cereal agriculture is rain-fed. North of it, at current
yields, it is not.**

### 0.3 The third layer — the Han core is a federation of linguistic nations

The author has settled that the Sinian Federation is drawn with internal divisions along the mutually
unintelligible Sinitic languages. The companion file
`worldbuilding/nations/China - Ethnolinguistic Map (read from reference).md` reads those divisions off the
author's language map, and **this survey defers to it entirely on the linguistic layer.** Two corrections from
that file govern how assets are tagged below:

- **The Sinitic layer is eight-plus groups, not seven.** **Jin** (over Shanxi), **Ping** (central Guangxi) and
  **Huizhou** (adjoining Wu) are mapped separately from Mandarin, Yue and Wu.
- **Min is five separate languages** — Bei, Dong, Zhong, Nan and Pu-Xian — not one. **It is Min-Nan specifically
  that covers both the southern Fujian coast and Taiwan**, and that single fact is the only Sinitic bridge across
  the strait.

Where an industrial asset below sits clearly inside one of these regions, it is tagged. The short version, because
it recurs:

### 0.4 The shape of the thing

Five materially distinct blocks:

1. **The Han core inside the Wall** — roughly **1.26 billion people**, the North China Plain, the Yangtze basin,
   the world's largest concentration of ports and steel capacity, and a mineral base that is deep in some things
   and thin in the two that matter most for steel.
2. **The outer territories** — Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia and Manchuria. Roughly **156 million people**, and
   between them **the world's largest rare earth deposit, about 40% of China's coal reserves, the headwaters of
   the Yangtze and the Yellow River, the best agricultural soil in East Asia, and the country's founding oil
   field.**
3. **Japan** — the highest or second-highest economic complexity on Earth, **no copper ore, no iron ore, almost no
   coal, and 97% of its oil imported.**
4. **Korea and Taiwan** — between them the most advanced semiconductor fabrication in existence, on two small
   territories, both importing nearly all of their fuel.
5. **Mongolia** — 3.5 million people on 1.56 million km², holding world-scale copper, coking coal and fluorspar,
   and the lowest economic complexity in the region by a very wide margin.

**The one-sentence headline, argued in §6: East Asia is simultaneously the world's workshop and the world's most
import-dependent region, and a collapse deletes the first condition while leaving the second as a permanent fact
about geography — and for the Sinian Federation specifically, the line the author has drawn puts the people on one
side and a very large share of the inputs on the other.**

## 1. Economic Complexity — the Quantified Backbone

### 1.1 What ECI is

The **Economic Complexity Index** ranks economies by the diversity and ubiquity of what they successfully export —
in effect, how much productive *know-how* a society has accumulated and can recombine. A country that exports many
things few others can make scores high. A country that exports one thing many others could make scores low. It is
not a measure of wealth and it is **emphatically not a measure of what is in the ground.** That distinction is the
hinge of this file, exactly as it was for the Latin America survey.

All values below are from the **Observatory of Economic Complexity**, HS92 four-digit cube, retrieved directly
from the OEC API on 2026-09-23
([OEC Economic Complexity data](https://oec.world/en/resources/bulk-download/international?category=ECI&topic=Complexity)).

### 1.3 Japan's twenty-eight years, and the year it ended

**Japan ranked first in the world in economic complexity every single year from 1995 through 2022 — twenty-eight
consecutive years — and was displaced by Taiwan in 2023 and again in 2024.** This is the single most striking
finding in the section and it answers the brief's question directly: Japan's position at the top was not a recent
snapshot artifact but a generation-long fact, and it is over.

Japan's absolute score peaked at **2.411 in 2020** and has fallen to **2.200**. Taiwan's rose from 0.806 in 1995 to
**2.214**. The crossover is small — 0.014 in 2024 — and could reverse. What is not small is Taiwan's trajectory:
**twentieth in the world in 1995, first in 2023.**

### 1.6 What ECI does and does not predict about rebuilding

The Latin America survey's argument applies here in a sharper form, because East Asia sits at the opposite end of
the same axis.

ECI is a genuinely good predictor of one specific thing: **the breadth of technical and organizational know-how
resident in a population.** On that reading Japan, Taiwan and South Korea begin a reconstruction with the largest
inherited advantage of any societies on Earth, China with a very large one, and Mongolia with essentially none.

**But the ranking partially inverts over a long enough horizon, for three reasons, and the third is specific to
this region:**

1. **Know-how decays across generations; geology does not.** A century and a half out, the accumulated advantage
   has largely evaporated. What is left is ore bodies, soil, rainfall and river gradient.
2. **The high scorers are high because they are integrated into a system that is gone.** Taiwan's 43.41%
   integrated-circuit share is not a domestic capability that can be exercised in isolation; it is a position in a
   global supply chain. Japan's copper refineries are fed entirely by imported concentrate.
3. **East Asia's complexity is unusually *material*-hungry rather than *material*-backed.** Latin America's low
   scorers were low while sitting on copper, tin and iron. East Asia's high scorers are high while sitting on
   almost nothing. **A collapse does not merely reduce these economies; for Japan, Korea and Taiwan it removes the
   inputs that the economy was built to process.** That is a different and harsher failure mode than the one the
   Latin America file describes.

**The right way to use this table is as a measure of how far each society has to fall, not of how high it can
climb back.** The spread between Taiwan at +2.214 and Mongolia at −1.125 is the largest in any of the author's
regional files, and it describes 2026 economic organization, not 2283 material prospects.

**China produced about 69% of the world's rare earths in 2024 and holds roughly 49% of world reserves.**

**Bayan Obo, and it is outside the line.** The Bayan Obo Mining District sits in western **Inner Mongolia**,
administered by **Baotou City**, whose downtown is more than 120 km to the south. It was discovered as an iron
deposit in 1927; rare-earth minerals were identified in 1936 and niobium-bearing ores in the late 1950s. The
deposit is:

- **more than 40 million tonnes of rare-earth minerals grading 3–5.4% REE — described as roughly 70% of the
  world's known REE reserves;**
- **1 million tonnes of Nb₂O₅** (niobium — see the Latin America file §2.4 for why that matters to steel);
- **470 million tonnes of iron;**
- **approximately 130 million tonnes of fluorite.**

**It is the world's largest known rare earth deposit, and the fluorite content also makes it the world's largest
fluorite deposit.** As of 2005 it was responsible for **45% of global rare-earth production**. Two circular open
pits are visible along with tailings ponds and piles; the mined area doubled over twenty-five years and new pits
opened to the west from 2013. The ore occurs in an east–west Mesoproterozoic rift zone on the northern margin of
the Sino-Korean Craton, hosted chiefly in dolomite, with stratiform and lenticular orebodies
([Bayan Obo Mining District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayan_Obo_Mining_District)).

**The processing side matters for §6.** Per the Chinese Society of Rare Earths, every tonne of rare metals mined
there releases **9,600–12,000 m³ of waste gas** carrying dust concentrate, hydrofluoric acid, sulfur dioxide and
sulfuric acid, plus roughly **75 m³ of acidic wastewater and about one tonne of radioactive residue.** Rare-earth
separation is not a mining problem; it is an industrial-chemistry problem requiring acid at scale, and that is the
reason it appears in §6.4 rather than §6.2.

**Consequence for the setting, stated plainly: a Han-core Sinian Federation does not hold the world's rare earths.
Inner Mongolia does.** China's other significant rare-earth source is the ion-adsorption clay of the **Nanling**
in Jiangxi, Guangdong and Fujian — inside the line, in **Gan**, **Yue** and **Min** country — and **Guizhou ranks
second nationally in rare earths** (§2.6). ⚠️ I could not verify the Nanling ion-adsorption districts' current
production share or reserve figures against a primary source this session; the districts' existence and location
are not in dispute, the numbers are not verified here.

### 2.2 Coal by province — split two and two

**China produced 4.71 billion tonnes of coal in 2023, over half of global production**, from roughly 4,700 mines
(down from more than 10,000 in 2015), of which about 1,900 produce a million tonnes a year or more
([Coal in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_China)).

**Reserves are concentrated in Inner Mongolia, Shanxi and Shaanxi. As of 2021, production had concentrated in
Xinjiang, Shaanxi, Inner Mongolia and Shanxi — four divisions accounting for 80% of national output.**

| Division | Inside or outside | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Shanxi** | **INSIDE** (Jin linguistic region) | **Roughly one third of China's total coal reserves** ([Shanxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanxi)) |
| **Shaanxi** | **INSIDE** (Mandarin) | Top-four producer; the Ordos Basin's southern portion |
| **Inner Mongolia** | **OUTSIDE** | Top-four producer; the Dongsheng coalfield and the Ordos City districts ([Inner Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Mongolia)) |
| **Xinjiang** | **OUTSIDE** | **About 40% of the country's coal reserves, roughly 2.2 trillion tonnes** ([Xinjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang)) |
| **Guizhou** | **INSIDE** — but see §2.6 | **49.227 billion tons proven** at end-2002, full range of coal types, excellent quality ([Guizhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guizhou)) |

⚠️ **Shanxi's "roughly one third of reserves" and Xinjiang's "about 40% of reserves, 2.2 trillion tonnes" come
from different reserve concepts and do not sum.** The Xinjiang figure is plainly an estimated in-place resource —
its own source qualifies it as sufficient for a century of national thermal demand "even if only 15 percent of the
estimated coal reserve prove recoverable" — while the Shanxi figure reads as a share of proven recoverable
reserves. **Do not add them. Do not treat either as a proven-reserve percentage without checking.** The
directional conclusion survives the ambiguity: **the core retains a very large coal endowment in Shanxi and
Shaanxi, and an even larger one lies outside it in Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang.**

**Three further coal facts that bear on §6:**

1. **Moving coal from the northern mines to the southeastern coast consumes nearly half of China's rail capacity.**
   The coal and the consumption are 1,500–2,000 km apart, and the link between them is the single largest logistic
   task the Chinese economy performs.
2. **China is simultaneously the world's largest coal importer** — a record **474.42 Mt in 2023**, 490 Mt in the
   first eleven months of 2024. Most thermal coal is domestic; **coking coal for steelmaking is imported**, from
   Mongolia, Australia and the United States.
3. Coal supplied **59% of Chinese electricity generation in 2024** and about **54.6% in 2025**, the first decline
   since 2015. Coal plant capacity factors fell from about 60% in 2011 to 52% in 2024 and 48.2% in 2025.

**Chinese coal is not a modern discovery.** Surface mining in what is now China may go back to around 3490 BC;
Fushan is cited as the earliest coal mine in the ancient world, from around 1000 BC. By the eleventh century a
timber shortage in the north drove rapid expansion, **iron in north China was being smelted in coke-fired blast
furnaces**, and ironworking centers along the Henan–Hebei border consumed at least 140,000 tonnes of coal a year.
Song Yingxing estimated that about 70% of iron was smelted with coal and 30% with charcoal. Shanxi was the center
of the iron industry in late traditional times. Mines in Shanxi reached 120 m depth on natural drainage; Henan and
Manchurian mines reached 90 m or more (same source). **This is the single most encouraging fact in the file for
§6.3: the coal–iron combination in Shanxi was worked at industrial scale with pre-industrial technology, and the
technique is documented.**

**Which of these survive a collapse, and which do not, splits cleanly:**

- **Tungsten is the most important entry in this table for a reconstruction society**, and it is worth more than
  its tonnage suggests. Tungsten carbide and tungsten high-speed steel are what let a machine tool cut hardened
  steel. **A society with tungsten can make cutting tools; a society without it is back to carbon-steel tooling
  and a tenth of the metal-removal rate.** Chinese tungsten is concentrated in the **Nanling** belt across
  Jiangxi (**Gan**), Hunan (**Xiang**) and Guangdong (**Yue**) — **inside the line.** North Korea also holds
  1,700 t/yr of production and 29,000 t of reserves, which is not nothing for a polity of that size.
- **Antimony** is a hardening alloy for lead, and therefore is **lead-acid battery grid metal.** Chinese antimony
  is in Hunan (**Xiang**) and Guizhou — inside the line. This pairs directly with the Latin America file's
  argument that lead-acid is the realistic electrical storage of a reconstruction society.
- **Graphite** is crucible and refractory material, foundry facing, and electrodes — all of which a low-technology
  metals industry uses heavily. Chinese reserves are 81 Mt of a world 290 Mt. **South Korea holds 1,800,000 t of
  reserves and produces 9,600 t/yr; North Korea holds 2,000,000 t and produces 8,100 t/yr** — the single largest
  domestic mineral endowment either Korea has.
- **Fluorspar** is hydrofluoric acid feedstock and a steelmaking flux. China 5,900 kt, **Mongolia 1,200 kt with
  34,000 kt of reserves** (§2.7), and Bayan Obo's 130 Mt of contained fluorite (outside) is the largest single
  fluorite body known.
- **Gallium, indium and bismuth are byproducts of zinc, aluminum and lead refining and have no independent
  existence.** They go with the smelters, i.e. they go. Their numbers are here because the concentration figures
  are extraordinary and the author may want them, not because they matter after a collapse.
- **Magnesium and silicon are electrothermal.** Magnesium smelting in China is overwhelmingly the Pidgeon process,
  which is a thermal reduction of calcined dolomite with ferrosilicon — energy-intensive but not chemically
  exotic. Silicon metal is a carbothermic submerged-arc furnace product: quartz plus carbon plus a great deal of
  electricity. **Silicon metal at a reduced scale is genuinely recoverable and matters for §6.5; semiconductors
  are not and do not.**

### 2.6 ⚠️ THE FOUR OPEN-QUESTION PROVINCES — Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi and Gansu

**This subsection exists because the author has explicitly instructed that the southern and western border be
researched and marked, not decided. It presents both sides and resolves nothing.**

All four were Qing Eighteen Provinces, so a strict reading of the line puts them **inside** the Sinian Federation.
All four hold large non-Han populations, so a Han-majority reading of the line puts them **outside**. The material
stakes are very large in both directions, and they point in the opposite direction from the ethnolinguistic
evidence. That tension is the most useful thing in this file for the author's border decision.

**Side one — the material case for keeping them. These four provinces are not marginal; they carry a
disproportionate share of everything the core is otherwise short of.**

**Side two — the ethnolinguistic case against keeping them, and it is the stronger evidence.** The companion file

finds that **Yunnan, Guizhou and Guangxi are a shatter zone of non-Han peoples, not Han territory with minorities

Nasu, Lolopo, Axi, Nisu, Lahu, Hani, Honi, Biyo, Lu, Bugan, Drung, Tai-Nua, Riang, Parauk, Vo-Wa** — plus **Hlai**

territory, not an enclave. Census corroboration: Guangxi has the largest ethnic-minority population of any Chinese
division, with Zhuang at 31.36% of the region in a 2021 report, and Pinghua, Zhuang, Kam, Cantonese, Hakka and Min

Han with Hui, Dongxiang and Tibetan minorities and Mandarin as the common language — so Gansu is the weakest of
the four cases for exclusion on ethnic grounds and the strongest on material grounds.

**The tension, stated once, plainly, and left unresolved:**

> **The four provinces whose Han-ness is most questionable are the four that carry the Federation's nickel, tin,
> manganese, aluminum and phosphate.** A Federation drawn on strict Qing lines is materially complete and
> ethnically incoherent. A Federation drawn on Han-majority lines is ethnically clean and cannot make stainless
> steel, bronze or fertilizer from its own ground.

**That is a genuine trade, not an oversight, and it is the author's to make.** This file marks it and stops. Note
one partial escape the author may want: the four are separable. Gansu's case for inclusion is far stronger than
Guangxi's on the ethnolinguistic evidence and far stronger than any of them on the material evidence (nickel plus
the corridor). Yunnan and Guizhou can be argued either way. Guangxi is the hardest to keep and the cheapest to
lose, since its tin and manganese overlap with Yunnan's.

### 2.7 Mongolia — a mineral state with 3.5 million people

Mongolia holds **1,564,116 km² and about 3.5 million people — the world's most sparsely populated sovereign state**
([Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolia)). Its merchandise exports of $15.8 billion are **85.2%
five commodities**, and 53.77% is coal alone (§1.4).

- **Oyu Tolgoi**, Khanbogd, South Gobi — a combined open-pit and underground copper–gold porphyry **80 km north of
  the Chinese border**, discovered in 2001, first copper shipped July 2013. A joint venture of Turquoise Hill
  Resources (majority-owned by Rio Tinto) at 66% and the Government of Mongolia at 34%. **Contained metal is
  estimated at 2.7 million tonnes of copper, 1.7 million ounces of gold, 1,900 tonnes of silver and 205,000 tonnes
  of molybdenum.** Designed for **430,000–450,000 tonnes of copper a year — about 3% of global production** — plus
  425,000 ounces of gold, over an anticipated life of more than fifty years. Initial throughput 70,000 t/day
  through one SAG mill, with underground expansion toward 140,000–170,000 t/day. Late Devonian quartz
  monzodiorite intrusions: the Southwest Oyu, South Oyu, Central Oyu, Hugo Dummett and Heruga deposits, in a
  middle-to-late Paleozoic island-arc terrane. Estimated capital cost rose from $4.6 billion in 2010 to about
  $10 billion by 2013. It is expected to account for **more than 30% of Mongolian GDP**
  ([Oyu Tolgoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyu_Tolgoi_mine)).
- **Tavan Tolgoi**, Tsogttsetsii, Ömnögovi — **one of the world's largest untapped coking and thermal coal
  deposits, total estimated resource 6.4 billion tonnes, one quarter of it high-quality coking coal.** Six
  sections: Tsankhi (the largest, split East and West), Ukhaa Khudag, Bor Tolgoi, Borteeg, and the Southwest and
  Eastern coalfields ([Tavan Tolgoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tavan_Tolgoi)).
- **Fluorspar: 1,200 kt in 2024 against a world total of 9,500 kt, with 34,000 kt of reserves** — a top-three
  world producer and a far larger position than Mongolia's size suggests
  ([USGS fluorspar](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-fluorspar.pdf)).
- **Molybdenum 3,100 t** in 2024 ([USGS molybdenum](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-molybdenum.pdf)).

⚠️ **I could not verify the current operating status, ownership terms or 2026 production of either Oyu Tolgoi or
Tavan Tolgoi this session.** Both have long and contested political histories, the Oyu Tolgoi investment agreement
has been renegotiated more than once, and the figures above are design and resource figures rather than confirmed

**The structural point, and it is the mirror image of Taiwan's:** Mongolia has the lowest economic complexity in
the region (−1.125, rank 120) and an endowment of copper, coking coal and fluorspar that does not depend on any of
the things a complexity score measures. **Tavan Tolgoi is the closest large source of metallurgical coal to the
Chinese steel industry, and it is outside every polity in the setting.**

## 3. Agriculture, Soil and Water

### 3.1 The arithmetic that governs everything

**China's arable land is approximately 1.4 million km² — about 10% of its own land area, and about 10% of the
world's arable land — and it supports over 20% of the world's population.** That land is divided among roughly
**200 million households averaging 0.65 hectares each**, and **525,800 km² of it is irrigated** — about 37%
([Agriculture in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_China)). A separate 2006 figure puts
irrigated area at 626,000 km² ([Water resources of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resources_of_China));
⚠️ the two figures use different definitions and different years and should not be reconciled without checking.

About 75% of cultivated area is in food crops. The efforts to expand west and north have had limited success,
because that land is colder and drier, and farm area has been under pressure from urban and industrial expansion
since the 1950s. **China became a net grain importer in the mid-1990s, and its own sources attribute part of that
to unsustainable groundwater mining having removed land from production** (same source).

### 3.2 The wheat/rice divide and where the two crops actually are

The Qinling–Huaihe Line (§0.2) is the divide, and it is an 800 mm rainfall line rather than a cultural preference.

- **Rice** is China's most important crop, grown on about **25% of cultivated area**. **The majority is grown
  south of the Huai River**, plus the Pearl River delta and Yunnan, Guizhou and Sichuan. In linguistic terms that
  is **Wu, Gan, Xiang, Min, Yue, Ping** and the Mandarin southwest — essentially the whole southern half of the
  Federation.
- **Wheat** is the second grain, grown across most of the country but especially on **the North China Plain**, in
  the **Wei and Fen river valleys on the Loess Plateau**, and in Jiangsu, Hubei and Sichuan. That is **Mandarin
  and Jin** country.
- **Corn and millet** are northern and northeastern crops; oats matter in Inner Mongolia and Tibet — both outside.
- China is the **world's largest potato producer**; white potatoes in the north, sweet potatoes in the south.
- Soybeans are grown in north and northeast China. Peanuts in Shandong and Hebei. Cotton on the North China
  Plain, the Yangtze delta and the middle Yangtze — **and in Xinjiang, which produces 84% of Chinese cotton and
  supplies a region that accounts for 26% of global cotton export** ([Xinjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang)).
  Cotton is therefore a largely outside-the-line crop.
- Tea on the hillsides of the middle Yangtze and in Fujian and Zhejiang; sugarcane in Guangdong and Sichuan;
  **sugar beet in Heilongjiang and on irrigated land in Inner Mongolia** — outside.

### 3.3 The North China Plain — INSIDE the line

**Area about 409,500 km²**, most of it under 50 m above sea level. Geologically it is a late Paleogene and Neogene
downfaulted rift basin subsequently filled by Yellow River sediment — **China's largest alluvial plain.** Bounded
by the Yanshan mountains to the north, the Taihang to the west, the Dabie to the south, and the Yellow and Bohai
seas to the east. It covers much of Henan, Hebei and Shandong plus northern Jiangsu and Anhui, and merges
southward into the Yangtze delta
([North China Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_China_Plain)).

It produces **wheat, maize, sorghum, millet, peanuts, sesame, cotton and vegetables, and is China's main area for
sorghum, millet, maize and cotton.** Beijing sits on its northeastern edge, with Tianjin near the northeast coast
and Jinan and Zhengzhou on the Yellow River. The **Shengli Oil Field** is in its Shandong portion.

Two structural facts the author should hold onto:

1. **The plain is flat and uninterrupted, and that flatness is the standard explanation for the linguistic
   uniformity of Mandarin** — communication by horse is rapid across it, unlike the mountainous south with its
   many mutually unintelligible languages. **The plain is the physical reason the Mandarin member of the
   federation is three times the size of all the others combined.**
2. **The weather is unpredictable and the plain floods.** It sits where humid Pacific air meets dry continental
   air, and is prone to both flood and drought; its flatness turns any failure of river works into a large
   inundation. The "hydraulic society" argument — that centralized states arose here to manage granaries, levees
   and canals — is contested, but the underlying physical fact is not.

### 3.4 The Yangtze basin — INSIDE the line, but its source is not

**The Yangtze rises at Jari Hill in the Tanggula Mountains of the Tibetan Plateau and runs 6,236 km to the East
China Sea.** Its drainage basin is **one-fifth of China's land area and home to nearly one-third of the
population.** The delta alone generates as much as **20% of national GDP**
([Yangtze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze)).

**Mean discharge at the mouth is about 956 km³ per year, and annual flow does not fall below roughly 600 km³ even
in the driest years**
([South–North Water Transfer Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South%E2%80%93North_Water_Transfer_Project)).
That last clause is the most important hydrological fact in this file. **The Yangtze is a reliable river.** The
Yellow is not.

### 3.5 The Yellow River — the strategic problem, stated fully

**The Yellow River rises in the Bayan Har Mountains** — Qinghai, on the Tibetan Plateau, **outside the line** —
and runs **5,464 km** with a drainage basin of **795,000 km²**. From Gansu it enters the **1,500 km Ordos Loop,
which runs northeast through the Ordos Plateau and turns east across Inner Mongolia**, then turns sharply south to
form the Shanxi–Shaanxi border, turns east at the Wei River confluence, and crosses the North China Plain to the
Bohai Sea. Its color and its sediment load come from the **Loess Plateau**
([Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River)).

The numbers that matter:

- **The basin holds 120 million people, and over 420 million live in the provinces that depend on it.**
- **The basin comprises 13% of China's cultivated land and receives only 2% of national water runoff.**
- Water and sediment flow have fallen roughly fivefold since the 1970s, and **until recently the river frequently
  failed to reach the sea at all.**
- One estimate counts **1,593 floods in the 2,540 years between 595 BC and 1946 AD**, with repeated course
  changes. It is called both "China's Pride" and "China's Sorrow" for exactly that reason.

**The consequence for the setting is worse than the brief anticipated, and it deserves stating in full.**

> **A Han-core Sinian Federation would not hold the source of the Yellow River, and it would not hold the river's
> middle course either.** The headwaters are in Qinghai, on the Tibetan Plateau. The Ordos Loop — a fifth of the
> river's length — runs through Inner Mongolia. **The river physically leaves the Federation and re-enters it.**
> Every diversion, every barrage and every silt-trap along 1,500 km of the middle course would sit in a foreign
> polity, upstream of 420 million people who depend on the water.

The Yangtze is in a comparable position with one crucial difference: its source is outside, on the Tibetan
Plateau, but its course from Sichuan down is entirely inside the line, and — see §3.4 — **it carries enough water
that upstream interference matters far less.** A dry year on the Yangtze still delivers 600 km³.

**So the author has, in one sentence: the Federation's reliable river is the Yangtze, and the Yangtze is a
southern river. The northern river is unreliable, over-committed, and controlled at both its source and its middle
by other people.** That is a permanent strategic asymmetry between the Mandarin north and the Wu, Gan, Xiang and
Yue south, and it exists in the real world today.

### 3.6 The Tibetan Plateau — OUTSIDE the line, and it is the water tower of Asia

**Area 2,500,000 km², average elevation above 4,500 m, roughly 1,000 km north to south and 2,500 km east to
west — the world's largest and highest plateau.** It covers most of the Tibet Autonomous Region, most of Qinghai,
the western half of Sichuan, southern Gansu and southern Xinjiang, and extends into Bhutan, the Indian regions of
Ladakh and Lahaul and Spiti, Gilgit-Baltistan, northwestern Nepal, eastern Tajikistan and southern Kyrgyzstan
([Tibetan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau)).

**It contains the headwaters of most of the streams in the surrounding regions, including the three longest rivers
in Asia — the Yellow, the Yangtze and the Mekong.** Its tens of thousands of glaciers act as a natural water tower
that stores water and regulates flow. **It is sometimes called the Third Pole, because its ice fields hold the
largest reserve of fresh water outside the polar regions.**

The rivers that rise on it or along its edges, with the ones in scope named first:

| River | Source region | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Yellow** | Bayan Har Mountains, Qinghai | North China Plain, Bohai Sea — **in scope** |
| **Yangtze** | Tanggula Mountains (Jari Hill) | Sichuan, the Three Gorges, Shanghai — **in scope** |
| **Mekong** | Eastern plateau, via northwest Yunnan | Southeast Asia — **out of scope past the border** |
| **Salween** | Eastern plateau, via western Yunnan | Burma — **out of scope past the border** |
| **Brahmaputra** | Southern plateau | India and Bangladesh — **out of scope past the border** |
| **Indus** | Western plateau, near Lake Manasarovar | Pakistan — **out of scope past the border** |

**Stated as the brief asked: a Han-core China would not control the sources of its own two great rivers.** Nor
would it control the headwaters of four rivers on which polities outside this survey's scope depend. The plateau
is the single most consequential piece of ground in East Asia that the Sinian Federation would not hold, and
whichever polity does hold it holds a hydrological position over most of the continent.

⚠️ From the ethnolinguistic file: **ethnic Tibet is much larger than the Tibet Autonomous Region**, extending far
east into Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan and northern Yunnan, with **Amdo** labeled separately within it. If the frontier
polities are drawn on ethnic rather than provincial lines they take territory from provinces the author's
whole-first-order-subdivision rule forbids splitting — a direct collision the author will have to resolve one way
or the other entire.

### 3.7 The Northeast China Plain — Manchuria's black soil, OUTSIDE the line

**This is the best agricultural land in East Asia and it is outside the Federation.**

The Northeast China Plain, also called the Songliao or Manchurian Plain, covers **350,000 km² — China's largest
plain** — at under 200 m elevation, between the Greater and Lesser Khingan ranges and the Changbai mountains,
reaching the sea at Liaodong Bay. The Songhua, Nen and Liao rivers run through it. It comprises three sub-plains:
the **Songnen** in the north, formed by Songhua and Nen alluvium; the **Liaohe** in the south; and the
**Sanjiang** in the northeast, at the confluence of the Songhua, Amur and Ussuri
([Northeast China Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_China_Plain)).

**It is a major chernozem-producing region of the world — deep, thick, fertile black soil, suitable for mechanized
farming, with extensive arable area.** Crops: wheat, corn, soybeans, upland rice, sugar beet, sunflower, sorghum
and flax. Manchurian soils are more precisely described as **mollisols and fluvents** over very deep loess
deposited by wind from the glaciated Himalaya, Kunlun and Tian Shan and from the Gobi and Taklamakan
([Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchuria)). The **Sanjiang Plain was the swampy "Great Northern
Wilderness"** and was reclaimed to farmland after 1949 through state farms; its original wetland area is greatly
reduced. The plain also holds iron ore, coal and oil.

**Two facts the author should hold together.** First, **loess and chernozem are rainfed soils** — the Northeast
Plain does not depend on an aquifer or a canal system the way the North China Plain does. It is the most
collapse-durable farmland in this survey by a wide margin. Second, **Manchuria is Han, not Manchu.** Large-scale
Han migration occurred under the Qing in the process called *Chuang Guandong*, intensifying in the late nineteenth
century under the Guangxu Emperor, and bringing large-scale agriculture with it; the Qing had originally forbidden
Han settlement there in 1668 and built the **Willow Palisade** to mark the boundary. The author's language map
shows Manchu surviving only in a handful of tiny patches — **effectively gone as a living language.** Whatever
polity forms in Manchuria is a settler society, and it is the one clean case of that pattern anywhere in East
Asia. ⚠️ From the ethnolinguistic file: the map also shows **a Korean block in eastern Manchuria (Yanbian)**,
which bears on how a unified Korea's border would be drawn.

### 3.8 Water — the constraint that governs the north

This is the section that determines whether the Han core can feed itself, and the numbers are stark.

- **China holds about 18% of the world's population and about 6% of the world's water resources.** Per-capita
  water use is just over a quarter of the global average, a condition its own literature describes as water
  poverty. Total use was **599.82 km³ in 2022**, about 20% of renewable resources
  ([Water resources of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resources_of_China)).
- **Roughly 80% of those resources are in the south.** Surface water was 2,598.44 km³ in 2022 and groundwater
  792.44 km³, with total available water — accounting for the overlap between them — at 2,708.81 km³. In 2016
  surface water was 82% of total supply and groundwater 18%, **but the north depends on groundwater far more than
  the south because it gets less rain.**
- **Eastern China holds 42% of the population and over half the national GDP on 18% of the water resources.**
- **More than 160 areas nationally are officially over-exploited for groundwater, with average annual depletion
  exceeding 10 billion cubic meters. More than 60,000 km² of ground surface has subsided and more than 50 cities
  suffer serious land subsidence.** Northern China has drawn on **10,000-year-old aquifers**, producing ground
  cracking and subsidence.
- Of 514 rivers surveyed in 2000, 60 were dry; lake water volume had fallen 14%.
- **Storage:** 753 large and 3,896 medium reservoirs reported a combined **418.07 billion m³** of capacity at the
  end of 2022, with lakes holding a further 144.99 billion m³.
- ⚠️ **Large portions of Chinese aquifers suffer natural arsenic contamination**, and as wells are drilled deeper
  they increasingly tap arsenic-rich water. More than 30,000 poisoning cases have been reported and about
  25 million people are exposed. This is a public-health constraint independent of any collapse, and it is the
  direct analogue of the Comarca Lagunera arsenic problem in the Sonora file.
- In 2022 the Yangtze basin suffered a severe drought that dropped the river to its lowest level since 1865 and
  forced Sichuan hydropower plants to cut or stop output. **Even the reliable river is not invulnerable.**

### 3.9 The South–North Water Transfer Project — and why one route survives and one does not

The SNWTP is designed to move **44.8 km³ of fresh water a year** from the Yangtze to the north, at an investment
exceeding ¥500 billion (over US$70 billion). Construction began in 2003; the first phases of the Eastern and
Central routes became operational in late 2014, and **76.7 km³ had been transferred in the first ten years to
2024** ([South–North Water Transfer Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South%E2%80%93North_Water_Transfer_Project)).
It is the largest water transfer scheme in the world. A 2020 assessment found it had improved both water quality
and groundwater levels in the north.

**The three routes are not equivalent, and the difference is the whole point for §6:**

| Route | How it works | Post-collapse category |
|---|---|---|
| **Eastern** (Jiangdu Hydro Project) | Up an upgraded **Grand Canal** from Jiangdu, where a **400 m³/s pumping station** built in the 1980s lifts Yangtze water; further pumping stations raise it along the canal to the Yellow River crossing, through a tunnel, then down an aqueduct to reservoirs near Tianjin. Diversion to rise from 8.9 to 10.6 to 14.8 km³/yr. Water pollution has affected its viability. | **(c) — it is a pump lift and it stops** |
| **Central** ("the Grand Aqueduct") | From **Danjiangkou Reservoir** on the Han River, a Yangtze tributary. The Danjiangkou Dam's crest was raised from 162 m to 176.6 m, lifting the reservoir from 157 m to 170 m so that **the canal flows continuously downhill all the way to Beijing with no pumping stations at all.** Its hardest engineering was two tunnels 70 m beneath the bed of the Yellow River. | **(b) — it is gravity, and gravity is repairable** |
| **Western** | From three Yangtze tributaries near **Bayankala Mountain** — on the Tibetan Plateau, **outside the line** — to Qinghai, Gansu, Shaanxi, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia and Ningxia. Largely unbuilt. | Never existed; and its intake would be foreign |

**The Central Route is the single most valuable repairable water asset in the Han core**, and the author should
know why: raising a dam wall by 14.6 m to make 1,200 km of canal run downhill is a piece of civil engineering, not
a machine. It needs channel maintenance and tunnel integrity, and nothing else.

⚠️ A further note carried from the source: a group of former Chinese officials has advocated rerouting the
**Brahmaputra** northward as a later phase of the SNWTP. That river rises on the Tibetan Plateau — outside the
line — and flows to India and Bangladesh, out of this survey's scope. It is noted only because it shows the
direction planners look when the north runs short.

### 3.10 Fisheries — the northwest Pacific

- **China has one-fifth of the world's population and accounts for about one-third of world reported fish
  production and two-thirds of world reported aquaculture production.** Its 2005 reported wild catch from rivers,
  lakes and sea was **17.1 million tonnes**, against a second-ranked United States at 4.9 Mt. Its 2005 aquaculture
  harvest was **32.4 million tonnes**, more than ten times second-ranked India's 2.8 Mt. Aquaculture accounts for
  about two-thirds of Chinese output, and the producers are concentrated in the coastal regions
  ([Fishing industry in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_industry_in_China)).
- China has imposed a **hot-season moratorium in the Yellow Sea and East China Sea since 1994**, affecting 120,000
  vessels and a million fishermen; trawling and stake-net fishing are banned and set nets closed for at least two
  months. From 2004 all fishing except large-mesh gillnetting was banned in Bohai Bay between 16 June and
  1 September. By the end of 2004, 8,000 vessels had been scrapped and 40,000 fishermen relocated. **The fishery
  is under real pressure, and Chinese authorities have said so.**
- **The physical basis of the fishery is the meeting of two currents.** The **Kuroshio** is the North Pacific's
  warm western boundary current — the Pacific's structural equivalent of the Gulf Stream — carrying equatorial
  water poleward along the west side of the basin, and its nutrient and sediment transport supports a
  biologically rich ecoregion. Off eastern Japan it **merges with the cold Oyashio to form the North Pacific
  Current** ([Kuroshio Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuroshio_Current)).
- **Abutting but out of scope:** the **Sea of Okhotsk**, surrounded on all sides except Hokkaido by Russian
  territory, carries an estimated **17.2 million tonnes of fish and squid biomass in its epipelagic zone and a
  further 15.0 million tonnes in the mesopelagic**, across roughly 300 fish species of which about 40 are
  commercially exploited — dominated by walleye pollock, Pacific herring, capelin and pink salmon. Catches there
  fell by at least half after the 1990s through overfishing, and the sea has warmed by up to 3 °C since
  preindustrial times, three times the global mean, driving fish north. **The salmon catch on the northern
  Japanese coast has fallen 70% in fifteen years while the Russian chum catch has quadrupled**
  ([Sea of Okhotsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Okhotsk)).
- Japan's major Pacific fishing ports include **Hachinohe, Kesennuma and Ishinomaki** on the Sanriku coast, and
  **Choshi, Yaizu, Shimizu and Misaki** near Tokyo, plus the Hokkaido ports
  ([Agriculture, forestry and fishing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture,_forestry,_and_fishing_in_Japan)).

⚠️ **The claim that the northwest Pacific (FAO Major Fishing Area 61) is the world's most productive fishing
region could not be verified against an FAO primary source this session.** It is widely repeated and is consistent
with everything above — the Kuroshio–Oyashio confluence, the Okhotsk biomass figures, and China accounting for a
third of world reported fish production — but **the author should not treat "the world's most productive fishing
region" as a sourced fact until it is checked.** The verified statements are the ones in the bullets above.

### 3.11 Japan, Korea and Taiwan — the food side of the dependency

- **Only 12% of Japan's land is suitable for cultivation**, and agriculture, forestry and fishing together account
  for **1.3% of gross national product**. Farmland occupies terraces and lower slopes, planted with wheat and
  barley in autumn and sweet potatoes, vegetables and dry rice in summer, with intercropping of beans and peas.
  Japanese agriculture has been described in its own literature as a structurally ailing sector, constrained by
  rapidly diminishing arable land and falling farm incomes. **In 1990 Japan was 67% self-sufficient in
  agricultural products and supplied around 30% of its cereal and fodder needs**
  ([Agriculture, forestry and fishing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture,_forestry,_and_fishing_in_Japan)).
  ⚠️ **The commonly cited modern figure for Japan's calorie-basis food self-sufficiency ratio — around 38% — could
  not be verified against a MAFF primary source this session.** The MAFF English statistical pages did not return
  the figure and the web search budget was exhausted. **The author should verify it independently before using a
  number.** The 1990 figures above and the import table in §1.5 are verified and make the same point.
- Japan imports **$4.54B of maize, $2.75B of frozen fish, $2.14B of soybeans, $1.91B of wheat, $0.90B of soybean
  meal, $0.74B of rice and $0.38B of barley** a year. The maize and soybean meal are animal feed; **Japan's meat
  and dairy production is therefore also an import.**
- South Korea imports **$2.88B of maize, $1.61B of wheat, $1.33B of frozen fish, $0.88B of soybeans and $0.72B of
  soybean meal.** Its rice import of $0.25B is small, which reflects a deliberate and long-standing policy of rice
  self-sufficiency. ⚠️ Not independently verified this session; the trade figures are.
- Taiwan imports **$1.52B of soybeans, $1.21B of maize, $0.49B of wheat and $0.09B of rice** on 35,808 km² with
  mountains over two-thirds of it and 23.9 million people.

## 4. Energy

### 4.1 Coal, and where the electricity comes from

See §2.2 for reserves and the provincial split. The demand side:

- **Coal supplied 59% of Chinese electricity generation in 2024** and about 54.6% in 2025, the first annual
  decline since 2015. Capacity factors have fallen from roughly 60% in 2011 to 52% in 2024 and 48.2% in 2025, as
  new coal plants are increasingly built for grid reliability and peak backup rather than baseload
  ([Coal in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_China)).
- China is developing roughly **1,280 Mt/yr of new coal mine capacity**, nearly 80% of it greenfield.
- Coal consumption produces large health costs: air pollution from coal is estimated to cause about **750,000
  premature deaths a year** in China, with indoor rural coal use affecting millions more.

### 4.2 Hydro — the largest such asset anywhere in the author's material-base files

**China's installed hydroelectric capacity was 390.9 GW in 2021 (including 36.4 GW of pumped storage), up from
233 GW in 2011, and 426 GW in 2024. In 2021 hydropower generated 1,300 TWh — roughly 18% of national electricity
generation — making China the world's largest hydroelectric producer by a wide margin**
([Hydroelectricity in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectricity_in_China)).

**Three Gorges Dam** — Sandouping, Yiling District, Yichang, **Hubei** — **inside the line**, in Mandarin
territory just upstream of the Wu region:

- A **hydroelectric gravity dam**. The dam body is **185 m high and 2,309 m wide** and was completed in 2006.
- **22,500 MW installed — the largest power station on Earth by installed capacity**, from 32 main turbines of
  700 MW plus two 50 MW plant-service units.
- **Generates 95 ± 20 TWh a year on average**, varying with basin precipitation. After the 2020 monsoons it
  produced nearly **112 TWh**, breaking Itaipu's 2016 record of 103 TWh.
- **Navigation:** two flights of ship locks, each of five stages, **280 m long by 35 m wide by 5 m deep**, maximum
  vessel 10,000 tonnes, about four hours transit. The locks are 30 m longer than those on the St Lawrence Seaway
  but half as deep. The project was intended to raise Yangtze shipping from ten million to **100 million tonnes a
  year** and cut shipping costs by roughly a third.
- ⚠️ **Sedimentation projections are disputed, and the dam sits on a seismic fault.** About **40 million tonnes of
  sediment** enter the Yangtze annually in the area, with 80% of local land eroding
  ([Three Gorges Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam)).

**Baihetan Dam** — **16,000 MW** from sixteen 1,000 MW turbines, **the largest single hydro turbines in the world**
at 50 m tall and 7,500 tonnes each, producing at 24 kV. Estimated at **62.44 TWh a year**, it is the second-largest
hydroelectric plant on Earth after Three Gorges. Water storage began April 2021
([Baihetan Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baihetan_Dam)).

Add **Xiluodu, Wudongde** and the rest of the upper Yangtze cascade, and the Yangtze system carries the largest
concentration of installed hydroelectric capacity in the world. ⚠️ I did not verify Xiluodu's or Wudongde's
individual capacities this session.

**Yunnan's position is worth restating here** (§2.6): the province produces 200 km³ of water a year in-province —
three times the Yellow River's entire flow — with another 160 km³ arriving from outside, and it is the site of the
Chinese Mekong cascade beginning with Manwan in 1993. **If Yunnan is outside the Federation, a very large block of
hydroelectric potential goes with it.**

### 4.4 Oil and gas — almost all of it outside the line

- **Daqing Oil Field**, Songliao Basin, **Heilongjiang — OUTSIDE the line, in Manchuria.** China's largest oil
  discovery and the field whose output changed the country's development path. It originally contained
  **16 billion barrels (2.2 billion tonnes)** and **has produced over 10 billion barrels since production began in
  1960**; remaining recoverable reserves are about **3.6 billion barrels (500 million tonnes)**. Output peaked at
  **40 Mt/yr in 2008**, fell to 32 Mt in 2018, 30.9 Mt in 2019 and 30 Mt in 2021. A shale discovery there in 2021
  is estimated at 1.268 billion tonnes of geological reserve
  ([Daqing Oil Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daqing_Oil_Field)).
- **Changqing**, in the **Ordos Basin** spanning Shaanxi, Gansu and Inner Mongolia, is now **China's most
  productive oil and gas field at over 42 million tonnes** — and it straddles the line, with the Shaanxi and Gansu
  portions inside and the Inner Mongolian portion outside (same source).
- **Xinjiang — OUTSIDE — is China's largest natural-gas-producing region and holds the highest concentration of
  fossil fuel reserves of any region in the country, over a fifth of national hydrocarbon resources.** The
  **Tarim Basin is the largest oil-and-gas-bearing area in China, with about 16 billion tonnes of oil and gas
  discovered**; a 2021 CNPC find of about 907 million tonnes was described as the largest in recent decades. As of
  2021 the basin produced hydrocarbons at 2 million tonnes a year, up from 1.52 Mt in 2020. Oil and petrochemicals
  account for up to **60% of Xinjiang's economy**, centered on **Karamay** and Aksu, with the **West–East Gas
  Pipeline** running to Shanghai ([Xinjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang)).
- **Shengli**, in Shandong on the North China Plain, is **inside the line** and is the major in-core field
  ([North China Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_China_Plain)). ⚠️ I could not retrieve verified
  production or reserve figures for Shengli this session.

**Consequence: the Han core's petroleum position is poor even by pre-collapse standards, and the two largest
Chinese hydrocarbon provinces — the Tarim and the Songliao — are both outside it.**

### 4.5 Japan, Korea and Taiwan — near-total import dependence, quantified

- **Japan is the fifth-largest primary energy consumer in the world. Fossil fuels were 67% of primary energy in
  2023. It imports 97% of its oil and is the world's largest LNG importer.** Renewables were 7.8% of primary
  energy supply in 2019; solar reached 11.4% of electricity by 2024 against a 15% target for 2030; wind was 0.85%
  of production in 2022. The government's target is to raise the national energy self-sufficiency rate to about
  **30–40% by fiscal 2040** — a target that states the current position better than any single number
  ([Energy in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Japan)).
- **Taiwan relies on imports for almost 98% of its energy.** In 2024, **83.2% of its 288 TWh of generation came
  from fossil fuels — 42.4% natural gas and 39.3% coal** — with 4.2% nuclear, 11.6% renewables and 1.1% pumped
  storage. Domestic LNG output is **350–400 million m³ a year**, against imports that make up the rest
  ([Energy in Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Taiwan)).
- **South Korea's position is the same in kind:** crude petroleum is 13.65% of all merchandise imports, petroleum
  gas 6.05%, refined petroleum 3.63% and coal 3.04% — **about 23.3% of everything the country buys** (§1.5).

**Stated once: these three economies burn almost nothing they produce.** That is the single fact that most
distinguishes East Asia from every other region in the author's material-base files, and §6.4 is largely about it.

Further detail worth having:

- **Ningbo-Zhoushan is also the busiest port in the world by cargo tonnage — 888.96 million tonnes in 2015.** It
  sits at the crossing of the north–south coastal route and the inland waterway system, with canals reaching the
  Yangtze, and comprises the Beilun seaport, the Zhenhai estuary port and the old inland river harbor. Ningbo has
  been a port since 738 and was one of the Tang dynasty's three great foreign-trade seaports
  ([Port of Ningbo-Zhoushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Ningbo)).
- **Shanghai** overtook Singapore as the world's busiest container port in 2010 at 29.05 million TEU and reached
  43.3 million TEU in 2019. It comprises a deep-sea port and a river port at the confluence of the Yangtze,
  Huangpu and Qiantang ([Port of Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Shanghai)).
- **Busan** handled over three-quarters of Korean container shipping in 2022 and **24.4 million TEU in 2024, its
  busiest year on record.** It sits at the mouth of the Nakdong River facing Tsushima, and is split into Busan
  North Port and the highly automated Busan New Port. 50% of Busan's manufacturing jobs are export-related
  ([Port of Busan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Busan)).
- **Kaohsiung** is Taiwan's largest harbor, handling about 10.26 million TEU in 2015, operated by the state-owned
  Taiwan International Ports Corporation. It was a natural lagoon developed over several centuries — by the Dutch
  East India Company from the 1620s, then through the Koxinga and early Qing eras, and opened to foreign trade
  under the 1858 Treaties of Tianjin ([Port of Kaohsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Kaohsiung)).

**The distribution across the federation matters.** The two largest ports on Earth are both in **Wu**. Three of the
top ten are in **Yue**. **Min** holds Xiamen and, across the strait, Kaohsiung. Mandarin holds Qingdao and
Tianjin — very large ports, but not the largest. **Dalian and Yingkou are outside the line entirely.** A Sinian
Federation's maritime capacity would be concentrated in two of its smaller linguistic members.

### 5.2 Steel — the largest industry of its kind ever built, on imported ore

**China produced over 1 billion tonnes of crude steel in 2022 — 52.9% of world production — up from 123 million
tonnes in 1999 and 100 million tonnes in 1996**
([Steel industry in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_industry_in_China)). The top five groups by 2015
volume were Baosteel–Wuhan, Hesteel, Shagang, Ansteel and Shougang; as of at least 2024 the industry remains
highly fragmented across a large number of companies.

**Two mills carry the argument, and they sit on opposite sides of the line:**

- **Baosteel, Baoshan District, Shanghai — INSIDE, in Wu.** Planned from 1978 under Deng Xiaoping as a large
  integrated works **located near the port of Shanghai.** That siting decision is the whole point: Baosteel was
  built at tidewater because its ore arrives by ship. It is a coastal plant fed by the sea, not a plant fed by a
  Chinese ore body. Its Shanghai Stock Exchange listing in 2000 was the largest Chinese IPO to that date
  ([Baowu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baowu)).
- **Ansteel, Anshan, Liaoning — OUTSIDE, in Manchuria.** The Anshan Iron and Steel Group is state-owned and among
  the largest producers in the world by volume. **Under the First Five-Year Plan (1953–57), Anshan was the only
  large Soviet-model steel mill in China operating close to its design capacity by 1956**
  ([Ansteel Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansteel_Group)). It is there because the ore, the coal and the
  limestone are there.

**That contrast is the most useful single pair of facts in this section.** The Federation's flagship mill is a
coastal import processor. The mill built on co-located ore and coal is in another country.

**For §6.3, note the historical precedent already established in §2.2:** north Chinese iron was smelted in
coke-fired blast furnaces by the eleventh century, Shanxi was the center of the iron industry in late imperial
times, and roughly 70% of iron was coal-smelted by Song Yingxing's estimate. **Ironmaking on Shanxi coal is not a
speculation; it is a recovered technique.**

### 5.3 Shipbuilding

**China, South Korea and Japan dominate world shipbuilding by completed gross tonnage.** The major yards are
**China State Shipbuilding Corporation** and **China Shipbuilding Industry Corporation**; **HD Hyundai Heavy
Industries, Samsung Heavy Industries and Hanwha Ocean** in Korea; and **Imabari Shipbuilding** in Japan. Between
them they supply most of the global market for large container ships, bulk carriers, tankers and ro-ro vessels
([Shipbuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipbuilding)).

**As of 2023, China's shipbuilding output, newly received orders and orders on hand accounted for 50%, 67% and
55% of the global market respectively**, with double-digit growth in all three against the previous year.

⚠️ **A September 2023 report by the Alliance for American Manufacturing, based on a leaked US Navy briefing slide
described as verified by the Office of Naval Intelligence, put Chinese shipbuilding capacity at 232 times that of
the United States.** That multiple is widely repeated and it rests on a single leaked slide. **Treat it as
contested, not as a measurement.** The 50/67/55 market-share figures above are the defensible statement.

Historical note for §6.3: Japan used shipbuilding in the 1950s and 1960s to rebuild its industrial structure;
South Korea made it a strategic industry in the 1970s; China is repeating the pattern with state-supported
investment. **Shipbuilding is the industry these three economies have each used to rebuild from a low base, and
each of them did it within a generation.** That is directly relevant to a reconstruction scenario.

### 5.4 Semiconductors — the most advanced fabrication on Earth, on two small territories

- **TSMC**, headquartered in **Hsinchu Science Park, Taiwan.** It holds a commanding majority of the semiconductor
  foundry market, **approximately 70% of global market share**, and was the world's largest non-US company by
  market capitalization as of 30 June 2026. It is the principal supplier to Nvidia, Apple, Broadcom and Qualcomm.
  **Global capacity of about thirteen million 300 mm-equivalent wafers per year as of 2020**, producing across
  process nodes from 2 microns down to **3 nanometres**. It was the first foundry to market at 7 nm and at 5 nm,
  and **the first to commercialize ASML's extreme ultraviolet lithography in high volume.** Its own literature
  notes that its role as a supply-chain bottleneck raises concerns that a disruption would cause a worldwide chip
  shortage ([TSMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSMC)). Taiwan's integrated-circuit exports were **$226.1 billion
  in 2023 — 43.41% of everything Taiwan sold** (§1.4).
- **Samsung Electronics**, headquartered in Yeongtong District, Suwon, with its flagship semiconductor complex at
  **Pyeongtaek**. It is **the world's largest manufacturer of semiconductor memory, both DRAM and NAND flash**
  ([Samsung Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung_Electronics)).
- **SK Hynix**, with production facilities at **Icheon and Cheongju** in South Korea and at Wuxi, Chongqing and
  Dalian in China. Along with Samsung and Micron it is one of the **"Big Three" memory manufacturers**
  ([SK Hynix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SK_Hynix)).

**Stated as the brief asked: the most advanced semiconductor fabrication on Earth sits on two small territories.**
Taiwan is 35,808 km² with 23.9 million people. South Korea is comparable in area. **Between them they hold roughly
70% of world foundry capacity and two of the world's three memory manufacturers.** There is nothing else like this
concentration in any industry in any of the author's material-base files, and §6.5 is devoted to what becomes of
it.

### 5.5 Automobiles, machine tools and the rest

- **Cars are 15.40% of Japanese exports ($121.4B) and 9.72% of Korean exports ($67.5B)**, with vehicle parts a
  further 3.71% and 2.68%. Chinese cars are $77.5B and vehicle parts $48.1B, on a far larger export base
  (§1.4).
- **"Machinery having individual functions"** — the OEC category that captures much specialized industrial and
  machine-tool equipment — is **$34.9B of Japanese exports (4.43%)** and $12.6B of Korean. It is also **$18.9B of
  Taiwanese imports (5.80%)** and $19.6B of Korean imports, which says where the capital equipment comes from and
  where it goes. Japan also exports **$15.1B of large construction vehicles and $10.8B of industrial printers.**
- **Japan exports $10.9B of hot-rolled iron and Korea $6.5B** — semi-finished steel, made from imported ore.
- **China exports $80.2B of electric batteries**, the single largest such trade in the world.
- **Liuzhou, Guangxi** is a motor-vehicle manufacturing center with a large steel works and a General Motors joint
  venture ([Guangxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangxi)) — one of the material assets at stake in §2.6.
- **The Grand Canal** connects the Yangtze system to the north and now carries the SNWTP Eastern Route (§3.9). As
  a transport artifact it is the oldest and longest artificial waterway in the world and is a masonry-and-
  earthwork structure, which places it squarely in §6.3. ⚠️ I did not retrieve verified dimensions or current
  navigable extent this session.

## 6. POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

*Everything above is sourced fact. This section is reasoning from those facts, and is the author's to accept,
modify or discard. Assumption, matching the Sonora and Latin America files: a civilization-ending nuclear exchange
in 2083, followed by a reconstruction era 150–250 years later, with no functioning global trade and no external
supply chains.*

**Structure note, for cross-file comparison.** This section follows the same six-part spine as the nine North
American material-base files and the Latin America survey, so the regions can be read against each other
directly: **headline (6.1) → (a) survives regardless (6.2) → (b) repairable (6.3) → (c) vanishes (6.4) →
carrying capacity (6.7) → summary judgment (6.8)**. Two extra subsections sit inside that spine because the brief
asked for them specifically and because they are too large to fold into the lists: **6.5, the semiconductor
question**, and **6.6, whether the Han core can feed itself**. Both are elaborations of category (c) and category
(a) respectively.

### 6.1 The headline

**East Asia is simultaneously the world's workshop and the world's most import-dependent region. A collapse
deletes the first condition and leaves the second standing as a permanent fact about geography.**

And for the Sinian Federation specifically, one sentence:

> **The line the author has drawn puts the people on one side and a very large share of the inputs on the other.**

Four things follow, and they are the spine of everything below:

1. **The ECI ranking inverts harder here than anywhere else in the author's files.** Taiwan and Japan hold the
   first and second highest economic complexity scores on Earth and among the thinnest material bases on Earth.
   Mongolia holds the lowest score in the region and copper, coking coal and fluorspar that do not care what
   anyone's score is. Over 150–250 years, know-how decays and ore bodies do not.
2. **The Han core keeps the farmland and loses the minerals.** Roughly **1.257 billion people inside the line,
   about 88% of mainland China's population**, against **156 million outside** — and outside sit Bayan Obo, about
   40% of the national coal reserve, the sources of both great rivers, the Manchurian black soil, Daqing, and the
   only Chinese integrated steel mill built on co-located ore and coal.
3. **Japan, Korea and Taiwan face a harsher failure mode than any other territory in this project.** They do not
   merely lose export markets; they lose the ore, the fuel and a large fraction of the food that their industries
   and their populations run on. Japan mines no copper and holds no copper reserves while refining 1.6 million
   tonnes a year. Taiwan imports 98% of its energy.
4. **The south of the Federation can feed itself and the north, at current density, cannot.** The
   Qinling–Huaihe line stops being a culinary boundary and becomes a demographic sorting line.

### 6.2 (a) Things that survive regardless — geology, soil, climate, water

These cannot be destroyed by war or neglect. They are simply there in 2283.

**Inside the line:**

- **The North China Plain.** 409,500 km² of alluvium, mostly under 50 m elevation, the largest alluvial plain in
  China. The soil does not go anywhere. Whether it can be *farmed* at current intensity is §6.6, and the answer
  is different from "does the soil exist."
- **The Yangtze basin.** One-fifth of China's land area, and **956 km³ of water a year that never falls below
  about 600 km³ even in the driest years.** This is the most valuable single natural asset in the Federation and
  it is essentially unfailable. Gravity, monsoon and a Himalayan snowpack deliver it.
- **The rice terraces and paddy systems of the south.** Earthworks and bunds, built by hand over centuries, fed by
  rainfall above 800 mm. **South of the Huai, cereal agriculture needs no lift and no aquifer.**
- **Shanxi's coal measures**, in the **Jin** linguistic region — roughly a third of China's coal reserves, in
  seams that were worked to 120 m depth on natural drainage before any pump existed (§2.2).
- **The Nanling tungsten and rare-earth belt** across Jiangxi (**Gan**), Hunan (**Xiang**) and Guangdong
  (**Yue**), and the Hunan and Guizhou antimony districts. Tungsten is the single most valuable mineral the core
  retains, for the reason given in §2.4: it is what lets a machine tool cut hardened steel.
- **Chinese graphite reserves of 81 million tonnes**, a substantial share of them inside the line. Crucibles,
  refractories, foundry facings and electrodes.
- **The Loess Plateau.** Deep, soft, wind-deposited soil that can be worked with hand tools and cut into
  cave-dwellings; it is also the source of the Yellow River's sediment, which is why the river is both fertile and
  dangerous.
- **The coastal fishery of the Yellow Sea, the East China Sea and the Bohai**, driven by the Kuroshio and, in the
  north, by the confluence with the Oyashio. Currents are driven by wind and the Earth's rotation and will keep
  arriving regardless of who is alive to net the fish. ⚠️ Note the heavy pre-collapse exploitation documented in
  §3.10 — the moratoria exist because the stocks were in trouble. A century of no industrial fishing is more
  likely to restore this fishery than to have ended it.

**Outside the line, and this is the part the author should feel the weight of:**

- **Bayan Obo.** More than 40 Mt of rare-earth minerals at 3–5.4% REE, 470 Mt of iron, 1 Mt of Nb₂O₅, 130 Mt of
  fluorite. The rock does not care. Whether anyone can *use* the rare earths is §6.4; the iron and the fluorite
  are usable by a low-technology society and the niobium would be, if it could be separated.
- **The Manchurian chernozem.** 350,000 km² of deep black soil, **rainfed**, the best farmland in East Asia and
  among the best in the world. It needs no irrigation, no aquifer and no canal.
- **The Tibetan Plateau's glaciers and headwaters** — the largest freshwater reserve outside the poles.
- **Xinjiang's coal and the Tarim's hydrocarbons** — physically present, mostly unreachable without machinery
  (§6.4), and in any case in someone else's country.
- **Mongolia's Oyu Tolgoi and Tavan Tolgoi.** Copper porphyry and 6.4 billion tonnes of coal of which a quarter is
  coking grade. **Tavan Tolgoi is the nearest large metallurgical-coal resource to the Chinese steel districts,
  and it is shallow, in the Gobi, in a country of 3.5 million people.**

**Japan, Korea and Taiwan:**

- **The Kuroshio–Oyashio fishery** off eastern Japan, and the Sanriku and Hokkaido fishing grounds.
- **Korean graphite** — 1.8 Mt of reserves in the south, 2.0 Mt in the north, and **North Korean tungsten at
  29,000 t of reserves.** On the geological evidence in §2.8, **the northern half of the peninsula is better
  endowed than the southern half**, which is worth the author's attention.
- **Japan's forests, its volcanic geothermal gradient and its rainfall.** Japan is a wet, mountainous, heavily
  forested country and that does not change.
- ⚠️ **Japan's closed coal and copper workings.** Japan supplied over 80% of its own energy from domestic coal in
  the 1950s, and the Ashio, Besshi and Hitachi copper districts were historically significant. Those workings
  physically exist. **I could not verify remaining reserves or the condition of any of them this session, and the
  author should not assign a number to this.** But the existence of pre-industrial and early-industrial Japanese
  mining is not in dispute, and it is the most plausible route by which a post-collapse Japan gets any metal at
  all.

### 6.3 (b) Things that depend on infrastructure but could plausibly be repaired

The common thread, as in the Sonora and Latin America files: **gravity, masonry and earthworks** — things whose
expensive part is civil engineering rather than machinery.

- **The South–North Water Transfer Central Route. This is the most valuable repairable asset in the Han core and
  the author should know exactly why.** The Danjiangkou Dam's crest was raised 14.6 m so that water runs
  **downhill from the reservoir to Beijing with no pumping stations at all** (§3.9). That is a dam wall and a
  lined channel. It needs silt clearance, bank maintenance and the integrity of two tunnels under the Yellow
  River. It does not need a power supply. **A reconstruction society in the north that can keep that channel open
  has moved the water problem from insoluble to merely difficult.**
- **The Yangtze dams as gravity structures.** Three Gorges is a **concrete gravity dam, 185 m by 2,309 m**, with
  32 turbines of 700 MW. Baihetan has sixteen of 1,000 MW. The turbines and generators need bearings, rewinding
  and machining; **the dam, the penstocks and the head do not go anywhere.** Even a small fraction of nameplate is
  transformative: **two working units at Three Gorges is 1.4 GW**, which is more electricity than any plausible
  reconstruction economy in the basin knows what to do with. ⚠️ The same siltation caveat as the Latin America
  file applies and applies harder here — the Yangtze carries about 40 Mt of sediment a year past the dam site,
  and a society without sluicing capability watches its reservoir fill over generations. ⚠️ Three Gorges also
  sits on a seismic fault.
- **Small and micro hydro in the southwest.** Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan and the upper Yangtze tributaries are
  among the best small-hydro terrain in the world — steep, wet, and with head everywhere. A micro-hydro
  installation is a penstock, a turbine and a generator, all within reach of a competent machine shop. **Yunnan
  alone produces 200 km³ of water a year in-province.**
- **The Grand Canal and the Yangtze river navigation.** Channel maintenance and lock repair, nothing more. The
  Three Gorges locks are five-stage flights, 280 × 35 × 5 m, for vessels to 10,000 tonnes — **and note that even
  if the locks fail, the Yangtze below Yichang is navigable, which is the stretch that carries the Wu industrial
  region.** River and canal transport is the correct technology for a low-energy society moving grain, coal and
  ore, and China has the largest such network on Earth already graded and embanked.
- **Rail alignments.** China's rail network was built in large part to move coal from Shanxi and Inner Mongolia to
  the coast — a task consuming nearly half of national rail capacity. **Relaying track on an existing graded,
  bridged, tunneled alignment is vastly easier than surveying a new one**, and the alignment from the Shanxi
  coalfields to the North China Plain is the single most valuable one in the country.
- **Charcoal and coke ironmaking on Shanxi coal.** This is the most important item in the list and it is not
  speculation. By the eleventh century **north Chinese iron was being smelted in coke-fired blast furnaces**,
  ironworking centers on the Henan–Hebei border burned at least 140,000 tonnes of coal a year, and roughly 70% of
  iron was coal-smelted by Song Yingxing's estimate, with Shanxi as the center of the industry in late imperial
  times (§2.2). **A reconstruction society in Shanxi and Henan is re-walking a road that is documented, not
  inventing one.** The constraint is the ore, not the fuel or the furnace — see §6.4.
- **Copper and lead smelting sites.** China refines 12 Mt of copper a year and Japan 1.6 Mt. The *plants* are
  gone as modern plants, but the sites, the slag heaps, the flue systems and the accumulated metallurgical
  knowledge are located where they are for permanent reasons. The Latin America file's argument applies: a
  smelting *site* is a durable thing even when the smelter is not.
- **Tailings, slag and scrap.** East Asia holds the largest concentration of processed metal on the planet.
  Chinese steel output alone has exceeded a billion tonnes a year; the standing stock of steel in Chinese, Korean
  and Japanese cities is measured in tens of billions of tonnes. **Rock already drilled, blasted, hauled and
  crushed, and metal already reduced, are the energy-intensive steps, and they are already paid for.** For the
  first century of any reconstruction, East Asian metal comes from demolition, not from mines.
- **Shipbuilding.** Japan rebuilt its industrial structure on shipbuilding in the 1950s and 1960s; Korea did the
  same from the 1970s; China from the 2000s. **All three did it within a generation, from a low base, in the
  twentieth century.** The dry docks, slipways and sheltered water remain. Wooden and simple steel hulls are
  within reach of a society that can make plate and rivets.
- **Lead-acid batteries.** Chinese antimony (60% of world production, in Hunan and Guizhou), Chinese lead, and
  1859 technology. As in the Sonora and Latin America files, this is the realistic electrical storage of a
  reconstruction society, and East Asia is unusually well placed for it because the antimony is domestic.
- **Silicon metal at reduced scale.** A carbothermic submerged-arc furnace, quartz and carbon. China makes 85% of
  the world's silicon metal today. This is emphatically **not** semiconductors (§6.5), but silicon for alloying,
  for ferrosilicon, and for the Pidgeon-process magnesium reduction is genuinely within reach of a society with
  hydroelectricity.
- **Terraces, paddy bunds and the irrigation works of the south.** Masonry and earthwork, built without machines.
  Many are still in continuous use.

### 6.4 (c) Things that are pure artifacts of global trade and industrial energy — these vanish

- **Imported iron ore, and with it most of East Asian steelmaking. This is the largest single entry in the
  category anywhere in the author's files.** China imports **$122.4 billion of iron ore a year** to feed an
  industry making **over half the world's steel**, because its own reserves average **34.5% Fe** against a world
  average of 44% and need crushing, grinding, magnetic separation and pelletizing before they can be charged.
  Japan imports $9.7B, Korea $8.0B, Taiwan $2.28B, and none of the three has any domestic ore at all. **Baosteel
  was deliberately sited at tidewater in 1978 for exactly this reason, and a tidewater plant with no ships is a
  building.** What survives is Shanxi coal plus whatever iron can be won from low-grade domestic deposits and,
  overwhelmingly, from scrap.
- **Imported metallurgical coal.** China is the world's largest coal producer and still imports coking coal. The
  nearest large resource is Tavan Tolgoi in Mongolia.
- **Every semiconductor fabrication plant.** Treated separately in §6.5.
- **Japan's and Korea's copper refineries.** Japan: zero mines, zero reserves, 1,600 kt of refining. Korea: zero,
  zero, 620 kt. **These are not industries that shrink; they are industries with no input.** The same is true of
  Japanese, Korean and Taiwanese oil refining — Korea exports $53.3B of refined petroleum made from $85.7B of
  imported crude.
- **The entire fossil-fuel supply of Japan, Korea and Taiwan.** Japan imports **97% of its oil** and is the
  world's largest LNG importer; Taiwan imports **almost 98% of its energy** and generates 83.2% of its electricity
  from fossil fuels. There is no domestic substitute at any scale. This is the single hardest constraint in the
  file.
- **The container ports, as container ports.** Shanghai's 49 million TEU, Ningbo-Zhoushan's 35 million,
  Shenzhen's 27 million, Busan's 24 million. Gantry cranes, container handling, and above all the *ships* and
  the *trade* they serve. The **harbors** survive and are excellent harbors; the throughput does not. Note that
  Ningbo has been a working port since 738 and Kaohsiung since the Dutch period — **these are good places to put a
  port for reasons that predate containers by a millennium.**
- **Rare-earth separation.** Bayan Obo's ore survives; the ability to use it does not. Separating the lanthanides
  from one another requires hundreds of stages of solvent extraction, hydrofluoric and sulfuric acid at scale, and
  the disposal of roughly a tonne of radioactive residue and 75 m³ of acidic wastewater per tonne of product
  (§2.1). **This is industrial chemistry of a high order and it is the first thing to go.**
- **Gallium, indium and bismuth.** Byproducts of zinc, aluminum and lead refining, with no independent existence.
  China makes 99% of the world's low-purity gallium; that number means nothing after the smelters stop.
- **The SNWTP Eastern Route.** A 400 m³/s pump lift at Jiangdu and further pumping stations up the Grand Canal.
  Pumps at that scale are a power station with extra steps. **Contrast the Central Route in §6.3 — the same
  project, two routes, and only one of them is gravity.**
- **North China Plain aquifer irrigation.** Deep wells into 10,000-year-old water, at a national depletion rate
  exceeding 10 billion m³ a year, with 60,000 km² of ground already subsided. **Every one of those wells is a
  pump.** This is the hinge of §6.6.
- **Nuclear power in Japan, Korea and China.** Korea's 30% of electricity, Japan's pre-2011 30%, and China's
  63.4 GWe. Fuel fabrication, enrichment and the entire cycle are gone. ⚠️ Whatever the author decides happened
  to these plants in 2083 is a separate question; this file only states that the fuel supply ends.
- **Mongolia's export economy.** 85.2% of exports are five commodities and 53.77% is coal sold to a single
  neighbor. The **coal and the copper survive as rock** (§6.2); the buyer does not.
- **Xinjiang's cotton and the Tarim's oil.** 84% of Chinese cotton comes off irrigated desert oases; Tarim
  hydrocarbons are deep-basin production feeding a 4,000 km pipeline to Shanghai.
- **Chinese soybean imports — $56.6 billion a year.** These are animal feed. **The Chinese meat and dairy sector
  at its current scale is an import**, in exactly the way the Latin America file describes the Cerrado as an
  industrial artifact. The feed and the livestock are two ends of the same vanished chain.

### 6.5 The semiconductor question — what happens to the most concentrated industry on Earth

**The short answer is that it stops, completely, and it does not degrade gracefully into a simpler version of
itself. This is worth stating carefully because it is counterintuitive.**

Most industries have a low-technology ancestor to fall back to. Steel has the bloomery. Textiles have the
handloom. Electricity has the water wheel. **Semiconductor fabrication does not.** There is no cottage version of
a 3 nm process, and there is no partial version either — a fab that cannot maintain its cleanroom, its ultrapure
water, its ultrapure gases and its photolithography does not make larger, cruder chips; it makes none.

The specific dependencies, from the facts above:

1. **Extreme ultraviolet lithography.** TSMC was the first to commercialize EUV in high volume (§5.4). The
   machines come from one supplier on another continent, out of this survey's scope. There is no second source
   anywhere, and there never was.
2. **Scale of concentration.** TSMC holds roughly **70% of global foundry market share** and about **thirteen
   million 300 mm-equivalent wafers a year.** Samsung is the world's largest memory manufacturer and SK Hynix one
   of the three. **Two territories, both small, both importing nearly all of their energy.**
3. **Input dependency runs both ways.** Taiwan *imports* **$59.3 billion of integrated circuits a year — 18.19% of
   all its imports** — alongside exporting $226.1 billion of them. Korea imports $50.7B and exports $128.7B. China
   imports $181.7B. **These are not self-contained industries; they are stages in a loop that crosses the Pacific
   several times per chip.**
4. **The power.** Taiwan generates 288 TWh a year, 83.2% of it from imported fossil fuel, and a modern fab is one
   of the most electricity-intensive facilities ever built.

**What does survive, and the distinction is important enough to state plainly:**

> **Semiconductors go. Silicon does not.** China produces **85% of the world's silicon metal and 69% of its
> ferrosilicon** from carbothermic submerged-arc furnaces — quartz, carbon and a great deal of electricity. That
> is a recoverable process for a society with hydropower (§6.3). Silicon metal gives alloying silicon, ferro-
> silicon for steel deoxidation and for the Pidgeon magnesium process, and silicon carbide abrasives. **What it
> does not give is a transistor.**

**The consequence for the author's setting, stated as a scene rather than a statistic:** in 2283, Hsinchu and
Pyeongtaek are the most valuable ruins on Earth and the most useless. They contain more precision engineering per
square meter than any other structures ever built, and nothing in them can be restarted, cannibalized into
something simpler, or explained to anyone who was not trained in the system that built them. **Taiwan in
particular went from twentieth in the world to first in economic complexity in twenty-eight years, on one product
line, and that product line has no afterlife.**

Electronics in the reconstruction era are therefore **salvage and vacuum tubes**: recovered components from the
enormous installed base, and thermionic valves, which are glass, tungsten wire and a vacuum pump — and note that
**China holds 83% of world tungsten production, mostly inside the line** (§2.4).

### 6.6 Can the Han core feed itself?

**The brief asked for a specific answer. It is: the south comfortably can, the north at current density cannot,
and the Qinling–Huaihe line becomes the sorting mechanism.**

The argument, from the facts:

**What the core has.** Essentially all of China's 1.4 million km² of arable land lies inside the line — the outer
territories are plateau, desert, steppe and the one great exception of the Manchurian plain. The North China Plain
is 409,500 km² of alluvium; the Yangtze basin is a fifth of the country; the southern rice lands take about a
quarter of all cultivated area. **Rice south of the Huai is rainfed at over 800 mm a year and needs no lift.** The
Yangtze delivers at least 600 km³ in its worst year.

**What the core does not have.**

1. **Northern irrigation without pumps.** **525,800 km² of Chinese cropland is irrigated** and a large share of
   the northern portion runs on groundwater from aquifers being mined at over 10 billion m³ a year, through wells
   that are pumps. The Yellow River basin holds **13% of China's cultivated land on 2% of its water runoff**, and
   the river frequently failed to reach the sea even *with* the full apparatus of the modern state behind it.
2. **The Yellow River's source and middle course**, which are in other polities (§3.5).
3. **The SNWTP Eastern Route** (pumped, gone). It keeps the Central Route (gravity, §6.3), which is a genuine and
   large partial answer — but 44.8 km³/yr was the design figure for **all three routes**, and only one survives.
4. **The nitrogen leg of the fertilizer tripod.** Haber-Bosch ammonia needs natural gas, high pressure and
   catalysts. It goes. Nitrogen reverts to legumes, manure, night soil and green manure — **and here East Asia is
   better placed than almost anywhere, because intensive organic nitrogen cycling in paddy and garden agriculture
   is the region's own traditional practice, not a novelty.**
5. **Phosphate, if Guizhou goes.** Guizhou holds over 40% of national phosphate reserves (§2.6). Phosphorus must
   be mined; it cannot be fixed.
6. **Imported soybeans** — $56.6B a year of animal feed (§6.4). **The protein in the current Chinese diet is
   substantially imported, and that ends.**

**The provisional judgment, offered as reasoning and not as fact:**

- **South of the Qinling–Huaihe line — the Wu, Gan, Xiang, Min, Yue and Ping regions plus the Mandarin
  southwest — the Federation feeds itself comfortably.** Rainfed rice, double cropping in the far south, terraces
  that are already built, rivers that do not fail, fish and aquaculture ponds, and a traditional agronomy that
  never depended on synthetic nitrogen. This is among the most food-secure ground in the post-collapse world the
  author is building.
- **North of it — the Mandarin and Jin heartland, the North China Plain, Shandong, Hebei, Henan, Shanxi,
  Shaanxi — the Federation does not feed its current population.** The soil is excellent and the water is not
  there. What the north supports is dryland wheat, millet and sorghum at pre-industrial yields on rainfall, plus
  whatever the Central Route and surviving surface irrigation add. That is a real agricultural economy. It is not
  one that feeds the hundreds of millions currently living on that plain.
- **Millet and sorghum deserve emphasis.** They are the north's drought-tolerant traditional grains, they were
  domesticated on exactly this ground, and the North China Plain is described in the sources as China's main
  sorghum and millet area. **The crops for a drier, unirrigated north already exist and are native.** The yields
  are lower than irrigated wheat and maize, which is precisely the point.
- **And the bitter one: the best farmland available to any polity in the region — the Manchurian chernozem, which
  is rainfed and needs nothing — is outside the Federation.**

**Two things worth the author's attention before any reasoning about collapse:**

1. **These populations are already falling, hard, in the real world.** The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences
   projects mainland China at approximately **525 million by 2100** at current rates — a downward revision from a
   previous 597 million — and the demographer Yi Fuxian has estimated as low as 330 million. Japan is projected at
   42 million by the early 22nd century. **The author does not need a catastrophe to get large depopulation; the
   demographic projections already deliver it.** A 2083 event lands on populations that are on this evidence
   already well below their 2025 peaks.
2. **The historical anchor.** Pre-industrial China supported a very large population on essentially this ground
   with essentially the agronomy described in §6.6 — intensive paddy rice in the south, dryland cereals in the
   north, organic nitrogen cycling, and no synthetic inputs. ⚠️ **I could not verify specific historical Chinese
   population figures against a primary source this session**, and the author should look them up rather than take
   a number from me. But the shape of the answer is not in doubt: **this ground has carried hundreds of millions
   of people without a single pump, a single bag of urea or a single tonne of imported grain.** That is a
   materially different starting point from the North American territories in this project, most of which have no
   such precedent at all.

**Reasoning about where people are, and are not, in 2283:**

- **The Yangtze basin and the south are the demographic center of gravity.** Rainfed rice, reliable water, built
  terraces, fish. The **Wu**, **Yue**, **Min**, **Gan** and **Xiang** regions hold the most durable population
  base in the Federation, and the Yangtze delta — currently 20% of GDP — remains the most valuable ground in East
  Asia for reasons that predate industry.
- **The North China Plain depopulates substantially but does not empty.** The soil is superb and the rainfall is
  marginal. A dryland millet-sorghum-wheat economy on 409,500 km² supports a great many people; it does not
  support the current density.
- **The northern megacities are the sharpest case.** Beijing, Tianjin and the Hebei conurbation sit on the drier
  end of the plain, on mined aquifers, at the end of a water-transfer canal. **Beijing's water supply was already
  the strategic justification for the SNWTP.** With the Central Route working, a much smaller Beijing is
  supportable. Without it, the city has no basis.
- **Shanghai and the Wu delta are different and better placed.** They sit on the Yangtze, at its mouth, with more
  water than they can use — the opposite of Beijing's problem.
- **Japan is the hardest case in the region and possibly in the project.** Twelve percent of the land is
  cultivable, 97% of the oil is imported, there is no copper ore and no iron ore. What Japan has is **rainfall,
  forest, geothermal gradient, terraced rice land and the world's best-placed fishery.** A pre-industrial Japan of
  some tens of millions is a historically attested thing; an industrial Japan of 123 million is not. **Japan's own
  demographic projection of 42 million by the early 22nd century may be closer to its carrying capacity than its
  current population is.**
- **Korea sits on the same logic with a slightly better mineral position** (graphite and, in the north, tungsten)
  and the same fuel problem.
- **Taiwan is 23.9 million people on 35,808 km² with mountains over two-thirds of it, importing 98% of its energy
  and most of its grain.** The western plain is fertile and the rainfall is high, and that is the whole basis.
- **Mongolia barely changes.** 3.5 million people on steppe, herding livestock, is what the country did for
  millennia and is close to what the land supports. **Mongolia is arguably the least disrupted society in East
  Asia by a collapse**, and it happens to sit on the region's best accessible copper and coking coal. The author
  may find that combination useful.
- **Manchuria is the region's agricultural prize.** Rainfed black soil, 350,000 km², currently holding about
  96 million people across Liaoning, Jilin and Heilongjiang. **It is the one place in East Asia whose farmland
  could plausibly feed more people after a collapse than the land currently does in calorie terms, because its
  fertility is intrinsic rather than applied.**

### 6.8 Summary judgment for the author

**Eight findings, in the order of their usefulness.**

1. **The line the author drew is the right line and it is expensive.** The Great Wall, the 400 mm isohyet and the
   Hu Line nearly coincide, so the Federation's border is a genuine climatic and demographic boundary that has
   been redrawn in the same place for two thousand years. It is also the boundary that puts **88% of the people
   inside and the rare earths, 40% of the coal reserve, the river sources, the black soil and the founding oil
   field outside.** That is not a flaw in the premise; it is the premise's engine.
2. **The Yellow River is the Federation's permanent strategic wound.** Not just its source but a fifth of its
   length runs through foreign territory, and 420 million people in the provinces below depend on it. **The
   Yangtze is the Federation's reliable river, and the Yangtze is a southern river.** That is a built-in north–
   south asymmetry inside a federation that is already lopsided toward the Mandarin north demographically. The
   author now has a structural tension that does not need inventing.
3. **The Federation can make steel, but only in Shanxi, and only from bad ore.** The coal is there, in the **Jin**
   region, and the technique is historically documented. The high-grade ore is not there and never was. For at
   least a century the answer is **scrap**, of which East Asia holds the largest accumulation in human history.
4. **The four open-question provinces are the sharpest decision in the file, and the evidence points two ways.**
   Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi and Gansu hold the Federation's nickel, most of its tin and manganese, its largest
   aluminum, lead and zinc reserves, over 40% of its phosphate, a large block of coal and the Hexi Corridor —
   160.88 million people. **And they are, on the author's own language map, a non-Han shatter zone.** A strict
   Qing-lines Federation is materially complete and ethnically incoherent; a Han-majority Federation is
   ethnically clean and cannot make stainless steel, bronze or fertilizer from its own ground. **This file marks
   the trade and does not resolve it.** Note only that Gansu is separable from the other three — strongest
   material case, weakest ethnic case against.
5. **Taiwan and Japan hold the highest productive sophistication on Earth and the thinnest material base on
   Earth.** Japan was first in the world in economic complexity for twenty-eight consecutive years and lost the
   position to Taiwan in 2023; Taiwan earns 43.41% of its foreign exchange from one product line that has no
   post-collapse afterlife. **Neither has copper ore, iron ore, or fuel.** They are the highest-falling societies
   in the author's entire material-base project.
6. **Mongolia is the region's inversion.** Lowest complexity, least disrupted society, and world-scale copper,
   coking coal and fluorspar sitting shallow in the Gobi eighty kilometers from the Chinese border. **Tavan
   Tolgoi is the nearest large metallurgical-coal body to the Chinese steel districts.** A reconstruction-era
   Chinese polity that wants good steel has to deal with Mongolia, and Mongolia has 3.5 million people.
7. **Manchuria holds the best farmland in East Asia and it is outside every line the author has drawn.** Rainfed
   chernozem over deep loess, 350,000 km², needing no irrigation. It is also — on the author's language map — Han
   by settlement and not Manchu, which makes it the one clean settler society in the region and a genuinely
   different kind of polity from the rest.
8. **The south feeds itself and the north does not.** The Qinling–Huaihe 800 mm line stops being a wheat-versus-
   rice boundary and becomes the line between a region that farms on rainfall and a region that farmed on
   aquifers. **The single most valuable repairable object in the northern half of the Federation is the
   South–North Water Transfer Central Route, because it is gravity-fed from Danjiangkou to Beijing and needs no
   pump.** Whether that channel stays open is, more than any other single artifact, what determines whether
   northern China in 2283 holds tens of millions of people or hundreds of millions.

## 7. Explicit Uncertainties and Unverified Items

**Every item below is flagged in the body text as well. They are collected here so nothing is lost when a section
is read in isolation. This folder feeds a canon repository with a zero-error standard, and an unflagged guess
reads as settled fact later.**

1. ⚠️ **Japan's modern food self-sufficiency ratio.** The commonly cited calorie-basis figure of roughly 38% could
   **not** be verified against a MAFF primary source this session; the MAFF English statistical pages did not
   return it and the web search budget was exhausted. The verified statements are the 1990 figures (67% of
   agricultural products, ~30% of cereal and fodder needs) and the import table in §1.5. **Verify before using a
   number.** (§3.11)
2. ⚠️ **"The northwest Pacific is the world's most productive fishing region."** Could not be verified against an
   FAO primary source (FAO Major Fishing Area 61). The claim is widely repeated and consistent with the verified
   facts around it, but it is **not** sourced here. (§3.10)
3. ⚠️ **Shanxi's "roughly one third of China's coal reserves" and Xinjiang's "about 40% of reserves, 2.2 trillion
   tonnes" use different reserve concepts and do not sum.** The Xinjiang figure is plainly an in-place resource
   estimate; the Shanxi figure reads as a share of proven recoverable reserves. **Do not add them.** (§2.2)
4. ⚠️ **The container-port table mixes two sources.** The ranking is Lloyd's List 2023; the leading volume column
   is World Shipping Council 2024. They are not consistent with each other — Guangzhou ranks sixth on a volume
   below seventh-ranked Busan's. Treat ordering and volume as separate statements. (§5.1)
5. ⚠️ **The "232 times greater than the United States" Chinese shipbuilding capacity multiple** rests on a single
   leaked US Navy briefing slide reported by the Alliance for American Manufacturing in September 2023 and
   described as verified by the Office of Naval Intelligence. **Contested, not a measurement.** The 50%/67%/55%
   market-share figures are the defensible statement. (§5.3)
6. ⚠️ **Oyu Tolgoi and Tavan Tolgoi current status.** Operating status, ownership terms and 2026 production could
   not be verified. The figures given are design and resource figures. Both projects have contested political
   histories and the Oyu Tolgoi agreement has been renegotiated more than once. (§2.7)
7. ⚠️ **Province-by-province Chinese iron ore reserves.** Not obtained. Bayan Obo's 470 Mt and the Anshan district
   are verified; the residual inside/outside split is not. (§2.3)
8. ⚠️ **The Nanling ion-adsorption rare-earth districts.** Location and existence not in dispute; production share
   and reserve figures not verified. (§2.1)
9. ⚠️ **Japanese domestic coal and copper reserves** and the condition of closed workings (Ashio, Besshi,
   Hitachi). Not verified. Do not assign a number. (§2.8, §6.2)
10. ⚠️ **Chinese chromium.** Gansu is listed as holding chromium among many minerals, but China is not a
    world-scale producer and the reserve position was not verified. Without chromium there is no stainless steel
    regardless of nickel. (§2.9)
11. ⚠️ **Chinese potash.** Lop Nur in Xinjiang is described as the country's second-largest source. The largest,
    Qarhan in Qinghai, is also outside the line and its figures were not verified. (§2.9)
12. ⚠️ **Taiwanese minerals.** Taiwan does not appear as a named producer in any USGS table consulted. Its
    limestone, marble, coal and gas were not independently verified. (§2.8)
13. ⚠️ **North Korean production figures** in USGS are estimates, and North Korean trade figures reflect sanctions
    and reporting conditions rather than productive capacity. (§1.4, §2.8)
14. ⚠️ **Shengli Oil Field** production and reserve figures not retrieved. (§4.4)
15. ⚠️ **Xiluodu and Wudongde** individual capacities not verified. (§4.2)
16. ⚠️ **The Grand Canal's** verified dimensions and current navigable extent not retrieved. (§5.5)
17. ⚠️ **Japanese and Korean uranium fuel-cycle dependence** is stated as an inference from their absence in USGS
    uranium coverage, not from a direct source. (§4.3)
18. ⚠️ **Korean rice self-sufficiency policy** is asserted from the small rice import figure, not from a policy
    source. (§3.11)
19. ⚠️ **Historical Chinese population figures** for the carrying-capacity anchor in §6.7 were not verified. The
    shape of the argument is sound; the author should source any specific number.
20. ⚠️ **Chinese arable and irrigated area figures conflict across sources** — 525,800 km² irrigated in one and
    626,000 km² in another, from different years and definitions. Not reconciled. (§3.1)
21. ⚠️ **Carried forward from the ethnolinguistic file**, and repeated here because this survey leans on it: that
    map is a **language map, not an ethnicity map**; it carries **no visible date or source** and its filename is
    a social-media hash; **Sinitic classification is contested** (whether Jin, Huizhou and Ping are top-level
    groups is a live dispute); and it shows **language distribution, not population**, so a large area may hold
    very few people. (§0.3, §2.6)
22. **Three Gorges siltation projections are disputed in the source itself, and the dam sits on a seismic fault.**
    Not an unverified claim — a disputed one, flagged as such. (§4.2)
