# Sonora — Real-World Material Base

**What this file is:** a factual, real-world research baseline for the neo-country **La República de Sonora**, compiled
2026-09-22. It is *not* fiction and contains no invented content. It documents what the territory actually holds —
geology, water, soil, industry, infrastructure — so the author can extrapolate a post-2083 reconstruction society from
an accurate material floor rather than from guesswork. The closing **Post-Collapse Assessment** is the only
interpretive section, and it is reasoning *from* the facts above it, clearly labeled as such.

**Standing constraint observed:** the author's **"No National Stereotypes"** rule. This territory is treated in exactly
the register that would be used for any other — ore grades, acre-feet, hectares, megawatts, tonnages. No cultural
characterization, no cartel or security framing, no assumptions about governance or political temperament. Where a
human-conflict fact is included (the 2020 La Boquilla water protest, the Yaqui water-rights dispute, the Cananea labor
history), it is included because it is a *material* fact about who controls a physical water or ore asset, and it is
stated neutrally.

## 0. Territory Definition and Caveats

**In scope (per the author's map, as corrected 2026-09-22):**

- **Mexico:** Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas
- **United States:** Arizona and New Mexico, both entire

**Explicitly out of scope:** all of Texas (assigned to the neighboring CSA) and the Baja California peninsula (assigned
to Cascadia). This departs from Colin Woodard's "El Norte," which does reach into south and west Texas; the author's
map does not follow him there. Exact borders remain the author's to set, and El Norte's northern/eastern reach is
genuinely contested with other regions in the source material.

**Three consequences of the corrected borders, which change the strategic picture materially:**

1. **The territory is NOT landlocked on the Gulf of Mexico.** Tamaulipas is bordered on the east by the Gulf along its
   full length, and holds the ports of Tampico, Altamira, and the Matamoros coast. This was flagged as uncertain and is
   now confirmed: Tamaulipas borders Texas for about 370 km to the north and the Gulf of Mexico to the east
   ([Tamaulipas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamaulipas)). I could not pin down an authoritative figure for
   Tamaulipas's exact coastline length; treat "roughly 400 km of Gulf frontage" as approximate. **The territory is
   two-ocean.**
2. **The Gulf of California is a shared frontier, not an internal sea.** Losing Baja means Sonora holds only the
   *eastern* shore. Guaymas and Puerto Peñasco face a body of water whose western shore belongs to Cascadia. Naval and
   fishing access to the Gulf of California is contested by construction.
3. **The Rio Grande / Río Bravo becomes an international border with the CSA** for essentially its entire lower course.
   Everything in the 1944 water treaty section below converts, in-world, into a Sonora/CSA dispute — and note the
   direction of leverage: the *upper* Rio Grande and Elephant Butte Reservoir are in New Mexico, i.e. **in-territory
   and upstream**, while the Río Conchos, the river's single largest tributary, rises in Chihuahua, also in-territory.
   Sonora sits upstream of the CSA on both the main stem and the main tributary.

**Severed twin cities.** Juárez–El Paso, Nuevo Laredo–Laredo, and Matamoros–Brownsville are cut by the new CSA border.
**Twins that become internal** to Sonora, because both halves are in scope: Nogales–Nogales, Douglas–Agua Prieta,
Columbus–Palomas, Santa Teresa–San Jerónimo.

## 1. Minerals and Mining

This is the territory's defining material asset and the section that most rewards depth. The short version: **this may
be the most metal-rich territory in North America**, and unusually, it is rich across the *complete* set needed for an
industrial base rather than in one or two commodities.

### 1.1 Copper — world-class, on both sides of the line

Arizona was the leading US copper-producing state in 2024, accounting for **about 70% of domestic output**. Total US
mine production was an estimated **1.1 million metric tons** of recoverable copper, worth roughly $10 billion. US
reserves stand at **47 million tons**; **Mexico's at 53 million tons** — larger than the US figure
([USGS MCS 2025, Copper](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-copper.pdf)). Mexican mine production was
about **700,000 tons** in 2024. Put those together and this single territory contains a very large share of North
American copper reserves and the great majority of its production.

**Named Arizona and New Mexico operations:**

| Mine | Location | Operator | Scale / notes |

| **Bagdad** | Yavapai Co., AZ | Freeport-McMoRan | ~873.6 Mt at 0.36% Cu. Concentrator rated 75,000 t/day. Copper **and molybdenum**; oxide minerals chrysocolla, malachite, azurite. |

**Named Sonoran operations:**

- **Buenavista del Cobre (Cananea)**, Cananea municipality, Sonora — Grupo México. One of the largest copper reserves
  in the world: approximately **4.52 billion tonnes of ore at 0.42% Cu**. Sits about 35 km south of the international
  border. Copper has been mined continuously on the site since 1899; ownership ran Greene Consolidated → Anaconda →
  Mexican state → Grupo México (1990). In August 2014 about **40,000 m³ of acidic wastewater** spilled into the
  Bacanuchi and Sonora rivers — relevant to the author as a durable fact about downstream water quality in the Río
  Sonora corridor. ([Buenavista mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buenavista_mine))
- **La Caridad**, Nacozari de García, Sonora — Grupo México. A very large copper–molybdenum operation with associated
  smelting and refining at Nacozari. **I could not retrieve a verifiable public profile with tonnages this session**
  (no English or Spanish encyclopedia article resolved, and my web-search budget was exhausted). The author should
  treat La Caridad as certainly major but should verify specific numbers independently.
- **Milpillas**, Sonora — an underground copper operation; unverified this session.

**Smelting and refining — the strategically important part.** The USGS counts, for the whole United States, only
**2 primary copper smelters, 2 primary electrolytic refineries, and 14 electrowon refineries**
([USGS Copper](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-copper.pdf)). Arizona hosts primary copper smelting at
**Hayden** (ASARCO) and historically at **Miami** (Freeport). **Flagged discrepancy:** if Hayden, Miami, and Kennecott's
smelter near Salt Lake City were all operating, that would be three, not two. I could not reconcile which two the USGS
is counting in the 2025 edition. The load-bearing point for the author survives the ambiguity either way: **US primary
copper smelting capacity is concentrated in Arizona**, i.e. inside this territory, and Mexico's is at Nacozari and La
Caridad, also inside it.

### 1.2 Silver — Mexico is the world's leading producer

Mexico produced an estimated **6,300 metric tons of silver in 2024**, far ahead of China (3,300 t) and Peru (3,100 t),
against a world total of about 25,000 t. Mexican reserves are **37,000 t**. The US produced 1,100 t and imports 44% of
its silver from Mexico ([USGS MCS 2025, Silver](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-silver.pdf)).

**Important honesty caveat for the author:** Mexico's *single largest* silver operations — the Fresnillo/Proaño and
Saucito complex and Juanicipio — are in **Zacatecas**, which is **outside this territory**, as is Cienega in Durango
([Fresnillo plc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnillo_plc)). So "La República de Sonora inherits the world's number
one silver producer" is *not* quite right as stated. What it does inherit:

- The historic silver–lead–zinc districts of **Chihuahua** — Santa Bárbara, Parral, and **Naica** (the last globally
  famous for its giant selenite crystal caves, and a working lead–zinc–silver mine).
- Silver as a byproduct of the great Sonoran copper mines. Buenavista's reserve statement alone cites **33.4 million
  ounces of silver** alongside the copper and zinc.
- **The refining capacity.** This is the real prize — see below.

**Met-Mex Peñoles, Torreón, Coahuila.** Industrias Peñoles is the **world leader in refined silver production**, and its
main product — 99.99% pure silver in ingot and granulated form — is made at the **Met-Mex metallurgical complex in
Torreón**, from which it is exported worldwide. Peñoles produces roughly **80.5 million troy ounces of silver
(~2,500 t) and 756,100 troy ounces of gold (~23.5 t) annually**, plus zinc, lead, copper, **bismuth, and cadmium**
([Industrias Peñoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrias_Pe%C3%B1oles)). Torreón is **in-territory**. A
reconstruction polity holding Torreón holds a non-ferrous smelting and refining complex capable of separating a
polymetallic ore stream into finished metals — a capability that is much rarer and harder to rebuild than a mine.

### 1.3 Gold

Mexico produced about **130 t of gold in 2024** (reserves 1,400 t); the US produced 160 t, but **Nevada accounts for
~70% and Alaska ~16%**, so Arizona and New Mexico gold is minor and largely a copper byproduct — roughly 7% of US gold
comes as a byproduct of base-metal, chiefly copper, ores
([USGS MCS 2025, Gold](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-gold.pdf)). Mexico supplies 38% of US gold
doré imports.

In-territory gold is concentrated in **Sonora** and **Chihuahua**: **La Herradura** in Sonora (Fresnillo plc; the one
Fresnillo mine that *is* inside this territory), **Mulatos** in Sonora (Alamos Gold), and **Pinos Altos** and
**Palmarejo** in Chihuahua. Sonora is commonly described as Mexico's leading gold-producing state; **I could not verify
that ranking against a primary source this session** — flagged.

### 1.4 Zinc, lead, and the refining gap

- **Zinc:** Mexico produced **700,000 t** in 2024 with reserves of **14 million tons**, against US production of
  750,000 t and reserves of 9.2 Mt. US zinc is mined in five states, **none of them Arizona or New Mexico** — so
  essentially all in-territory zinc is on the Mexican side
  ([USGS MCS 2025, Zinc](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-zinc.pdf)).
- **Lead:** Mexico produced **180,000 t** with reserves of **5.6 million tons**
  ([USGS MCS 2025, Lead](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-lead.pdf)).

**The single most important structural fact in this subsection:** the United States has had **no primary lead refinery
since 2013**. Nearly all US lead concentrate has been exported since then, and ~70% of US lead consumption is met by
**secondary** lead recovered from scrapped lead-acid batteries. Mexico, by contrast, retains primary lead capacity at
Torreón. For a reconstruction society, lead matters enormously — lead-acid batteries are the most forgiving,
most repairable electrical storage technology in existence, and the lead-acid battery industry accounts for about 86%
of US lead consumption today. **The territory's primary lead smelting capability sits at Met-Mex Torreón, not
anywhere on the US side.**

### 1.5 Molybdenum

US mine production was **33,000 t** in 2024, with **four of the seven US byproduct molybdenum operations in Arizona**.
Mexico produced **17,000 t** (reserves 130,000 t), making it a top-five world producer, overwhelmingly as a byproduct
of Sonoran copper — principally La Caridad
([USGS MCS 2025, Molybdenum](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-molybdenum.pdf)). Molybdenum is an
alloying agent for cast iron, steel, and superalloys — i.e. this territory can make *good* steel, not just steel.

### 1.6 Potash — New Mexico, and a quietly enormous fact

The **majority of US potash production comes from southeastern New Mexico**, around Carlsbad, where two companies run
two underground mines and one deep-well solution mine. The ores are **sylvinite and langbeinite**
([USGS MCS 2025, Potash](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-potash.pdf)). US marketable production was
about **420,000 t K₂O** in 2024 against apparent consumption of 6.4 million tons — the US is massively import-dependent
(chiefly on Canada). About 70% of US potash output is SOPM/langbeinite and SOP, the chloride-free forms needed for
chloride-sensitive crops.

**Why this matters more than its dollar value suggests:** potassium is one of the three irreplaceable macronutrients.
Nitrogen can be fixed industrially or biologically; phosphorus and potassium must be mined. A territory that holds a
working potash district holds one leg of the fertilizer tripod permanently. **Carlsbad is in-territory.**

### 1.7 Uranium, and the fuel cycle

**New Mexico has the second-largest identified uranium reserves of any US state**, after Wyoming — but **no uranium ore
has been mined in New Mexico since 1998**. Almost all of it lies in the **Grants Mineral Belt** along the southern edge
of the San Juan Basin in McKinley and Cibola counties, comprising the Gallup, Grants, and Laguna districts and the
areas of Church Rock, Smith Lake, Ambrosia Lake, and Paguate/Jackpile. Between 1950 and 1980 the majority of all US
uranium came out of this belt; the **Jackpile mine** alone produced 25 million tons of ore at its height. The host rocks
are Jurassic Morrison Formation sandstones (Poison Canyon, Westwater Canyon). There are long-lasting contamination
impacts on Navajo and neighboring communities from this era, with ongoing remediation
([Uranium mining in New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining_in_New_Mexico)).

Arizona holds breccia-pipe uranium deposits near the Grand Canyon (Pinyon Plain / Canyon Mine) — high-grade but small
and politically constrained; unverified in detail this session.

**URENCO USA / National Enrichment Facility, Eunice, New Mexico** — a gas-centrifuge uranium enrichment plant using
Zippe-type centrifuges, built for a full capacity of **5.9 million SWU**
([National Enrichment Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Enrichment_Facility)). This is the only
commercial uranium enrichment facility in the United States, and it is inside this territory. So is the **Waste
Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP)** near Carlsbad.

### 1.8 Lithium — treat the Sonora story with real caution

The Bacadéhuachi / "Sonora Lithium" clay deposit in northeastern Sonora is frequently described in press coverage as
one of the world's largest lithium deposits. **The USGS numbers do not support the strongest versions of that claim.**
In the 2025 Mineral Commodity Summaries, **Mexico does not appear as a named country in the world lithium reserves
table at all** — it falls into "other countries" — and Mexico's *resources* are listed at **1.7 million tons** of
lithium content, against Argentina and Bolivia at 23 million tons each and Chile at 11 million. USGS notes only that
"lithium-clay sources were in various stages of development or exploration in Mexico"
([USGS MCS 2025, Lithium](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-lithium.pdf)).

Two further cautions, both important:

1. **Clay-hosted lithium has never been brought into large-scale commercial production anywhere in the world.** It is a
   harder metallurgical problem than brine or hard-rock spodumene. A deposit is not a mine.
2. Mexico nationalized lithium in 2022 and created a state entity (LitioMx), and the status of the Bacadéhuachi
   concessions has been disputed since. **I could not verify the current status** — my web-search budget was exhausted
   before I could confirm it. Flagged as unresolved.

**Recommendation to the author:** lithium is the *weakest* of this territory's mineral claims, not the strongest. The
copper–silver–lead–zinc–molybdenum–potash–iron–coal set is where the genuine, verifiable wealth is, and it is far more
useful to a reconstruction society than lithium would be anyway.

### 1.9 Iron ore, metallurgical coal, and integrated steel — the hidden keystone

This is the fact the author should probably build the most on, because it is rarer than the copper.

**Altos Hornos de México (AHMSA), Monclova, Coahuila** was Mexico's largest integrated steel producer, founded 1942.
Critically, it was built at Monclova precisely because of **co-location**: it operated its own **metallurgical coal
mines in the Coahuila Región Carbonífera, about 110 km from Monclova**, moved by rail; and its own **iron ore deposits
in the Coahuila desert**, whose concentrate reached Monclova through a **295-kilometer slurry pipeline (ferroducto)**.
Two steel plants covering about 1,200 hectares. Capacity rose from 40,000 t of liquid steel per year in 1944 to
2 million t by 1960 and about **3.75 million t/yr** after 1970s expansions
([Altos Hornos de México](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altos_Hornos_de_M%C3%A9xico)).

**Current status: collapsed.** AHMSA has been in bankruptcy proceedings on the Mexican exchange for many years; its
long-time chairman was removed in April 2023 amid major fraud proceedings, the company was sold to Argentem Creek
Partners that month, workers had gone years without pay, and debt of roughly US$650 million remained. **The author
should verify AHMSA's status as of 2026 independently — my information runs to roughly 2024–25 and the situation was
actively changing.**

**Coal.** Industrial coal mining in Mexico dates to **1884 in the Sabinas Basin, Coahuila**, and **the vast majority of
the nation's known reserves remain in that relatively small Región Carbonífera**. Mexican proved recoverable reserves
were about **1.3 billion tons** as of 2013 (25th in the world). Since 1910, Mexican coal has gone primarily to
steelmaking ([Coal mining in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_mining_in_Mexico)).

**So: iron ore, metallurgical coal, limestone (CEMEX's raw material base is in the same region), and an integrated
steel mill all sit within a few hundred kilometers of one another in Coahuila.** That is the complete ingredient list
for primary ironmaking. Almost no other territory in North America has all four co-located inside one plausible
border. Note also the alternative: Ternium and Deacero run **electric-arc** steelmaking, which remelts scrap and needs
a large reliable power supply but no coke — a different and in some ways easier path.

### 1.10 What the territory does *not* have

Being honest about gaps matters as much as cataloging strengths:

- **No bauxite / no aluminum.** Mexico has no significant bauxite; primary aluminum is out of reach. Nemak's aluminum
  engine-block business in Nuevo León runs on imported or recycled metal.
- **Tin, nickel, and chromium** are weak to absent. Stainless steel and bronze alloying are constrained.
- **No large-scale domestic rare-earth separation.**
- Copper reserves are enormous but at **very low grades** (0.16–0.42%), which is entirely an artifact of cheap energy.
  See the post-collapse section — this is the single biggest trap in reading the mining statistics.

## 2. Water — the Defining Constraint

Every other section in this file is conditioned on this one. The territory's mineral wealth is close to unbounded; its
water is hard-capped and currently over-allocated on both of its major river systems.

### 2.1 The Colorado River

The **1922 Colorado River Compact** divides the basin into an Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and a
Lower Basin (Arizona, Nevada, California), with **7.5 million acre-feet per year (maf/yr) apportioned to each**, and
requires the Upper Basin not to deplete flow below **75 maf over any ten consecutive years**. The apportionment was set
from rainfall patterns observed in the years before 1922 — **an unusually wet period — and the compact therefore
over-allocated the river from the day it was signed**
([Colorado River Compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_River_Compact)). The **1944 treaty** allocates
**1.5 maf/yr to Mexico**. Arizona's Lower Basin apportionment is 2.8 maf/yr.

**Storage, and how bad it currently is:**

- **Lake Mead** — capacity **28.23 maf** at full pool (surface elevation 1,229 ft), the largest US reservoir by
  capacity. It has been below full since 1983. It fell to **1,040.40 ft on 7 August 2026 — the lowest level since the
  reservoir was first filled — and to 1,038.83 ft by 31 August 2026.** Hoover Dam **stops generating electricity below
  950 ft**, and the reservoir reaches dead pool, stabilizing at the lowest outlet, at about **895 ft**. Las Vegas spent
  roughly $1.5 billion on a third intake tunnel at elevation 860 ft to stay below any plausible drawdown
  ([Lake Mead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Mead)).
- **Lake Powell** — capacity **24.322 maf**. It hit **3,519.07 ft in August 2026**, around 22–23% of capacity, below
  even the April 2022 record. Glen Canyon Dam's required annual release to Mead is **8.23 maf**, reduced to 7.48 maf in
  2014 for the first time since the reservoir filled. Powell is also **losing about 33,270 acre-feet of storage
  capacity per year to sediment** from the Colorado and San Juan rivers — roughly 7% of capacity lost since 1963
  ([Lake Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Powell)).
- Mead's structural deficit: inflow moderated by Glen Canyon at ~8.23 maf against outflow (including evaporation and
  deliveries) of **9.5–9.7 maf**, i.e. a **net annual deficit of roughly 1.2 maf**.

**The delta.** The Colorado River Delta has largely disappeared and the river no longer reliably reaches the Gulf of
California. **Morelos Dam**, at the Baja California–Arizona line, is the last major diversion. Minute 319 to the 1944
treaty produced a deliberate **pulse flow in 2014** that briefly reconnected the river to the sea; subsequent minutes
(323 and later) continued environmental-flow and storage cooperation. Note the border consequence: the delta itself now
**straddles the Sonora/Cascadia line**, and the Mexicali Valley irrigation district (DR 014) is in Baja California and
therefore **out of territory**. **San Luis Río Colorado is in Sonora** — Sonora holds the *east* bank of the lower
Colorado ([Colorado River Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_River_Delta),
[Morelos Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morelos_Dam)).

### 2.2 The Central Arizona Project

The **CAP is a 336-mile (541 km) diversion canal** taking Colorado River water from Lake Havasu near Parker into
central and southern Arizona, serving Phoenix and Tucson and, by design, nearly a million acres of irrigated farmland
in Maricopa, Pinal, and Pima counties. In exchange for federal funding, Arizona was required to **restrict groundwater
withdrawals and recharge depleted aquifers with CAP water** — the origin of the 1980 Groundwater Management Act and the
Active Management Areas ([Central Arizona Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Arizona_Project)).

Authorization also existed to extend CAP facilities into Catron, Hidalgo, and Grant counties in **New Mexico**, but
**these were never built**, for cost, demand, repayment, and environmental reasons. New Mexico gets no CAP water.

**The CAP is a pumped system.** It lifts water roughly 2,900 feet over its length. It has the **junior-most priority**
on the Colorado, meaning it takes the first and deepest cuts in a shortage. Both facts matter enormously below.

### 2.3 The Rio Grande / Río Bravo — in-world, the CSA frontier

The Rio Grande runs **1,896 miles (3,051 km)**, fourth-longest in North America by main stem. **Since the mid-twentieth
century only about 20% of its water reaches the Gulf of Mexico**, the rest consumed by irrigation and municipal supply
([Rio Grande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Grande)).

- **Elephant Butte Reservoir** (New Mexico, Rio Grande Project, filling from 1915): capacity **2,065,010 acre-feet**,
  drainage 28,900 sq mi, irrigating **178,000 acres**. In recent years it has held **as little as 3% of capacity**
  ([Elephant Butte Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant_Butte_Reservoir)). **Caballo** sits below it.
- **The dry reaches are real and recurring.** From Las Cruces downstream through Ojinaga the river frequently runs dry
  — the stretch nicknamed "the Forgotten River." Average discharge near **Presidio is 178 cubic feet per second, and is
  frequently zero**, down from 945 cfs at Elephant Butte Dam. The river is restored below Presidio **by the Río
  Conchos**. In late July 2022 the Rio Grande **ran dry for about 50 miles in the middle valley, including five miles
  inside Albuquerque** — the first time in over 40 years — and went dry in Albuquerque again in 2023.
- **The Río Conchos** is the Rio Grande's largest tributary and the main river of Chihuahua, rising in the Sierra Madre
  Occidental and joining the Rio Grande at Ojinaga. It is dammed at **La Boquilla** (forming Lago Toronto), at **El
  Granero**, and at **Toribio Ortega**. Camargo and Delicias are its agricultural centers
  ([Río Conchos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Conchos)).
- **La Boquilla Dam**: masonry arch-gravity, built 1910–15, capacity **2.903 km³ (2,354,000 acre-feet)**, powerhouse
  **25 MW** (164.66 GWh in 2004). In **September 2020** farmers protested the release of water from La Boquilla toward
  the United States under the 1944 treaty; **one demonstrator was killed**
  ([La Boquilla Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Boquilla_Dam)).
- **Treaty obligations.** The **1906 Convention** accorded **60,000 acre-feet per year** to Mexico on the upper Rio
  Grande, tied to the Rio Grande Project and Elephant Butte. The **1944 treaty** governs the lower river; Mexico's
  obligation is widely cited as **1,750,000 acre-feet per five-year cycle** delivered from six named tributaries,
  chiefly the Conchos. **I was not able to verify that specific figure against a primary IBWC source this session** —
  flagged, though it is standard in the literature. Mexican delivery shortfalls have recurred, notably in the
  2020 and 2024–25 cycles.
- **Interstate conflict:** *Texas v. New Mexico and Colorado* has been litigated at the US Supreme Court over Rio Grande
  Project deliveries. In 2022, New Mexico's water debt to Texas rose from 31,000 to over 130,000 acre-feet; New Mexico
  expanded payments to farmers to fallow fields, and **Albuquerque shut off its surface diversion and switched to full
  groundwater pumping in 2021**.
- **San Juan–Chama Project:** a trans-basin diversion carrying **62,780 acre-feet/yr** from San Juan River tributaries
  under the Continental Divide to Heron Reservoir and thence to the Rio Chama and Rio Grande. **Albuquerque owns 48,200
  acre-feet**, about three-quarters. Completed 1971; Albuquerque only began using it municipally in 2008. Note for the
  post-collapse section: **this is a gravity tunnel, not a pumped lift.**
- Main-stem dams: Rio Grande, Cochiti, Elephant Butte, Caballo, **Amistad**, **Falcon**, Anzalduas, Retamal. Amistad and
  Falcon are jointly operated international reservoirs — in-world, **shared Sonora/CSA infrastructure**.

### 2.4 The Sonoran and northeastern Mexican rivers

**Río Yaqui** — the largest river system in Sonora. It forms at the confluence of the Bavispe and Aros/Papigochi in the
Sierra Madre Occidental and reaches the Gulf of California near San Ignacio Río Muerto. Length about 410 km (or
~1,050 km counting the Papigochi headwaters). Three major dams
([Río Yaqui](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADo_Yaqui)):

- **La Angostura (Lázaro Cárdenas)** — uppermost.
- **El Novillo (Plutarco Elías Calles)** — in operation since November 1964, **capacity 2,925 hm³**, powerhouse
  **135 MW**. Its construction drowned the towns of Tepupa, Suaqui, and Batuc
  ([Presa El Novillo](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presa_El_Novillo)).
- **El Oviáchic (Álvaro Obregón)** — the irrigation dam that directly serves the Yaqui Valley and Ciudad Obregón.

These supply **Distrito de Riego 041 (Río Yaqui)**. The **Río Mayo** (Mocúzarit dam) supplies **DR 038**. The
**Independencia aqueduct**, which moves Yaqui basin water to Hermosillo, has been the subject of a sustained legal and
political dispute over **Yaqui tribal water rights** — a material fact about who controls the Yaqui's flow. The Yaqui
people irrigated corn, beans, and squash along this river from at least the 6th century AD.

**Río Sonora** — smaller, serves Hermosillo's corridor; carries the legacy of the 2014 Buenavista spill.

**Nuevo León / Tamaulipas:** the **Río San Juan** and **Río Salado** join the Rio Grande from the south in Tamaulipas.
**Presa El Cuchillo (Cuchillo-Solidaridad)**, on the San Juan at China, Nuevo León, inaugurated 1994, **capacity
1,123 hm³**, operated by Servicios de Agua y Drenaje de Monterrey — **its primary function is supplying the Monterrey
metropolitan area**, via a pumping station ([Presa El Cuchillo](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presa_El_Cuchillo)).
**Presa Marte R. Gómez** also sits on the San Juan. Monterrey additionally draws on Cerro Prieto and La Boca.

### 2.5 Groundwater

- **Arizona:** the 1980 Groundwater Management Act created Active Management Areas with pumping restrictions, but large
  rural basins — the **Willcox** basin is the standard example — remain effectively unregulated, with ongoing overdraft,
  **land subsidence, and earth fissures**. The CAP's original bargain was explicitly to substitute Colorado water for
  groundwater and to recharge aquifers.
- **New Mexico:** the eastern plains sit on the **Ogallala/High Plains aquifer**, which is being mined rather than
  recharged; the Mesilla and Mimbres basins are separately stressed. Albuquerque reverted to **full groundwater
  pumping in 2021**.
- **Mexico:** CONAGUA maintains a national register of overexploited aquifers, and a large share of them are in these
  northern states. The **Costa de Hermosillo** aquifer in Sonora is the canonical case of overdraft with **saltwater
  intrusion**. The **Comarca Lagunera** has both severe overdraft and **naturally occurring arsenic** in deep
  groundwater — a genuine public-health constraint independent of any collapse. **I could not verify the current
  CONAGUA overexploited-aquifer counts per state this session** — flagged.

**The Comarca Lagunera deserves a specific note** because it is an instructive closed system: it spans Coahuila and
Durango across 44,887 km², built on the rich alluvium of the **Nazas and Aguanaval rivers**, **neither of which reaches
any ocean** — they are endorheic and historically fed inland lakes. Large-scale irrigated cotton was developed here;
after the Lázaro Cárdenas dam was built to control flooding, **the groundwater ceased to be recharged by flooding**
([Comarca Lagunera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comarca_Lagunera)). It is now a major dairy basin sustained by
pumping from an aquifer that no longer refills.

### 2.6 Climate

The territory spans the **Sonoran Desert** (west, bi-seasonal rainfall, winter frontal plus summer monsoon, the
saguaro/mesquite/palo verde community) and the **Chihuahuan Desert** (east, higher elevation, summer-dominated
rainfall, grassland-shrubland). Rainfall ranges from **under 100 mm/yr** at Yuma and the lower Colorado to roughly
**300–500 mm/yr** in the Chihuahuan highlands and around Monterrey, rising to **900–1,200 mm/yr** on the Tamaulipas
coastal plain around Tampico — which is subtropical, not desert, and is by a wide margin **the wettest part of the
territory**.

The **North American Monsoon** delivers the summer rainfall that makes dryland and floodwater farming possible at all
in the eastern two-thirds. The region has been in a **21st-century megadrought**; tree-ring paleoclimate work (Williams
et al. and successors) places the 2000s–2020s among the driest multi-decade periods in over a millennium, with a
substantial attributed anthropogenic component. Colorado River flows have been below average since 2000, with
average-or-better conditions in only about five years of the first sixteen.

**Yuma is, per Guinness, the sunniest city on Earth** — sunshine about 90% of daylight hours, the driest and least
humid populated place in the contiguous US ([Yuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuma,_Arizona)).

## 3. Agriculture

### 3.1 The Yaqui and Mayo valleys — the territory's breadbasket, and a place of real historical weight

The **Valle del Yaqui** lies in southern Sonora between the Sierra Madre Occidental and the Gulf of California, north
of the Mayo valley, with Ciudad Obregón at its head. Its extent is approximately **450,000 hectares (4,500 km²)**.
Much of it east of the river was semi-desert scrub — mesquite and cactus — until the irrigation project conceived by
Carlos Conant in the late 19th century. **Wheat is the principal crop**, with oilseeds, cotton, and vegetables also
significant ([Valle del Yaqui](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valle_del_Yaqui)).

**This is where the Green Revolution began.** In **1945 Norman Borlaug carried out the wheat and rust research in the
Yaqui Valley** that led to the semi-dwarf, rust-resistant, high-yielding wheats that transformed global cereal
production. CIMMYT's **Campo Experimental Norman E. Borlaug (CENEB)** near Ciudad Obregón remains its principal wheat
breeding station. The shuttle-breeding method — alternating generations between the Yaqui Valley and the central
Mexican highlands — is why these wheats were photoperiod-insensitive and therefore adaptable worldwide
([CIMMYT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Maize_and_Wheat_Improvement_Center),
[Norman Borlaug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Borlaug)).

For the author: **the world's most important wheat germplasm collection and breeding program for arid irrigated
agriculture is physically located inside this territory.** That is a plot-grade fact.

### 3.2 Arizona — Yuma and the winter vegetable system

**Yuma County produces about 90% of all leafy vegetables grown in the United States between November and March**, from
roughly **107,908 acres** of vegetables (2017), worth $782 million — first in Arizona and third among all US counties.
Total county agricultural output runs about $1.14 billion/yr. The county also produces nearly all of Arizona's dates
(~4,500 t, $35 million) and most of its vegetable seed, plus lemons, citrus, and jojoba. During the winter season
**some 40,000 Mexican workers cross the border daily** to work these fields. **The water is Colorado River water**
([Yuma County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuma_County,_Arizona)).

Elsewhere in Arizona: cotton, alfalfa, durum wheat, and a substantial dairy sector, concentrated in Maricopa and Pinal
counties — largely CAP- and groundwater-irrigated.

### 3.3 New Mexico

**New Mexico chile** (*Capsicum annuum*, New Mexico Group) was developed from Pueblo and Hispano landraces by
horticulturist **Fabián García at what is now New Mexico State University, beginning in 1894**, and continued by
**Roy Nakayama**. It is grown principally in the Hatch and Mesilla valleys on Rio Grande Project water
([New Mexico chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_chile)). New Mexico is also a significant **pecan**
producer (Doña Ana County, historically Stahmann Farms) and has a large dairy sector; **I could not verify New Mexico's
current national milk-production ranking this session** — flagged, though it is high for a state of its size.

**Acequias.** Spanish settlers built community irrigation ditches near present-day Española, Santa Fe, Las Cruces,
Doña Ana, and Mesilla before the reservoirs existed, and the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) upheld those acequia
property rights**, which remain legally recognized in New Mexico today. These are **gravity-fed, community-governed,
pre-industrial systems that have operated continuously for centuries** — see the post-collapse section, where they
matter a great deal.

### 3.4 Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas

- **Chihuahua** is Mexico's leading **pecan** producer, and Mexico is among the top two pecan producers worldwide
  alongside the US (**exact ranking unverified this session**). Also apples around **Cuauhtémoc**, chile, cotton, and a
  substantial dairy sector. Much of it is **groundwater-irrigated**, which is the vulnerability.
- **Comarca Lagunera** (Torreón/Gómez Palacio, spanning Coahuila and Durango) — developed for large-scale irrigated
  cotton, now one of Mexico's principal **dairy basins**. See §2.5 for its water problem.
- **Coahuila** — **Parras de la Fuente** is the site of Casa Madero, generally cited as the oldest winery in the
  Americas; also goat dairy.
- **Tamaulipas** — sorghum, citrus, and sugarcane on the coastal plain; **Nuevo León** — citrus around Montemorelos.
  These are the territory's only substantially **rainfed** croplands.

### 3.5 Rangeland cattle

Extensive rangeland grazing is the dominant land use by area across Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Arizona, and New
Mexico, with a long-standing trade in Mexican feeder calves moving north through crossings including Agua Prieta and
Santa Teresa. **Desert rangeland stocking rates are very low** — commonly tens of acres per animal unit, rising to well
over a hundred in the driest country — which caps the herd far below what the map area suggests. **I could not verify
specific stocking-rate figures or the current status of the New World screwworm outbreak and associated border cattle
restrictions this session** — both flagged as needing the author's own check.

### 3.6 The pre-industrial layer — the real floor

This is the most important agricultural subsection for reconstruction purposes, because it describes what grows here
with **no pumps, no dams, no fossil fuel, and no imported inputs**:

- **Ak-chin / floodwater farming** as practiced by the Tohono O'odham: crops planted at the mouths of arroyos to catch
  summer monsoon runoff, requiring no permanent watercourse at all.
- **Acequia gravity irrigation** in northern New Mexico (§3.3).
- **Hopi, Zuni, and Pueblo dryland farming** on stored soil moisture and deep planting.
- **The crop package:** tepary beans (*Phaseolus acutifolius*, among the most drought-tolerant cultivated legumes in
  the world), desert-adapted maize landraces, squash, devil's claw, and the gathered staples — **mesquite pods**
  (high-protein flour), **agave** (roasted hearts, and fiber), **cholla buds**, prickly pear.
- Germplasm for all of this has been actively conserved (Native Seeds/SEARCH, Tucson), and — the deeper point — **these
  systems supported settled agricultural populations in this exact territory for well over a thousand years before any
  dam existed.**

## 4. Energy

### 4.1 Solar — genuinely among the best on Earth

The Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts have world-class direct normal irradiance, combining very high sunshine fraction,
low humidity, and high elevation over much of the area. NREL's 2012 technical-potential assessment found Arizona alone
could host **5,147 GW of photovoltaics and/or up to 3,528 GW of concentrating solar power** — more than three times
total US electricity consumption at the time
([Solar power in Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power_in_Arizona)). **I was not able to retrieve precise
NREL GHI/DNI values in kWh/m²/day this session** (search budget exhausted); the author should pull them from the NREL
National Solar Radiation Database at <https://nsrdb.nrel.gov/> if exact figures are wanted. Directionally, the claim
"among the best solar resource on Earth" is well supported.

Named plants:

- **Solana Generating Station**, Gila Bend, AZ (2013) — **280 MW gross** parabolic trough CSP on 1,920 acres, two
  140 MW gross / 125 MW net turbines. When built it was the **largest parabolic trough plant in the world and the
  first US solar plant with molten-salt thermal storage** — i.e. a *dispatchable* solar design, which matters far more
  than raw capacity ([Solana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solana_Generating_Station)).
- **Agua Caliente Solar Project**, AZ — reached 250 MW (AC) by September 2012, briefly the largest PV plant in the
  world.
- **Mesquite Solar**, AZ — developed in phases toward a planned ~700 MW.
- **Springerville Generating Station Solar System** (2001) — one of the first large-scale PV plants in the US.

### 4.2 Oil and gas — New Mexico is a first-rank producer

(Delaware Basin) half of the Permian** in Lea and Eddy counties.

Other hydrocarbon systems:

- **San Juan Basin**, northwestern New Mexico — a mature gas province, historically important for coalbed methane
  ([San Juan Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan_Basin)).
- **Burgos Basin** (Tamaulipas / Nuevo León / Coahuila) — Mexico's principal non-associated natural gas province.
  **I could not retrieve a verifiable profile this session** — flagged.
- **Sabinas Basin**, Coahuila — gas and coal.
- **Correction confirmed:** Mexico's major oil production (Cantarell, Ku-Maloob-Zaap, and the newer Zama discovery) is

  territory's oil is the New Mexico Permian, not Mexican crude.
- Mexico imports a large majority of its natural gas from the United States by pipeline; in-world, with New Mexico
  inside the same polity as Chihuahua and Sonora, **that trade becomes internal rather than international** — a
  genuinely significant structural change worth the author's attention.

### 4.3 Coal

Essentially all Mexican coal is in the **Sabinas Basin, Coahuila** (§1.9), feeding both steelmaking and the Carbón I /
Carbón II and José López Portillo generating stations at Nava. On the US side, the **San Juan** and **Four Corners**
generating stations in northwestern New Mexico are the large coal plants; San Juan closed in 2022 and Four Corners is
slated for retirement, and the **Navajo Generating Station near Page closed in 2019** along with the Kayenta mine. The
coal-fired fleet in this territory is in terminal decline in the real world — but the **coal itself remains in the
ground**, which is what matters for reconstruction.

### 4.4 Nuclear

**Palo Verde Generating Station**, near Tonopah, about 45 miles west of Phoenix — **three pressurized water reactors**,
each uprated to about 1.4 GW, roughly **3.3 GW average output and about 32 million MWh (32 TWh) annually**, serving on
the order of four million people. Operated by Arizona Public Service; co-owned with Salt River Project, El Paso
Electric, Southern California Edison, PNM Resources, SCPPA, and LADWP. Licensed through 2045/2046/2047
([Palo Verde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palo_Verde_Nuclear_Generating_Station)).

**The critical detail:** Palo Verde is **the only large nuclear plant in the world that is not near a large body of
water.** It condenses its steam using **treated municipal sewage effluent piped from nearby cities**. It is, in other
words, a power station whose cooling supply is downstream of an urban population — which is exactly backwards from
every other large plant, and has sharp consequences in a depopulation scenario.

Note also the **ownership list**: Palo Verde's co-owners include El Paso Electric (CSA, in-world), Southern California
Edison, SCPPA, and LADWP (Cascadia or beyond). In-world, the largest power station in the territory was historically
co-owned by entities in three different successor nations.

### 4.5 Hydro

Small relative to the Pacific Northwest, but not negligible, and **the dams are gravity structures that keep working
without much technology**:

- **Hoover, Davis, and Parker** on the Colorado (Hoover at the Nevada/Arizona line; power ceases below Mead elevation
  950 ft).
- **Glen Canyon** upstream.
- **Elephant Butte**, New Mexico.
- **El Novillo, 135 MW**, and Oviáchic and Angostura on the Río Yaqui.
- **La Boquilla, 25 MW**, on the Río Conchos.

### 4.6 Wind, geothermal, and the grid

New Mexico has a genuinely good wind resource on its eastern plains (Broadview, Sagamore; the SunZia transmission
project was built to move it). **Arizona's wind resource is poor.** **Losing Baja California costs the territory the
Cerro Prieto geothermal field near Mexicali — one of the largest geothermal fields in the world — and the La Rumorosa
wind resource. Both are now in Cascadia.** That is a real subtraction from the energy portfolio and worth the author
noting explicitly.

**Grid topology:** Arizona and New Mexico sit in the **Western Interconnection (WECC)**, with the **Palo Verde hub** as
one of the West's major trading points. The Mexican states are on the **Sistema Interconectado Nacional (SIN)**, which
is a *separate* synchronous system. In-world, **La República de Sonora would straddle two non-synchronous grids** with
only limited DC ties between them — its northern half electrically oriented toward Cascadia and the Far West, its
southern half toward the rest of Mexico. (Baja California's own interconnection, tied to WECC rather than to the SIN,
is now out of scope.)

## 5. Industry and Manufacturing

### 5.1 The maquiladora / IMMEX belt

The program dates to the **Border Industrialization Program of 1965**, which lowered duties on imported machinery,
equipment, and raw materials so that foreign firms could import inputs duty-free, assemble, and re-export. By 1985
maquiladoras had overtaken tourism as Mexico's largest source of foreign exchange. Today **over 3,000 maquiladoras
along the border employ roughly one million workers and import more than $51 billion in supplies into Mexico**; they
accounted for about 45% of Mexican exports as of 2006 ([Maquiladora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maquiladora)).
**These figures are somewhat dated in my sources — the author should refresh them from INEGI's IMMEX statistics if
precision matters.**

In-scope clusters: **Ciudad Juárez** (the largest), **Reynosa**, **Matamoros**, **Nuevo Laredo**, **Nogales**, and
**Agua Prieta**. Tijuana and Mexicali, historically two of the biggest, are **out of scope** with Baja.

**The defining characteristic, and the one that matters for collapse: this is definitionally a cross-border
industry.** Inputs in, finished goods out, value added by assembly labor. It has no independent existence.

### 5.2 Monterrey and Nuevo León — Mexico's industrial core

Monterrey anchors **Mexico's second-largest metropolitan area, about 5,347,000 people as of 2026**
([Monterrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monterrey)), and is the country's principal industrial city.

- **CEMEX** — headquartered in San Pedro, near Monterrey; founded 1906. **Fifth-largest cement producer in the world**
  by volume in 2020 at **87.09 million tonnes**, operating in more than 50 countries
  ([Cemex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemex)). Cement is the single most important reconstruction material after
  steel, and the limestone is local.
- **Ternium** — flat and long steel, with **15.4 million tonnes/yr of capacity** group-wide and **55% of 2023 sales
  from Mexico**; its major newer Mexican investment is at **Pesquería, Nuevo León**
  ([Ternium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternium)). This is **electric-arc / scrap-based** steelmaking, structurally
  different from AHMSA's integrated route.
- **Nemak** (Grupo ALFA) — aluminum engine blocks and heads; **Vitro** — glass; **FEMSA** — beverages and retail;
  **Deacero** — long steel.
- **Kia** assembles at Pesquería. **Tesla announced and then paused a Gigafactory at Santa Catarina** (status as of
  2026 unverified — flagged).
- **Saltillo / Ramos Arizpe, Coahuila** is a major automotive cluster — **General Motors, Stellantis, and
  Daimler/Freightliner** all assemble there. **Plant-level capacities unverified this session** — flagged.

### 5.3 Primary steel — the key question, answered honestly

**Is there still integrated (blast-furnace, ore-to-steel) capacity in this territory?** As of my information: **AHMSA
at Monclova is the only integrated mill, and it is shut down and in bankruptcy.** What remains operating is
**electric-arc scrap remelting** — Ternium, Deacero, Grupo Simec. The distinction is decisive for the post-collapse
section: scrap remelting depends on an existing stock of scrap and abundant electricity; integrated ironmaking depends
on ore, coke, and limestone, all of which this territory has in the ground. **The plant is idle but the resource base
and the site are intact.** Author should verify AHMSA's 2026 status.

### 5.4 Hermosillo, aerospace, and Arizona/New Mexico high technology

- **Ford Hermosillo Stamping and Assembly Plant**, Sonora — a full vehicle assembly plant. **I could not retrieve a
  verified profile (models, capacity, employment) this session** — flagged.
- **Aerospace clusters** exist in Chihuahua and in Sonora (Guaymas/Empalme), with employers including Honeywell,
  Safran, and Latécoère. Note that **Mexico's largest aerospace cluster is Querétaro, which is outside this
  territory.** Details unverified this session.
- **Arizona:** **TSMC Arizona** in Phoenix — **US$165 billion pledged**, one of the largest foreign direct investments
  in US manufacturing history, planned as a "gigafab" cluster of **six fabs, two advanced packaging facilities, and an
  R&D center**. The first fab is complete and producing **4 nm** chips as of 2025, with 3 nm targeted for 2027 and
  2 nm for 2029 ([TSMC Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSMC_Arizona)). Also Intel's Chandler fabs, Honeywell
  Aerospace in Phoenix, Raytheon/RTX in Tucson, Boeing in Mesa, Lucid Motors in Casa Grande.
- **New Mexico:** **Sandia National Laboratories** (Albuquerque) and **Los Alamos National Laboratory** — two of the
  largest concentrations of physical-science and engineering capability anywhere in the world, both in-territory, along
  with White Sands Missile Range and Spaceport America.

### 5.5 Cross-border dependency

The analytically important point: an enormous share of this manufacturing base consists of **supply chains that cross
the international border multiple times** before a finished good exists — automotive components especially, under
USMCA rules of origin that are specifically designed to reward North American content accumulated across all three
countries. Maquiladoras constituted about **54% of US–Mexico trade in 2004**, and maquiladora exports were about half
of all Mexican exports by 2005. **I could not obtain a current, authoritative figure for US content embedded in
Mexican manufactured exports this session** — flagged; it is commonly cited at around 40% for maquiladora output, far
higher than for Chinese exports to the US, but the author should source that independently.

In-world, note the reshuffle: **Nogales and Santa Teresa crossings become internal** to Sonora, while **Juárez–El Paso
and Nuevo Laredo–Laredo become international frontiers with the CSA.** Nuevo Laredo, historically the largest land
freight gateway in the hemisphere, is on the wrong side of a new border from its twin.

## 6. Ports, Rail, and Connectivity

### 6.1 Seaports

**Pacific side (Gulf of California):**

- **Guaymas, Sonora** — the territory's principal Pacific port, on the Gulf of California, 340 nautical miles NNW of
  Mazatlán. An **industrial port handling dry bulk cargoes, petroleum products, ammonia, and LPG**, with a cruise
  terminal ([Port of Guaymas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Guaymas)). It is the natural export outlet for
  Sonoran copper concentrate and for Yaqui Valley grain, though **I could not verify current throughput tonnages**.
- **Puerto Peñasco, Sonora** — small, at the head of the Gulf.

**Gulf of Mexico side (Tamaulipas) — confirming the territory is two-ocean:**

- **Altamira** — a major deepwater container and petrochemical port.
- **Tampico** — on the north bank of the Pánuco River about 10 km inland. During Mexico's first oil boom it was the
  **"chief oil-exporting port of the Americas" and the second-busiest in the world**
  ([Tampico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampico)). Metro population about 929,174.
- **Matamoros / Puerto El Mezquital.**
- **Tonnage and TEU figures unverified this session** — flagged.

**Lost to neighbors:** Ensenada (Cascadia), Topolobampo (Sinaloa, outside), and all Texas Gulf ports including Corpus
Christi and Brownsville (CSA).

### 6.2 Rail

**Ferrocarril Chihuahua al Pacífico (ChePe)**, operated by Ferromex — runs **approximately 673 km (418 mi)** from
Chihuahua across the Sierra Madre Occidental through the Copper Canyon to Los Mochis. It includes **37 bridges and 86
tunnels** and reaches about **2,400 m (7,900 ft)** at the continental divide near Divisadero. Conceived under an 1880
concession and built out by the Kansas City, Mexico and Orient Railway from about 1900; Mexico acquired the rights in
1940 ([Chihuahua al Pacífico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chihuahua_al_Pac%C3%ADfico)).

**Strategic note for the author:** the ChePe is the only rail crossing of the Sierra Madre Occidental in this region —
and **its Pacific terminus, Los Mochis/Topolobampo, is in Sinaloa, outside the territory.** So Sonora's one mountain
rail crossing runs *out* of the country at its far end. Guaymas, by contrast, is in-territory but is reached by the
coastal line, not across the sierra.

Other rail: **Ferromex** (Grupo México) operates the northwestern network; **CPKC**, formed by the 2023
Canadian Pacific–Kansas City Southern merger, provides a single-line Mexico–US–Canada route through **Nuevo Laredo**.
US side: the **Union Pacific Sunset Route** (Los Angeles–Tucson–El Paso) and the **BNSF Transcon** across northern
Arizona and New Mexico. Rail border crossings at Nogales, Ciudad Juárez, Piedras Negras, and Nuevo Laredo.

### 6.3 Roads

Mexican Federal Highway **15/15D** is the Pacific corridor from Nogales south through Hermosillo and Ciudad Obregón;
Highways **45** and **57** serve the Chihuahua–Torreón–Saltillo–Monterrey interior. US side: **I-10** (the east–west
spine through Tucson, Phoenix, Las Cruces), **I-25** (Albuquerque–Las Cruces), **I-40** (northern New Mexico/Arizona),
**I-19** (Tucson–Nogales), and **I-8** (Yuma–Casa Grande).

**Water sourcing of the three most exposed US cities — this is the hinge of the whole post-collapse analysis:**

- **Tucson** historically ran almost entirely on **groundwater**; the Santa Cruz River is ephemeral. CAP water is what
  allowed it to stop mining its aquifer. It has **no significant local surface supply.**

## 8. POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

*Everything above is sourced fact. This section is reasoning from those facts, and is the author's to accept, modify,
or discard. Assumption: a civilization-ending nuclear exchange in 2083, followed by a reconstruction era 150–250 years
later, with no functioning global trade and no external supply chains.*

### 8.1 The headline: metals abundance against a hard water ceiling

**This territory is, plausibly, the most mineral-self-sufficient ground in North America.** Not in one commodity — in
the *whole set* an industrializing society needs:

> copper (world-class, at both Morenci and Buenavista) · silver (world-leading refining at Torreón) · gold · lead ·
> zinc · molybdenum (for alloy steel) · **iron ore and metallurgical coal, co-located with an integrated steel site at
> Monclova** · **potash at Carlsbad** (one of the three macronutrients) · limestone for cement · uranium in the Grants
> belt

Very few territories anywhere hold copper *and* iron *and* coking coal *and* potash *and* a functioning polymetallic
refinery site. The nearest comparisons on the continent would each be missing two or three of those.

And against that: **a water budget that is already over-drawn in the real world, on both major rivers, before any
catastrophe.** The Colorado is over-allocated by design and running a ~1.2 maf/yr structural deficit at Lake Mead; the
Rio Grande delivers ~20% of its water to the sea and runs dry in Albuquerque in bad years. **Water, not metal, sets
the population ceiling.** The author's instinct here is exactly right, and the contrast is the most interesting thing
about the territory.

### 8.2 (a) Things that survive regardless — geology, soil, climate

These cannot be destroyed by war or neglect. They are simply there in 2283.

- **Every ore body listed in §1.** Morenci, Buenavista, Ray, Bagdad, Chino, Safford, La Caridad, the Chihuahuan
  silver–lead–zinc veins, the Coahuila iron and coal, the Carlsbad potash beds, the Grants uranium sandstones.
- **A crucial and non-obvious point about *how* they'd be worked.** Modern operations mine ore at **0.16% copper**
  (Morenci) only because diesel, electricity, sulfuric acid, and 400-tonne haul trucks make it economic. A
  reconstruction society cannot do that, and does not need to. It works:
  1. **Near-surface oxide and carbonate ores** — malachite, azurite, chrysocolla, explicitly noted at Bagdad — which
     are smeltable to metallic copper in simple charcoal-fired furnaces, and which is exactly how copper was worked at
     Santa Rita/Chino by Apache, Spanish, and Mexican miners long before 1910.
  2. **The tailings, waste dumps, and spent leach piles**, which are the real inheritance. These are **millions of
     tonnes of rock that has already been drilled, blasted, hauled, and crushed** — the most energy-intensive steps —
     and which are often richer than the ore a pre-industrial miner could otherwise reach. A reconstruction metallurgy
     that re-works 20th-century mine waste is both historically plausible and dramatically interesting.
- **The solar resource.** Permanent, and the best on the continent. Useful even at low technology: solar drying,
  evaporative salt and mineral works, solar distillation of brackish water, and — with any recovered glass and
  copper — modest thermal and photovoltaic capability.
- **The alluvial soils.** The Yaqui and Mayo valleys (~450,000 ha), the Mesilla Valley, the Lagunera alluvium, the
  Tamaulipas coastal plain. Soil does not evaporate.
- **The pre-industrial crop package and the knowledge of it** (§3.6): tepary beans, desert maize landraces, squash,
  mesquite, agave, cholla. **This is the true floor, and it is a much higher floor than a bare desert map implies** —
  it fed settled populations here for a millennium-plus.
- **The Tamaulipas coastal plain's rainfall.** Roughly 900–1,200 mm/yr around Tampico supports **rainfed** agriculture
  with no infrastructure at all. In a world without pumps, this becomes the territory's most reliable farmland — a
  complete inversion of its current secondary status.

### 8.3 (b) Things that depend on infrastructure but could plausibly be repaired

The common thread: **gravity, masonry, and earthworks**. Things that work without a power grid, and whose hard part
(the excavation, the alignment, the dam site) persists even when the machinery doesn't.

- **The gravity dam-and-canal irrigation systems.** This is the most valuable repairable asset in the territory, by a
  wide margin. **Oviáchic, El Novillo, and La Angostura** on the Yaqui; **Mocúzarit** on the Mayo; **La Boquilla** on
  the Conchos; **Elephant Butte and Caballo** on the Rio Grande. These are concrete and masonry gravity structures
  feeding **gravity canals** — no pumping required to move water from reservoir to field. Gates, spillways, and canal
  linings need maintenance, and **siltation is the long-term killer** (Powell loses 33,270 af/yr of capacity; a
  reconstruction society with no way to sluice sediment watches its reservoirs fill over generations). But for one to
  three centuries, these systems irrigate. **DR 041 and DR 038 in the Yaqui and Mayo valleys are the single most
  important thing in the territory for feeding people.**
- **Acequia systems in northern New Mexico.** Arguably belongs in category (a): they are gravity ditches, centuries
  old, community-governed, and were built and maintained without industrial technology in the first place. They
  survive essentially by default.
- **Small hydro at existing dams.** El Novillo (135 MW) and La Boquilla (25 MW) are the sort of scale a modest
  industrial society can eventually rebuild — water wheel to turbine is a solved problem, and the penstocks and dam are
  the expensive parts.
- **The San Juan–Chama tunnel** — a gravity trans-divide diversion. If the tunnel is intact, it keeps delivering.
- **Met-Mex Torreón and the Arizona copper smelters.** Repairable *in principle*, at enormous effort, and worth
  enormous effort — smelting and refining capability is harder to recreate than mining capability. Realistically a
  multi-generational project, and more likely rebuilt small and local than restored at original scale.
- **Monclova's integrated steel.** The *site* is right: ore, coking coal, limestone, water, and rail all converge
  there for good geological reasons. Whether the specific AHMSA blast furnaces survive matters less than the fact that
  **the reason to build a steel town at Monclova is permanent.**
- **Rail alignments, grades, tunnels, and bridges.** The ChePe's 86 tunnels and the Sunset Route's grades represent
  generations of earthmoving. Rail is also the *right* technology for a low-energy society moving ore and grain.
  Rebuilding track on an existing alignment is vastly easier than surveying and cutting a new one.
- **Cement.** Limestone plus fuel plus a kiln. CEMEX's raw material base is local and cement is rebuildable at village
  scale, badly, or at regional scale, well.
- **Lead-acid batteries.** Lead at Torreón, sulfuric acid from local pyrite or sour gas, and a technology invented in
  1859. This is the realistic electrical storage of a reconstruction society, and this territory can make it.

### 8.4 (c) Things that are pure artifacts of global trade and industrial energy — these simply vanish

- **The Central Arizona Project.** 336 miles of canal lifting water some 2,900 feet with pumping plants that need a
  large, reliable grid. **Without it, Phoenix and Tucson lose their Colorado River supply outright.** There is no
  low-technology version of the CAP. This is the single most consequential loss in the territory.
- **Deep groundwater pumping, everywhere.** Turbine pumps, well casing, and electricity. This erases: most of
  Chihuahua's irrigated agriculture, the Comarca Lagunera dairy basin, the Costa de Hermosillo, eastern New Mexico's
  Ogallala circles, and Tucson's historic supply. **Far more of this territory's current agriculture runs on pumped
  groundwater than on gravity surface water, and essentially all of it is lost.**
- **Palo Verde Generating Station.** Needs a fuel cycle, a functioning grid, and **50+ million gallons a day of treated
  municipal effluent from cities that no longer exist at that scale.** It is not a resource in 2283; it is a hazard and
  a ruin — and an unusually poignant one, a reactor complex in open desert that died of its cities drying up.
- **URENCO's Eunice enrichment plant.** Cascade of precision centrifuges. Gone, unrecoverable.
- **TSMC Arizona, Intel Chandler.** Semiconductor fabs are the most supply-chain-dependent objects humans build. Gone
  absolutely, and not rebuildable at any plausible reconstruction technology level. Ruins with very clean rooms.
- **The entire maquiladora belt.** Definitionally cross-border assembly of imported inputs. It has no domestic
  existence to fall back on. Juárez, Reynosa, Matamoros, and Nogales lose their economic reason for existing on the day
  the border trade stops.
- **The New Mexico Permian at anything like 2.2 million barrels/day.** This is the trap in the energy statistics.
  That production is horizontal drilling with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing, consuming enormous volumes of water,
  proppant sand, specialty steel, and diesel. **It is an artifact of an industrial system, not a resource a
  reconstruction society can tap at that rate.** What survives is modest: shallow conventional oil, seeps, San Juan
  Basin shallow gas, and — more usefully — the *refining* knowledge and some salvageable tankage. Treat the territory
  as **having oil, but not having the oil industry.**
- **Export winter vegetables.** Yuma's 90% share of US winter leafy greens depends on refrigerated trucking to distant
  markets. The *land and climate* survive (category a) and gravity Colorado diversion at Yuma survives (category b),
  but the crop mix reverts from lettuce-for-export to grain, beans, and oilseed for local consumption.
- **Modern low-grade copper metallurgy.** Solvent extraction/electrowinning, imported sulfuric acid at scale, giant
  haul fleets, flotation reagents. Gone — replaced by the far smaller-scale work described in §8.2.
- **Aluminum.** No bauxite, and smelting needs enormous electricity. Recycled aluminum stock only, then gone.
- **Veterinary and crop chemical inputs** — screwworm control, modern pesticides, synthetic nitrogen at scale. Livestock
  and crop losses rise substantially and permanently.

### 8.5 Carrying capacity and which cities depopulate

**Blunt version: the territory's population center of gravity moves decisively south and east.** The Arizona desert
metros are the most purely artificial large settlements in the entire territory, and they take the worst of it.

**Catastrophic depopulation:**

- **Tucson (1.08M)** — the most exposed large city in the territory. No meaningful local surface water; the Santa Cruz
  is ephemeral; it ran on groundwater until CAP and would have to run on groundwater again, with no pumps. Likely falls
  to a **small fraction of its present size** — a town on a seasonal river, at the tens-of-thousands scale at most.
- **Hermosillo (936k)** — every one of its water sources is pumped or piped: an overdrafted, salt-intruded coastal
  aquifer, a small and unreliable Río Sonora, and the Independencia aqueduct lifting water out of the Yaqui basin.
  Severe collapse.
- **Ciudad Juárez (~2.5M)** — loses its entire maquiladora economy at a stroke, and sits on the most depleted reach of
  the Rio Grande, dependent on releases from Elephant Butte far upstream. Severe collapse. (In-world it does retain one
  advantage: those upstream releases are now *domestic*, controlled from New Mexico rather than negotiated across a
  border.)
- **Chihuahua City (988k)** and the **Comarca Lagunera / Torreón (720k+)** — both groundwater-dependent, the Lagunera
  additionally endorheic and arsenic-burdened. Severe collapse, though **Torreón's Met-Mex complex is a reason for
  *somebody* to stay** — a smaller metallurgical town persisting on a reduced water base is very plausible.
- **Reynosa, Matamoros, Nuevo Laredo** — border assembly towns severed from their twins. Reynosa and Matamoros at least
  sit on the lower Rio Grande and a wetter coastal plain, so they decline rather than vanish. Nuevo Laredo is the most
  exposed of the three.

**Partial survival, substantially reduced:**

- **Phoenix (5.19M)** — loses CAP entirely, and with it most of its water. **But it does not go to zero**, and the
  reason is specific: the **Salt River Project**. The Salt and Verde rivers are real, mountain-fed, and impounded
  behind gravity dams, and the Salt River Valley supported the **Hohokam canal system** — one of the largest
  pre-industrial irrigation networks in North America — for roughly a thousand years, on exactly this water, with no
  technology beyond hand tools. **Phoenix reverts to being what it physically is: a good irrigated valley of modest
  size.** Something in the low hundreds of thousands, concentrated along the Salt, is defensible. Five million is not.
  The literal Phoenix-from-ashes resonance is there for the author to use or ignore.
- **Albuquerque (~560k)** — has an actual river and possibly an intact gravity tunnel. Declines, but persists as a
  real river town, and **Sandia and Los Alamos make the middle Rio Grande the most plausible location in the entire
  territory for surviving technical knowledge**, in the form of libraries, instruments, and institutional memory, if
  anything survives at all.
- **Nogales** — small, on an internal border that no longer divides anything, astride the one good rail-and-road
  corridor between the Sonoran coast and the Arizona uplands. Persists at modest scale as a transit town.

**Winners — where people actually end up:**

- **The Yaqui and Mayo valleys / Ciudad Obregón.** 450,000 hectares of alluvium under **gravity irrigation** from
  Oviáchic, with the best wheat-growing conditions and the best wheat germplasm in the hemisphere. **This is the
  breadbasket and it is not close.** Expect the population, and very likely the political center, to concentrate here.
- **The Monterrey–Saltillo corridor.** The best-positioned large urban area: real rainfall at the Sierra Madre
  Oriental front, reservoir supply, and — uniquely — **iron, coal, limestone, cement, and steelmaking all within
  reach.** Monterrey will not hold 5.3 million without pumped supply, but it holds the most of any major city and it
  holds the industry.
- **The Tamaulipas coastal plain — Tampico, Altamira, and the lower valleys.** Subtropical, **rainfed**, on the Pánuco
  River, with Gulf fisheries and deepwater port sites. In a world without irrigation infrastructure, this becomes
  disproportionately important relative to its current standing, and it is the territory's Atlantic-facing window.
- **The Mesilla Valley / Las Cruces and Hatch**, on gravity Rio Grande Project water below Elephant Butte.
- **Yuma**, small but well-sited: at the river, at low elevation, with superb soil and the option of gravity diversion.

### 8.6 Summary judgment for the author

**La República de Sonora is a territory that can make almost anything and feed comparatively few.**

It can smelt copper, refine silver and lead, alloy steel with its own molybdenum, mine its own iron and coking coal,
fire its own cement, dig its own potassium fertilizer, and — given a century or two — rebuild a genuinely
self-sufficient metal-working industrial base without importing a single ton of ore. In a post-collapse North America
it would be the continent's **foundry and its mint**, and the obvious trading partner for every neighbor that has grain
but no metal.

What it cannot do is water itself at anything like its 2026 population. Strip out the pumps, and roughly **35–40
million people fall to something on the order of a few million**, concentrated in four places: the Yaqui/Mayo valleys,
the Monterrey–Saltillo corridor, the Tamaulipas coast, and a chain of much smaller gravity-irrigated river towns along
the Rio Grande and the lower Colorado. The great desert metros of Arizona — the most spectacular and most artificial
settlements in the whole territory — become the largest ruins on the continent, with Phoenix surviving in miniature on
the same Salt River water the Hohokam used, and Tucson very nearly not surviving at all.

**The dramatic shape of this, if useful:** a nation that is rich in exactly the way that lets it *build* and poor in
exactly the way that limits how many people it can *be*. Metal without water. Its politics would organize around
irrigation districts and smelter towns, and its single most valuable physical possession would not be a mine at all —
it would be **the Oviáchic dam and the canal network below it.**
