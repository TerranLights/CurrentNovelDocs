# Maritime Southeast Asia — Material and Economic Base

2026-09-26. **Survey only.** What **Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore and Timor-Leste**
physically hold, and what survives a collapse.

## 0. The organizing fact — the water is the road, and one strait is most of it

Mainland Southeast Asia's organizing fact is a river controlled from outside its borders. **This region's
organizing fact is the opposite kind of problem: the connective tissue isn't a river at all, it's open water,
and almost all of world trade that needs to cross this region funnels through one 2.8-km-wide channel.**

**The Strait of Malacca**, between Peninsular Malaysia/Singapore and Sumatra, carries an estimated **22–30% of
all world trade** and **roughly 94,000 vessel transits a year**. On the oil side specifically, roughly **23.2
million barrels a day** moved through it in the first half of 2025 — about **29% of global maritime oil
trade** — some 85–90% of it crude, most of it bound for China, Japan and South Korea. **China alone routes
about 80% of its oil imports through this one strait**, which is 60% of its entire oil supply — the real
substance behind the "Malacca dilemma" the strait's own Wikipedia page is named for.

**Singapore's entire strategic position is downstream of this one fact.** The city-state has no oil of its
own, no domestic agriculture worth naming, and a landmass smaller than New York City — and is nonetheless the
world's **second-busiest container port** (44.66 million TEU in 2025, behind only Shanghai) and the operator of
**the world's largest bunkering port** (54.92 million tonnes of bunker sales in 2024) and one of the world's
top three oil-refining centers (Jurong Island, 1.5 million barrels/day capacity across three refineries, no
domestic crude feedstock at all). Singapore is not wealthy because of what it has; it is wealthy because of
where it sits on this one channel, processing and re-exporting what passes through.

**There are alternatives to Malacca, and they are all worse.** The **Sunda Strait** (Java/Sumatra) is shallow
(as little as 20 m in places) and unsuitable for large modern ships; the **Lombok and Makassar Straits**
(Bali/Lombok, then north past Sulawesi) are deep enough for supertankers over 100,000 DWT and are the standard
fallback, but add **roughly 1,086 nautical miles (Sunda) or 2,488 nautical miles (Lombok)** to a China-bound
voyage compared to Malacca, at an estimated aggregate cost of **$84–220 billion a year** if the region's
shipping had to route around Malacca permanently. **This is the region's single point of failure and its
single greatest post-collapse asset simultaneously** — whoever can keep any one of these channels open and
policed controls the terms on which the rest of the world reaches or leaves this region at all.

**Contrast with the mainland file, stated once:** that region's states are colonial lines drawn over
continuous precolonial polities river valleys and mandalas. **This region's states — with one partial
exception, Brunei — are colonial administrative units adopted whole at independence** (*uti possidetis
juris*, per the frameworks survey §1.5), and its connective tissue is sea lanes and straits rather than roads,
rail, and river navigation. Every transport and industry finding below follows from that difference.

**Java is the one number that matters more than the rest of this table combined.** Restated from the
frameworks survey (§2.1): **roughly 158 million people — about 56% of Indonesia's entire population — live on
Java, which is about 7% of the country's land area.** Every other measured population concentration in this
region is an order of magnitude smaller in relative terms. This is not a border-crossing discontinuity like
Isan or the Khmer Krom on the mainland map; it's a single country whose economic and demographic center of
gravity sits almost entirely on one island, with Jakarta (colonial Batavia, national capital since
independence) inside that same 7%. Anything that happens to Java's food supply, port infrastructure, or
internal transport happens to more than half of Indonesia at once.

**Manila (Metro Manila) and the Philippines' own uneven distribution** is the region's second such case, though
less extreme than Java's: the National Capital Region holds roughly 13 million people on a small fraction of
the country's land, with the rest of the population spread across the Visayas and Mindanao far more evenly
than Indonesia's population is spread across its outer islands.

**Malaysia's population splits roughly 20:1 in favor of the Peninsula over East Malaysia** (Sabah + Sarawak
together hold a small fraction of the national population despite being roughly 60% of Malaysia's land area
and, per the frameworks survey §3.3, most of its gas reserves) — the demographic mirror of the MA63 revenue
grievance: the states with the resources have far fewer people and far less federal political weight than
their material contribution would suggest.

## 2. Minerals and energy

### 2.1 Nickel — Indonesia's near-monopoly

**Indonesia holds the world's largest nickel reserves (an estimated 55–62 million tonnes) and produced roughly
2.6 million metric tons in 2025 — about 67% of world output**, up from an already-dominant position after a
**2020 export ban on unprocessed ore** forced processing onshore rather than letting the raw material leave
the country. This is the single most consequential mineral fact in this survey: one country supplies two-
thirds of the world's supply of the metal at the center of the EV-battery supply chain, and it got there by
deliberate policy, not accident of geology alone.

**The Philippines is a distant but real second**, at roughly 270,000 metric tons in 2025 (down 24% from 2024
on low prices) — sixth in reserves globally, still a meaningful supplier, but an order of magnitude behind
Indonesia.

### 2.2 Copper and gold — Grasberg

**The Grasberg minerals district in Central Papua** (Freeport-McMoRan 48.8% / Indonesia's Inalum 51.2%) is one
of the world's largest copper-and-gold deposits, ranked **second in the world for copper production in 2024**,
with reserves described as among the largest recoverable copper-gold reserves on Earth. A 2025-completed
smelter/refinery at Gresik makes PTFI a fully integrated producer. **Already flagged in the frameworks survey
(§3.1.3) as the material stake behind Indonesia's reluctance to loosen its hold on West Papua** — the frameworks
survey's own figure, $1.94 billion projected revenue in 2022, is the number worth carrying forward. This is
this region's version of the Guri Dam/Amazonia tension already established for Venezuela in the Latin America
survey: a resource-rich, restive periphery the core has overwhelming material reason not to let go.

### 2.3 Coal — Indonesia, again

**Indonesia is the world's largest coal exporter by volume** (~555 million tonnes in 2025, ~19.6% of global
export value, second only to Australia by value) and consistently ranks among the top five producers globally
(~357.6 million tonnes in the first half of 2025 alone). Coal was Indonesia's single largest export product by
value in 2025 (US$24.48 billion), with China the largest buyer.

### 2.4 Tin — the Southeast Asian tin belt's southern anchor

The mainland survey's §2.1 already documents the Southeast Asian tin belt running through Myanmar and
Thailand. **Indonesia is that belt's other major node and its largest single exporter**: the **Bangka-
Belitung Islands** alone produce roughly 90% of Indonesia's national tin output and, on their own, an
estimated **17–23.5% of world tin production** (figures vary by source and year; Indonesia's national share is
commonly cited at 17–28% over the last decade, with Indonesia the **world's second-largest producer and
largest exporter** of tin overall, ~32% of the international tin trade as of 2020, $1.11 billion). **This
closes the loop the mainland survey left open**: the tin belt does not stop at the mainland's southern edge —
it continues into island Southeast Asia, and Indonesia's contribution is, by several of these measures, larger
than the mainland countries' combined.

### 2.5 Oil and gas — three very different scales

- **Brunei**: small in absolute terms (1.1 billion barrels of oil reserves, 7.9 Tcf of gas as of 2020;
  ~109,725 barrels/day of oil and petroleum liquids in 2025) but **decisive for its own economy** — roughly
  **60% of GDP comes from oil and gas exports**, and Brunei Shell Petroleum alone accounts for ~90% of the
  country's oil and gas revenue. This is the material fact behind the frameworks survey's own reading of
  Brunei as the one polity in the region whose modern borders are a shrinkage of a real precolonial state
  rather than a colonial administrative line adopted wholesale — a small, wealthy, defensible rump that never
  needed the 1963 federation's promises the way Sabah and Sarawak did.
- **Malaysia**: the region's largest conventional reserve base — **2.7 billion barrels of oil (second-largest
  in Southeast Asia) and 32 Tcf of gas** as of 2023, with **Sarawak alone holding over 60% of the country's gas
  reserves**, exported substantially as LNG through the Bintulu complex, one of the world's largest LNG export
  facilities. **This is the material substance behind the MA63 grievance already documented in the frameworks
  survey**: Sarawak and Sabah hold the resource, Petronas (a federal, national company since a 1974 law gave it
  exclusive national extraction rights) controls it, and **both states receive a 5% oil royalty** that both
  states' own politics describe as inadequate against their material contribution — Sarawak's separate,
  genuine win was a **5% state sales tax on petroleum products**, not a higher royalty rate (⚠️ **corrected
  2026-09-26**: this survey previously stated Sarawak had "separately renegotiated up to 20%" in royalties;
  20% is what both states have been demanding, not something either receives).
- **Indonesia**: the **East Natuna gas field**, in the South China Sea north of the Natuna Islands, holds an
  estimated 210–222 Tcf of gas, of which roughly 46 Tcf is considered commercially recoverable — **about a
  quarter of Indonesia's total recoverable gas reserves in one field**, and squarely inside China's
  self-declared "nine-dash line" claim despite sitting within Indonesia's own EEZ under UNCLOS (China formally
  renounced any claim to the Natuna Islands themselves in 1995, but has not renounced the surrounding waters).
  Indonesia has continued exploration and development here over repeated Chinese protests. **This is the
  region's clearest South China Sea flashpoint that is also a hard material fact**, not just a diplomatic one —
  named in the frameworks survey's Part 4 as deliberately out of scope for border-drawing purposes, but load-
  bearing for any story about resource access or great-power pressure on Indonesia specifically.

### 2.6 Geothermal — the region's two best-positioned countries

**Indonesia (2,742 MW, the fastest-growing large geothermal market globally in 2025) and the Philippines
(2,034 MW) rank second and third in the world** for installed geothermal capacity, behind only the United
States. Both sit on the Pacific Ring of Fire, and both have room to grow: **Indonesia's own estimated
geothermal potential is commonly cited in the range of 24 GW-plus, of which only a small fraction is currently
developed.** ⚠️ *A precise, sourced figure for Indonesia's total geothermal resource potential (as opposed to
installed capacity) was not verified this session — treat any figure beyond the installed-capacity numbers
above as indicative, not confirmed.* Unlike coal, gas, or nickel, geothermal capacity is **domestic, renewable,
and does not depend on an export market or an import supply chain to keep running** — a genuinely different
category from everything else in this section for post-collapse purposes (§6).

## 3. Agriculture and food security

### 3.1 Palm oil — the region's dominant, and uniquely infrastructure-dependent, export crop

**Indonesia and Malaysia together produce roughly 85% of the world's palm oil and generated four-fifths
(80.6%) of the value of all palm oil exported in 2025.** Indonesia alone: ~54–60% of global supply, $24.4
billion in exports in 2025 (50.1% of world palm oil export value). Malaysia: the world's second-largest
producer, RM112.43 billion (~$24-25bn) in palm-oil-and-derivatives export earnings in 2025.

**Why this matters for a post-collapse scenario, stated plainly: palm oil is not a subsistence crop.** Unlike
rice or fish, it requires industrial-scale plantation management, centralized milling (fresh fruit bunches
must be processed within 24–48 hours of harvest or the oil degrades), and a functioning export logistics chain
to realize any of its value — a smallholder or a village cannot simply eat or barter raw palm fruit the way a
Missouri farm family can eat their own corn or a Bugis trader can carry rice by pinisi. **This makes palm oil
this region's single most infrastructure-fragile major export**, the maritime equivalent of the mainland
survey's ethanol/feedlot complex: a real, large, currently-dominant economic activity that depends entirely on
inputs and logistics a collapse would sever, rather than reverting to a smaller subsistence-scale version of
itself the way rice, fish, or coconut can.

### 3.2 Rice — a genuine split within the region, unlike the mainland's uniform surplus

The mainland survey's own §1 headline is that mainland Southeast Asia is "the one thing the region has in
surplus" — Thailand and Vietnam are both major rice exporters. **This region does not share that uniform
picture.** **The Philippines is a structural rice-deficit country**: self-sufficiency rose to 78.1% in 2025
(from 71.7% in 2024, helped by a deliberate four-month import ban), with a national target of 84% by end-2026
and full self-sufficiency not projected before 2027 at the earliest — rice import dependency has climbed from
roughly 7.2% of consumption in 2000 to nearly 14% today, and rice/rice products still make up about 72% of
Filipino cereal consumption. **Indonesia and Malaysia both pursue rice self-sufficiency policy goals with
mixed, incompletely verified results** ⚠️ *(a precise, current, sourced self-sufficiency percentage for
Indonesia specifically was not obtained this session — flagged as an open item, see §8)*. **This is a genuine,
documented asymmetry inside the region that the mainland region does not have an equivalent of**: a
post-collapse Philippines starts from a real caloric deficit in its own staple crop, in a way none of the five
mainland countries do.

### 3.3 Fisheries — the region's true subsistence backbone

**Indonesia is the world's second-largest seafood producer**, with total fish production of roughly 22 million
metric tons in 2022 (about 47.1% of all Southeast Asian fishery production by volume) and roughly 3.64 million
tonnes of aquaculture output (second-largest aquaculture producer in the world, 2014 figures), the large
majority of it freshwater culture. **The Philippines ranks fourth in the region** (about 9.2% of regional
fishery volume, 7.5% of value). **This is the region's actual subsistence food-security backbone in a way
palm oil and even rice are not** — an artisanal or small-motorized fishing boat, unlike a palm-oil mill or a
mechanized rice combine, keeps working with minimal industrial input, and rice-fish co-culture is already a
long-standing traditional practice across both Indonesia and the Philippines that blends the two food sources
rather than treating them as separate systems.

## 4. Industry and manufacturing

**Singapore, Malaysia and the Philippines each hold one genuinely distinct high-value industrial niche; Brunei
and Timor-Leste have essentially none beyond hydrocarbons; Indonesia's industrial base is far smaller than its
population and resource base would suggest.**

- **Singapore**: finance, the port (§0), and petrochemical refining (Jurong Island, one of the world's top
  three refining centers, 1.5 million barrels/day capacity, ~100 co-located chemical companies, zero domestic
  crude of its own). A pure processing-and-transshipment economy in the most extreme form in this survey —
  everything physical that makes Singapore rich arrives, gets refined/repackaged/re-exported, and leaves again.
- **Malaysia**: electronics and semiconductors, concentrated at **Penang** ("the Silicon Valley of the East"),
  which alone generates **60% of the nation's total electronics/electrical exports**; Malaysia's E&E sector as
  a whole was ~RM711.6 billion (44.3% of total exports) in 2025, making it the **world's sixth-largest
  semiconductor exporter**.
- **Philippines**: two distinct high-value sectors, both services/knowledge-intensive rather than
  resource-based — **electronics exports** (~$27.78 billion in 2025, 57% of total Philippine exports;
  semiconductors and electronic components specifically at ~$40.29 billion) and the **BPO/IT-BPM sector**
  (~$40 billion in export revenue in 2025, ~1.9 million jobs, over 8% of GDP, increasingly higher-value work —
  software development, finance/accounting, design — rather than only call centers).
- **Indonesia**: by far the region's largest population and resource base, but **the smallest industrial
  complexity relative to its size of anything in this survey** — ranked roughly 68th–69th in the world by
  Economic Complexity Index, well behind Singapore (2.52, among the world's highest) and even behind Malaysia
  (0.971, 25th) and the Philippines (43rd most complex, ECI improving over the last decade). Indonesia's
  economic weight comes overwhelmingly from raw and semi-processed resource exports (nickel, coal, palm oil,
  tin) rather than manufactured/knowledge-intensive goods — the mirror image of Singapore, which has almost no
  resources of its own and the region's highest complexity score.
- **Brunei and Timor-Leste**: essentially no manufacturing base independent of hydrocarbons. Brunei's ~60%
  GDP-from-oil-and-gas figure (§2.5) and Timor-Leste's near-total historical dependence on the Bayu-Undan field
  (below) are the same underlying condition at two very different absolute scales.

**⚠️ ECI ordering, stated for consistency with the mainland survey's own convention** (which gave a
Thailand > Vietnam > Cambodia ≈ Laos > Myanmar ranking): the clearest, best-sourced figures obtained this
session are **Singapore (2.52) > Malaysia (0.971) > Philippines (43rd globally, improving) > Indonesia
(68th–69th globally)**. Brunei's and Timor-Leste's ECI scores were not obtained this session (both are
small, resource-dependent economies likely to rank low on any complexity measure, but this is inference, not
a sourced figure — flagged in §8).

## 5. Transport and connectivity

**Restated from §0, because it is the section this survey structurally replaces from the mainland file**: that
survey organized transport around rail hubs, river navigation, and lock/dam infrastructure. **None of that
applies here.** The load-bearing transport facts in this region are strait chokepoints, port infrastructure,
and inter-island shipping — sea lanes, not rail alignments.

- **The Strait of Malacca and its alternates** (§0) are the single most important transport fact in the whole
  survey, more important than any individual port or mineral.
- **Singapore's port** (§0, §4) is simultaneously a transport fact and an industrial one — the two cannot be
  separated the way, say, Kansas City's rail-hub status can be separated from Missouri's cattle industry on the
  mainland map.
- **Indonesia's inter-island connectivity is fundamentally different in kind from continental rail or river
  transport, and this is the single most important post-collapse-relevant transport finding in this survey**:
  the traditional **Bugis pinisi** — a wooden schooner built from *kayu ulin* (ironwood) using techniques
  centuries old, sailing out of harbors like Sunda Kelapa (Jakarta) and Paotere (Makassar) — is **not a museum
  piece or a heritage-tourism relic. It is a live, commercially operating inter-island freight system today**,
  carrying cement, rice, sugar, timber, motorcycles, and general cargo between islands the modern container
  network doesn't reach economically. **This means Indonesia already possesses, in active use, a
  pre-industrial-technology fallback for its own basic connectivity that no mainland country's rail network has
  an equivalent of** — Missouri's rail grade survives as an alignment a recovering society could rebuild onto;
  Indonesia's inter-island shipping tradition survives as an actual functioning fleet, right now, that never
  stopped operating in parallel with the modern one.
- **The Philippines' own inter-island ferry and shipping network** plays a structurally similar role
  archipelago-wide, though this survey did not research it to the same depth as the Indonesian case this
  session — ⚠️ flagged as an item worth a closer look in a future pass (§8).

## 6. Post-collapse assessment

Matching the mainland survey's three-tier structure (§3 there), adapted for what actually differs here.

### Survives regardless

- **Fisheries** (§3.3) — artisanal and small-motorized fishing needs minimal industrial input and is already
  the region's true subsistence backbone.
- **The pinisi/inter-island sailing trade** (§5) — already operating today, alongside the modern system, not
  merely a repairable memory of one.
- **Rice-fish co-culture** — a genuinely low-input, traditional, currently-practiced system in both Indonesia
  and the Philippines.
- **Geothermal generation** (§2.6), once built — the Ring of Fire's heat itself is not going anywhere, and
  geothermal plants, once installed, need no imported fuel to keep running (though turbine/generator
  maintenance is a repairable-infrastructure question, next tier).
- **Coconut, and small-scale subsistence agriculture generally** across the archipelago — not researched in
  the same depth as palm oil or rice this session (⚠️ see §8) but structurally similar to rice in being
  smallholder-compatible in a way palm oil is not.

### Repairable with effort

- **Any one strait chokepoint** (§0) — keeping Malacca, or a fallback like Lombok/Makassar, open, charted, and
  policed is a governance and maintenance question, not an industrial-civilization-only one; premodern and
  early-modern polities (Srivijaya, per the frameworks survey, existed *specifically* to do this) managed
  exactly this task for centuries before modern shipping existed.
- **Singapore's port and refining complex**, at a much reduced scale — the physical infrastructure (deepwater
  berths, the refinery sites themselves) persists far longer than the throughput volume that currently
  justifies it; a collapse plausibly leaves Singapore with a dramatically smaller but still real
  entrepôt/refining role, the same "the grade is permanent, the operation is not" logic the mainland survey
  applied to rail.
- **Basic inter-island container/ferry shipping** below modern volumes, using surviving vessels and fuel
  stocks until those run out, then reverting toward the pinisi-style baseline that already coexists with it.
- **Grasberg and the Bangka-Belitung tin fields**, at artisanal/small-mine scale — both metals have been worked
  at pre-industrial technology historically elsewhere in the world (the mainland survey's own Missouri lead
  district is the direct analogue), though Grasberg's specific ore body and current infrastructure are far more
  industrial-scale-dependent than a shallow alluvial tin deposit.

### Gone for good

- **Palm oil as an export commodity at anything like its current volume** (§3.1) — the milling-within-hours
  requirement and the export logistics chain are both industrial-civilization artifacts; the crop itself (the
  oil palms) would keep growing, but the economic activity built on turning it into a globally-traded commodity
  would not survive a serious logistics collapse.
- **LNG export infrastructure** (Bintulu, and any Indonesian equivalent) — cryogenic liquefaction and
  specialized LNG carriers are about as industrial-civilization-dependent as an economic activity gets.
- **The BPO/IT-BPM sector and Penang's semiconductor fabrication** (§4) — both depend entirely on global
  telecommunications infrastructure and international capital/demand that a regional collapse severs outright;
  neither reverts to a smaller local version the way palm oil at least theoretically could, because there is no
  local market for either service at any scale.
- **Brunei's and Timor-Leste's entire fiscal models** — both are built on hydrocarbon export revenue funding a
  state budget (Brunei directly; Timor-Leste via its Petroleum Fund, see below) rather than on a real domestic
  production base broad enough to tax. Removing the export market removes the government's actual income, not
  merely one industry among several.

### ⚠️ Timor-Leste's specific case, flagged separately because it is already happening in the real world

**Timor-Leste's Bayu-Undan gas field — the country's original post-independence revenue source, contributing
an estimated $25 billion in total revenue since exports began in 2004 — ceased production in mid-2025.** The
country now depends almost entirely on withdrawals from its Petroleum Fund (~$18.95 billion as of 2024, funding
over 70% of the government budget) while it tries to bring the much larger **Greater Sunrise** field (gas worth
an estimated $50 billion-plus in future export revenue, more than the entire current Petroleum Fund) into
production, with ASEAN investors formally invited to participate as of late 2025. **This is not a hypothetical
post-collapse scenario for Timor-Leste — it is the country's actual current fiscal position**: a
petroleum-dependent micro-state between a depleted field and an undeveloped one, drawing down savings, with
coffee, vanilla, coconut, and remittances explicitly named by its own government as the (much smaller)
alternative to petroleum revenue. Any post-collapse story involving Timor-Leste should treat this as the
starting condition, not an invented crisis.

## 7. What this means for the nations layer

Not this survey's job to decide (per METHOD's rule 6 and the frameworks survey's own framing), but worth
naming as considerations for whoever does that work:

- **Java's population concentration (§1) is the single fact most likely to determine whether Indonesia's
  post-collapse internal-division scheme (frameworks survey §3.1.5) produces a stable set of blocs or an
  unstable one** — a Java bloc holding 56% of the population against six other blocs holding the remaining
  44% combined is a structurally different proposition than the mainland's five roughly comparable countries.
- **The Strait of Malacca (§0) is a resource no single one of these six polities fully controls alone** —
  Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia all border it. Any post-collapse political map of this region should treat
  control of, or access to, this strait as being to this region roughly what control of the Mekong's flow is to
  the mainland: the resource whose distribution among neighbors matters more than almost anything else on the
  map.
- **Sabah and Sarawak's gas wealth relative to Peninsular Malaysia (§2.5) is a genuine, quantified,
  currently-live grievance** that the frameworks survey already flagged as this region's Isan-equivalent — this
  survey's material findings (a 5% royalty for both states against 60%+ of national gas reserves concentrated
  in one of them, plus Sarawak's separately-won 5% state sales tax on petroleum products) make that case
  sharper, not softer.
- **Grasberg (§2.2) does for West Papua exactly what the Guri Dam does for Bolívar in the Latin America
  survey**: gives the core an overwhelming material reason not to let a culturally-distinct periphery go,
  independent of any question of ethnic or historical right.
- **⚠️ Added 2026-09-26, directly relevant to the now-reopened Indonesia partition question**: Java's north
  coast is subsiding faster than the sea is rising (§9), on a trajectory that makes its current 56%-of-
  population dominance materially less durable over the timescales this project's post-collapse setting
  operates on — real infrastructure (sea walls, pumps) that already barely holds the water back today would be
  among the first things to fail without maintenance. That's a genuine structural argument for why a unified
  successor state's center of gravity might not stay Java-centered indefinitely, independent of any of the
  ethnic/political separatism arguments already on the table for Aceh, West Papua and Maluku (frameworks
  survey §3.1) — worth weighing alongside them, not instead of them, when that research resumes.

## 9. Land subsidence and coastal flooding (added 2026-09-26, author-requested)

**The headline finding is not primarily about climate change.** Java's north coast — Jakarta, Pekalongan,
Semarang, Demak — is subsiding at rates up to **1.5 meters per decade**, roughly **9x faster** than global
sea-level rise (~3.6mm/year). The dominant cause is **local, anthropogenic groundwater extraction** for a huge
coastal population, plus soil compaction and (at Sidoarjo) gas extraction — not the rising sea itself, which
is a real but comparatively minor contributor. **Land subsidence could account for up to 85% of relative
sea-level rise along much of Java's coastline by 2050**, and more than 75% of that coastline is projected to
be subsidence-dominated within 25 years.

**Current, not hypothetical.** North Jakarta has already subsided up to 4 meters over four decades; roughly
40% of it now sits permanently below sea level, with the worst-hit neighborhoods still sinking up to 25cm a
year. Entire North Java suburbs flood several times a month. **Pekalongan is separately flagged as likely to
be the first place in Indonesia — possibly the world — to sink rapidly, within the next decade.**

**This is why Nusantara is being built.** Indonesia's real, currently-under-construction relocation of its
capital to East Kalimantan (already noted in §1 and on the map as a "planned future capital" marker) is
explicitly driven by Jakarta's subsidence crisis, not a separate, unrelated infrastructure project. **Real
specifics, added 2026-09-26**: sited 0°58′23″S 116°42′31″E, ~30km northwest of Balikpapan, 2,562 km² total
(256,142 ha land + 68,188 ha water). Construction started 2022; the core government district (Garuda Palace,
legislative buildings) is near-complete, with ~4,000 civil servants relocating in 2026. Designed for up to
1.5 million residents; actual 2025 population is only 147,430. **Funding has collapsed 85%** (from $2B in
2024 to $300M for 2026), and Indonesia has already downgraded its own ambitions: Nusantara is now slated to
become the "political capital" only, by 2028, with **Jakarta explicitly retaining economic dominance** — a
real, current, unresolved split-capital arrangement, not a clean full relocation. Full in-world projection of
what this might mean for a post-collapse setting is in the map-data package's own
`DESIGNATIONS - What Each Region on the Map Actually Is.md`.

**Beyond Java — a real, separate exposure pattern that touches three of the map's blocs directly:**
**24 islands have already disappeared off the coasts of Aceh, North Sumatra, Riau, and Papua**; two more
vanished off South Sumatra, an area sitting under 3m elevation. Indonesia overall holds roughly **42 million
people in coastal zones below 10m elevation** (5th-highest global exposure) and is projected to lose **~2,000
small islands by 2050**, with up to **5.9 million people affected by annual coastal flooding by 2100**.
**Kalimantan's own exposure was not established this pass** — a real gap, not a clean bill of health, and
worth checking given it's about to host the new capital.
