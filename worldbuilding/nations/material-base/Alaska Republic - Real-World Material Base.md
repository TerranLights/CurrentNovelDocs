## Territory Scope

Per `../../worldbuilding/nations/New North America - Territory Definitions (read from map).md`, the Alaska
Republic covers:

- **Alaska — but NOT the Panhandle**
- **Yukon**
- **Northwest Territories**
- **Nunavut** and the Arctic Archipelago

**The Panhandle exclusion is load-bearing and is applied throughout this file.** The coastal strip and
Alexander Archipelago — Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan, Petersburg, Skagway, Yakutat — belong to **Cascadia**.
Everything below marked **⚑ CASCADIAN** is inside the territory researched but outside this nation, and is
listed only so the boundary is unambiguous and so the loss is visible.

**Consequences of the Panhandle exclusion**, which are severe and worth stating up front:

1. **The Alaska Republic has no Inside Passage.** The entire protected coastal shipping route is Cascadian.
   Remaining maritime access: the Gulf of Alaska (Anchorage, Valdez, Seward), the Bering Sea, and the Arctic.
2. **It loses Greens Creek and Kensington** — the largest primary silver mine in the United States, and a
   producing gold mine.
3. **It loses the best-documented limestone in the territory** (Prince of Wales Island karst).
4. **The White Pass and Yukon Route railway becomes an international line.** Skagway is Cascadian; Whitehorse
   is Alaskan. Any Yukon route to a Pacific port crosses a frontier.

**The Northwest Territories and Nunavut** — the Mackenzie River system, Great Bear and Great Slave Lakes, the
NWT diamond fields (Ekati, Diavik, Gahcho Kué), Norman Wells oil, Baffin Island iron (Mary River), and the
Northwest Passage — are **covered in the companion supplement**, not here. For a territory this size that is
not a detail; it is most of the landmass.

# PART 1 — MINERALS AND MINING

## The Best Single Source

**Alaska DGGS Special Report 78** (Szumigala, 2026), *Critical Minerals, Strategic Minerals and Minerals in
Alaska*, 86 pp., January 2026 — a report to the Alaska Legislature mandated by SB118 (2024).
https://dggs.alaska.gov/webpubs/dggs/sr/text/sr078.pdf · DOI: https://doi.org/10.14509/31965

Its **Appendix E** is a state-compiled resource table covering essentially every significant Alaska deposit.
It is a scanned image table and does not appear in text extraction; most resource figures below come from it.

**Unit caution:** Alaska state sources use **short tons**; Teck, Canadian and NI 43-101 sources use **metric
tonnes**. Units are preserved as the source gave them and labeled.

## Statewide Scale

**2025:** ~$6.4 billion non-fuel mineral value, ~$6.5 billion including ~1 million tons of coal — a **38% jump
over $4.8 billion in 2024**, driven almost entirely by metal prices rather than volume. Gold ~960,000 oz
(~$3.3 B); silver ~12–13 Moz (~$560 M).

**2024 (USGS via SR78):** Alaska non-fuel mineral value $4.71 B, 6th among US states. Alaska metal production
$4.69 B ≈ **14% of all US metal production**. Alaska supplied **>42% of US silver, 15% of US gold, >80% of US
zinc, ~42% of US lead.** Globally: 1.9% of silver, 0.73% of gold, 5% of zinc, 2.9% of lead.

In 2025 Alaska had **seven large operating mines, one small hardrock producer, hundreds of placer gold
operations, and ~100 sand/gravel/rock producers.**

## Red Dog — the anchor, and the most fragile thing in the territory

Zinc-lead-silver-germanium. Operated by **Teck Alaska** on land owned by **NANA Regional Corporation**. In
production since December 1989. ~82 miles north of Kotzebue, **46 miles inland** from the Chukchi Sea, in the
De Long Mountains of the western Brooks Range. **No road connection to anywhere else.**
https://dnr.alaska.gov/mlw/mining/large-mines/red-dog/

- **2025 production:** 462,700 t zinc (1.02 billion lb), down 17% from 555,600 t in 2024. Lead 107,000 t.
- **Reserves:** 29.1 Mt proven & probable at 11.5% Zn, 3.3% Pb (Teck). DGGS App. E gives 32,077,221 **short
  tons** probable at 11.5% Zn / 3.3% Pb / 1.80 oz/t Ag, plus indicated and inferred for a total 51.8 Mst at
  11.0% Zn, 3.9% Pb, 2.12 oz/t Ag (88.1 Moz contained Ag).
- **Reserve life — sources differ:** DGGS, NANA and local reporting say the Aqqaluk pit depletes **~2031**;
  Teck's more recent guidance says out to **2032**. "Early 2030s" is the honest answer.
- **Forward guidance (Teck):** 2026: 375–415 kt Zn; 2027: 330–370 kt; 2028: 230–270 kt. Grades falling.
- **Germanium:** Red Dog is the **only US germanium producer**, ~10 t/yr. Ge-bearing concentrate is exported
  to a **refinery in Canada** — Alaska does not refine it.
- SR78 argues Red Dog "may be the largest critical mineral producer in the United States."

**The DMTS haul road and port — the fragile part:**

- The **DeLong Mountain Transportation System** is a **52-mile** private industrial haul road from mine to a
  **shallow-water** port on the Chukchi Sea. **24 miles crosses Cape Krusenstern National Monument.**
- Owned by **AIDEA**, a state agency, operated under contract — a public-infrastructure-for-private-mine model
  SR78 notes is unusual in the US.
- By 2001 it carried ~1.1 million dry tons of concentrate a year: 100-ton trucks with 72-ton payloads
  dispatched roughly **every 15 minutes, 24 hours a day, year-round.**
- Concentrate is stored in buildings at the port and loaded **seasonally**. ⚠️ Lightering distance, barge
  arrangement, and the exact open-water window (commonly described as ~July–October) are **unverified**.

**Mine Life Extension (Aktigiruq / Anarraaq):** two deeper deposits ~10 miles north on **state** land,
requiring **underground** mining. Teck plans $146–183 M, a **12-mile road** to the existing mill, and a
prefeasibility study. **FAST-41 permitting designation September 2026**, USACE lead; proposed 14-year initial
operating period.

| Deposit | Dec 2024 estimate | Sept 2026 estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Aktigiruq indicated | 32.7 Mt @ 16.2% Zn, 4.2% Pb | 42.2 Mt @ 15.8% Zn |
| Aktigiruq inferred | 26.6 Mt @ 13.7% Zn, 3.5% Pb | 18.8 Mt @ 11.8% Zn |
| Anarraaq | 16.4 Mt @ 14.3% Zn, 4.0% Pb (inferred) | 17 Mt @ 14% Zn (inferred) |

**Also at Anarraaq: an inferred 1.1 BILLION short tons of bedded barite** (grade unspecified; King et al.,
2002). An enormous industrial-mineral resource nobody discusses.

**Local economics:** NANA received ~$100 M in mine revenues in 2023; ~1,000 NANA shareholders worked at Red
Dog earning ~$62 M; the Northwest Arctic Borough received ~$26 M in payments-in-lieu-of-taxes plus $8 M for
village projects. NANA established a permanent fund in 2025 to outlast closure.

## Other Producing Mines

**Fort Knox (gold) — Fairbanks district.** Kinross. **2025: 410,822 oz**, +9%, >40% of Alaska's gold. Very low
grade, very high tonnage: **36,000 t/day mill**, ore ~0.3–0.4 g/t, heap leach since 2009. Total resource
205,468,379 short tons @ 0.402 g/t = **3.24 Moz**. Largest gold producer in Alaska history (>7.5 Moz through
2019). Mine life 2030+; SR78 expects closure by ~2035.

- **Manh Choh:** satellite skarn near Tetlin, Peak Gold JV (Kinross 70% / Contango 30%), ore **trucked to the
  Fort Knox mill** — a hub-and-spoke model. First gold July 2024. 4,053,786 st @ 7.05 g/t = 743,716 oz Au +
  1.44 Moz Ag. Mine life to ~2028.

**Pogo (gold) — ~85 mi SE of Fairbanks.** Northern Star. High-grade **underground**. **2025: 273,895 oz.**
Production since 2006. Mine life 2030+.

**Usibelli Coal Mine — Healy.** Alaska's **only** coal mine, operating continuously since **1943**. ~110
employees. **~1 million tons/year**, essentially all for in-state power (fuels **~29% of Interior Alaska's
electricity**). DGGS mine life: **"2075+?"** — effectively unlimited relative to any other Alaska operation.
Sub-bituminous, low-sulfur, shallow, strippable. **Critically: it sits on the Parks Highway and the Alaska
Railroad.** https://www.usibelli.com/

**⚑ CASCADIAN — Greens Creek** (Admiralty Island, ~20 mi S of Juneau). Hecla. **Largest primary silver
producer in the United States**, top-10 worldwide. 2025: 8.7 Moz silver, 59,349 oz gold, ~47,000 t zinc.
Resource 19,944,000 st @ 11.8 oz/t Ag (236.0 Moz Ag), 7.0% Zn, 2.6% Pb. **Mine life 2037 — the longest of any
mine in the region.** This is a major loss to the Alaska Republic.

**⚑ CASCADIAN — Kensington** (~45 mi N of Juneau). Coeur. **2025: 106,068 oz gold**, +11%. Has run on
one-to-two-year mine lives since opening.

**⚑ CASCADIAN — Dawson Mine.** Small hardrock gold/silver producer; DGGS mine life 2075.

Plus **150+ family-sized placer operations** statewide (see Part 1's historical districts below).

## Undeveloped Alaska Deposits

DGGS's blunt assessment: *none* of the major projects will produce within three or five years. Only
**Aktigiruq, Donlin and Graphite One** plausibly reach production inside ten years, all targeting **2031**.

**Pebble (Cu-Au-Mo-Ag) — Bristol Bay.** **The largest known undeveloped copper resource on Earth by contained
metal.** Northern Dynasty. 2023 PEA: **12,026,202,100 short tons @ 0.341% Cu, 0.009 oz/t Au, 0.0235% Mo =
75.65 billion lb copper, 106.5 Moz gold, 371 Moz silver, 4.58 billion lb molybdenum.** (Most of that is
inferred — note the category split.)

**Permitting status — effectively dead.** USACE **denied** the permit Nov 2020. **EPA exercised its Clean
Water Act §404(c) veto 31 January 2023** — only the 14th such action in the Act's 50-year history. Alaska sued
at the Supreme Court; **the Court denied relief in January 2024.** DGGS lists no construction or production
date. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pebble_Mine

**Donlin Gold — Yukon-Kuskokwim region near Aniak.** **33.8 Moz gold** in probable reserves — "ranks fourth in
the world." ~27-year mine life. Owned by Indigenous landowners **Calista Corporation** and **The Kuskokwim
Corporation** with NOVAGOLD. **All federal permits in hand.** DGGS timeline: permitting began 2012,
construction 2027, production 2031 — three decades from exploration to production, which SR78 uses as its
canonical example of Alaska project timelines. Would roughly **double Alaska's gold production.**
https://donlingold.com/

**Graphite Creek / Graphite One — Seward Peninsula, north of Nome.** **The largest known graphite deposit in
the United States**, on **100% state-owned land**. FS (March 2025): 489,286,898 short tons containing
**20,081,536 tonnes of graphite**. 20-year mine life **based on drilling only 1.2 miles (12%) of a 9.5-mile
geophysical anomaly** — open in three directions. $6.4 B pre-tax NPV, 30% IRR. Permitting began August 2025.
**Reality check: US natural graphite production in 2023 and 2024 was zero, and DGGS projects zero Alaska
graphite through 2030**, with 190,000 t only in 2035. Depends on the **Port of Nome** expansion; if deep-water
draft targets are missed, the plan is to **lighter to a ship anchored in deeper water.**
https://www.graphiteoneinc.com/graphite-creek/

**Ambler district — Arctic and Bornite (Trilogy Metals).**
- **Arctic** (FS Feb 2023): 44,312,862 st @ **2.86% Cu**, 3.96% Zn, 0.78% Pb — **2.54 billion lb Cu**,
  737,000 oz Au, 57.0 Moz Ag. A genuinely high-grade VMS deposit.
- **Bornite** (PEA Jan 2025): all **inferred**, 230,691,437 st @ 1.05% Cu = **6.36 billion lb copper.**
- **Stranded — there is no road.** The proposed **Ambler Access Project** would run ~211 miles across the
  Brooks Range foothills including through **Gates of the Arctic National Preserve**. Politically whipsawed:
  Interior held a signing ceremony **advancing** it in October 2025; Trilogy received a **$35.6 M US
  Department of War strategic equity investment** in 2026. ⚠️ Current legal/permit standing **unverified**.

**Livengood / Money Knob — ~70 mi NW of Fairbanks.** 793,979,980 short tons @ 0.62 g/t Au = **13.83 Moz gold.**
Enormous tonnage, very low grade — which is why it has never been built.

**Nikolai (Ni-Cu-Co-PGE, Delta River).** 3,607,948,000 short tons @ 0.21% Ni, 0.06% Cu, 0.02% Co, plus **7.889
Moz palladium and 4.295 Moz platinum.** Enormous but very low grade. (The table's iron column does not
reconcile arithmetically — treat the iron figure as unreliable.)

**Others:** Estelle/Korbel 5.17 Moz Au · Golden Summit 2.95 Moz Au · Lik 22,498,147 st @ 8.14% Zn (next to Red
Dog) · Waterpump Creek 2,622,760 st @ **9.87% Pb, 11.28% Zn, 8.15 oz/t Ag** (small, spectacular grade) ·
Whistler 3.96 Moz Au · Illinois Creek 357 koz Au.

**⚑ CASCADIAN undeveloped:** Bokan Mountain REE/uranium (Prince of Wales), Niblack, Palmer (near Haines, with
barite), Quartz Hill molybdenum (1,988,567,240 st @ 0.094% Mo = **3.73 billion lb Mo**, one of the largest moly
deposits on Earth, inside/adjacent to Misty Fiords National Monument wilderness).

**Probable error in a primary source:** DGGS SR78 App. E lists Johnson Tract indicated at 384,595,959 short
tons @ 0.156 oz/t Au for 598,000 oz — off by ~100×. The real figure is likely ~3.85 million short tons.

## Historical Gold Districts — Placer vs. Lode

**This distinction is the single most important thing in this file for a post-collapse scenario.**

**Placer gold is free metal in gravel, recoverable with water, motion and gravity alone** — a pan, a rocker, a
sluice box, at most a dredge. **Lode gold is locked in quartz-sulfide rock** and requires drilling, blasting,
crushing, stamp or ball mills, and usually cyanide or mercury amalgamation. **A collapsed-technology society
can work placers indefinitely and cannot work lodes.**

**Alaska cumulative gold production: ~49,270,000 troy ounces.** Peak single year: **1,022,987 oz.**

| District | Type | Production | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Klondike (Dawson City, Yukon)** | **Placer**, overwhelmingly | not verified | The 1896–99 rush. Frozen gravels worked by hand, then steam thawing, then bucket-line dredges. **Still actively placer-mined today.** |
| **Fairbanks** | **Both** | **Placers >8 Moz**, plus major lode | Felix Pedro, 1901; rush 1903. Fairbanks, Cleary and Ester Creeks produced ~2/3 of pre-1910 gold. |
| **Nome (Cape Nome)** | **Placer**, including the famous **beach sands** | **>3.6 Moz** from the creeks | "Three Lucky Swedes," 1898. >$1 million off the beach in 1899 alone; second and third buried beach lines found later. Anvil Creek produced Alaska's 2nd-largest nugget (**182 troy oz**). **Placer mining at Nome has continued to the present day** except during WWII. **The single most "recoverable with simple technology" district in the territory.** |
| **⚑ Juneau** | **Lode**, almost entirely | **>7 Moz lode vs. only 80,000 oz placer** | Treadwell: underground from 1883, and by the mid-1910s **960 stamps** were running; >3 Moz 1881–1922. Then the Alaska-Juneau mine, 1,000 workers in the 1930s. |
| **⚑ Admiralty** | Lode | ~500,000 oz, almost all Greens Creek | **No significant placer mining.** |
| **Iditarod (Otter Creek/Flat)** | Placer | ⚠️ not verified | Gap. |

**Take-away: Nome, Fairbanks and the Klondike survive a technology collapse. Juneau does not** — and Juneau is
Cascadian regardless.

## Yukon

⚠️ **The weakest section.** `yukon.ca`, `data.geology.gov.yk.ca` and `emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca` are all behind
Cloudflare and refused every request, so Yukon Geological Survey and Yukon government primary statistics were
unreachable. What follows is from company sources, NRCan, and trade press.

**Keno Hill / Elsa silver district.** Hecla (via the 2022 Alexco buyout). ~45 km NE of Mayo. **242 km²,
road-accessible.** More than **35 mines** have operated across the district in a century. **Restarted 2023:
2.8 Moz silver in 2024, 3.0 Moz in 2025**, guidance 2.9–3.2 Moz for 2026. The **440-ton-per-day mill continues
to run below capacity**, limited by available development ore. Resource: indicated 583,000 tons @ **24.09 oz/t
Ag**; inferred 662,000 tons @ 16.69 oz/t. Small tonnage, very high grade. **Historic Hector-Calumet mine alone
produced ~96 Moz silver.** Recent intercepts include 0.3 m @ **349.2 oz/t Ag** and 0.3 m @ 247.3 oz/t Ag with
**72.6% Pb**. https://www.hecla.com/operations/keno-hill/

**Eagle Gold, Dublin Gulch — the 2024 heap leach failure.** A **landslide at the heap leach facility on 24 June
2024**. Victoria Gold went into **receivership**. Cleanup has run at **more than $11 million per month**, and
total cleanup **will surpass $300 million since receivership** (CBC, May 2026). The receiver has **sued the
engineering firm** alleging negligence. **April 2026: Singapore-based Boroo was chosen as potential buyer** by
the Yukon government; the **First Nation of Nacho Nyäk Dun** stated it is neutral on the sale but criticized
the government's handling. ⚠️ Pre-failure production, slide volume and cyanide release quantity **unverified**.

**Minto copper mine.** ~250 km north of Whitehorse, in the traditional territory of **Selkirk First Nation**.
Produced **>500 million lb of copper over 16 years** before **shutting down in 2023** (abandoned by Minto
Metals). **After closure, Selkirk First Nation acquired the idled mine** and formed **Selkirk Copper Mines** —
a past-producing copper mine under **Indigenous-led ownership**, with the **4,100 t/day mill intact.** Updated
July 2026 after 52,288 m of drilling: **M&I up 280% to 47.8 Mt @ 0.89% Cu (940 Mlb), 0.34 g/t Au, 3.2 g/t Ag**;
inferred 16.9 Mt @ 0.76% Cu. A PEA targets **12–15 years at 4,100 t/day**; feasibility from Q3 2026.

**Casino (Cu-Au-Mo-Ag).** Western Copper and Gold. ~300 km NW of Whitehorse. 2022 FS reserves: mill P&P
**1,217.1 Mt @ 0.19% Cu, 0.22 g/t Au, 0.021% Mo, 1.7 g/t Ag**; heap leach P&P 209.6 Mt. M&I resources 2,259.0
Mt = **7,446 Mlb Cu, 12.9 Moz Au, 791.2 Mlb Mo, 103.1 Moz Ag.** **27-year mine life**, 120,000 t/d mill +
25,000 t/d heap leach, strip ratio **0.43:1**. Pre-production capital **C$3.62 billion**; C1 cash cost
**negative $0.80/lb Cu** net of byproducts. Undergoing **YESAB Panel Review — the highest level of assessment
in the Yukon**; ESE Statement submitted **October 2025**. Partnered with **Mitsubishi Materials**. **Blocked by
infrastructure: needs a long access road and power.** https://www.westerncopperandgold.com/casino-project/

**Yukon placer gold.** **NRCan: Yukon produced 6.0 tonnes of gold in 2024 (≈193,000 troy oz), 3% of Canada's
total.** ⚠️ **Caveat:** that is *all* Yukon gold and 2024 included several months of Eagle Gold production
before the June failure — it is **not** a placer-only figure. **The placer-only annual figure and the count of
active operations could not be obtained** (Cloudflare). Main districts: **Klondike, Indian River, Sixtymile,
Fortymile, Dawson Range**. The **Klondike Placer Miners' Association** states ~**90% of active operations are
family-run**, averaging **2.5 generations** on each site, and that **almost 80% of placer mines are owned or
co-owned by women.** https://www.kpma.ca/

**Faro.** A very large former lead-zinc mine, now **one of Canada's most expensive federal contaminated
sites.** ⚠️ All figures headline-only and needing re-sourcing: >$350 M spent by 2016 "with not an inch
remediated"; forecast to exceed $500 M by 2019; commonly cited total ~C$1 billion, **unverified**.

**Not researched:** Howard's Pass / Selwyn Basin zinc-lead, Wolverine, Coffee Gold, Cantung/Mactung tungsten
(note **Cantung is in NWT** and **Mactung straddles the Yukon-NWT boundary**), Mount Nansen, Brewery Creek,
Kudz Ze Kayah.

## Critical Minerals — Real vs. Speculative

**The 2025 US Critical Minerals List has 60 entries. DGGS assesses that 57 of 60 could conceivably be produced
in Alaska.** The three that cannot: **aluminum, potash, silicon.** That "57 of 60" is a *possibility*
assessment, not a resource statement.

**Actually produced in commercial quantity today:** zinc (Red Dog + ⚑Greens Creek, >80% of US), lead (~42% of
US), silver (>42% of US), **germanium (Red Dog, the only US producer, ~10 t/yr, refined in Canada)**. Copper is
negligible (⚑Greens Creek byproduct only, 0.15% of US production).

**Zinc alone — mostly Red Dog — was 70% of the total value of US primary critical-mineral mine production in
2024.** That is how concentrated US critical-mineral supply is on one Alaskan mine.

**Statewide resources (DGGS Table 5, 2025):** Barite 23,247,270 t · Cobalt 661,500 t · Copper 47,145,510 t ·
**Graphite 22,136,078 t** · Lead 8,020,905 t · Molybdenum 4,189,913 t · Nickel 6,223,500 t · Zinc 28,289,655 t ·
**REEs 38,752 t** · Palladium 7,889,000 oz · Platinum 4,295,000 oz · Gold 208,371,957 oz · Silver 1,238,062,799 oz.

DGGS's qualitative global comparison: Alaska holds roughly **7% of global zinc, 7% of global silver, ~2% of
world lead, >12% of global copper, 7% of global gold, 16% of world molybdenum, and 17% of global coal
resources.**

**Treat with real skepticism:**
- **⚑ Bokan Mountain REEs** — resource estimate dates from **May 2015**, no grade carried, no timeline, and
  Alaska's entire statewide REE resource is only **38,752 tons**. **The biggest gap between reputation and
  substance in the Alaska critical-minerals story** — and Cascadian anyway.
- **Antimony** — three projects announcing 2026–27 production with **zero announced reserves, zero resources,
  zero mine lives, no mining permits.** DGGS uses a 500 t/yr placeholder and expects Alaska antimony production
  to have **ended by 2030.**
- **Tin** — occurrence-level only (Seward Peninsula: Lost River, Ear Mountain, the York district). **Not even
  listed in the statewide resource table.**
- **Cobalt** — essentially all locked in Bornite (inferred only) and Nikolai (very low grade). No project.
- **Gallium** — **no Alaska resource or occurrence data found at all.** Treat as absent.
- **PGMs** — essentially all in the single very-low-grade Nikolai deposit. **Goodnews Bay** placer platinum was
  historically real (mined by the Goodnews Bay Mining Company **until 1979**; the village is literally named
  Platinum, Alaska) but has been exhausted as a commercial operation for decades. ⚠️ Total ounces produced
  **not verified.**

**DGGS's own framing, which is the honest one:** "Although the existence of critical minerals in these deposits
is likely or known, there remains considerable uncertainty as to the level of concentration of these associated
critical minerals, or whether these critical minerals can be extracted from the ore."

## Iron, Limestone, Cement, Aggregate — the Reconstruction Question

**This is where the news is worst, and it is the most important finding in Part 1 after the placer/lode split.**

**Aggregate and sand/gravel — YES, abundant.** 2025: **~6.5 million tons sand and gravel + 750,000 tons rock**
from ~100 producers statewide. Distributed everywhere, requires almost no technology. Glacial outwash and river
gravels are ubiquitous.

**Iron ore — NO commercial resource found.**
- **Alaska's statewide resource table does not list iron at all.** A strong negative signal from the state's
  own geological survey.
- The only iron figure anywhere is a byproduct grade at **Nikolai** (~9.8% Fe), whose contained-iron column
  does not reconcile arithmetically. Even at face value, 9.8% Fe is far below ore grade (commercial iron ore is
  55–65% Fe) — that is a nickel deposit with iron in the silicates, not an iron deposit.
- **Klukwan iron-titanium: NOT VERIFIED.** The Klukwan magnetite-ilmenite body (near Haines, **⚑ Cascadian**)
  is real and historically known, but no tonnage, grade, or development status could be obtained. Note also
  that titaniferous magnetite is a *difficult* ore — the titanium is exactly what makes it hard to smelt in a
  conventional blast furnace.
- **Bottom line: no evidence of a developed or development-ready iron ore resource in Alaska or the Yukon.**
  For a reconstruction scenario this is a hard constraint. The region's iron would have to come from **scrap**
  — which, given the Alaska Railroad, the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, Anchorage, and decades of abandoned mining and
  military infrastructure, is actually substantial.

**Limestone — probable but UNVERIFIED, and mostly Cascadian anyway.** **⚑ Prince of Wales Island has extensive
limestone and famous karst** — confirming carbonate bedrock at scale in the Panhandle. But **no active
limestone quarrying, cement plant, or lime production in Alaska could be verified**, and **Alaska appears to
have no cement plant at all** (⚠️ needs verification, but if true it is a severe constraint: no cement means no
concrete, and there is no evidence of domestic clinker capacity). **Because the best-documented limestone is
Cascadian, the limestone question for the Alaska Republic is genuinely open and unresolved.**

**Clay — not researched, no data.**

**Barite — the unexpected industrial-mineral win.** **Anarraaq: 1,100,000,000 short tons inferred**, adjacent
to Red Dog infrastructure. **Lakeview/Longview (southern NPR-A): 20,200,000 short tons @ 93% barite** (USGS
PP 1760-C). **Bion (Howard Pass): 11,079,331 short tons** (USGS OFR 93-215). ⚠️ Note the statewide barite
figure (23.2 Mt) is far smaller than Anarraaq alone — the two are not counted on the same basis.

## Coal

**Usibelli (Healy)** — see above. The only operating mine; ~1 Mt/yr flat through 2035 per DGGS; mine life
"2075+"; **on the Parks Highway and the Alaska Railroad.**

**Wishbone Hill** (Matanuska Valley) — proposed, currently **inactive**, renewing permits.

**DGGS states Alaska holds roughly 17% of global coal resources** — the largest relative endowment of any
commodity in the state, far larger than its share of any metal.

⚠️ **Cook Inlet and Western Arctic / Chicago Creek coal tonnages: NOT FOUND.** What can be said structurally
rather than by citation: the 17% figure is driven overwhelmingly by the **North Slope / Western Arctic** coals,
which are enormous and **essentially completely inaccessible** — no road, no rail, no port, permafrost,
hundreds of miles from any load-out. **Alaska's only *accessible* coal is Usibelli.** That contrast — a vast
stranded resource versus one small railroad-served mine — is the accurate shape of the picture, but **get the
actual tonnages from USGS before using a number.**

# PART 2 — FISHERIES

## Scale

**The North Pacific — essentially Alaska — accounted for 62.5% of US commercial landings by volume in 2023.**
US total: 8.4 billion pounds, $5.1 billion ex-vessel.

**Statewide economics (2022, McKinley Research for ASMI):**
- Harvest **5.8 billion lb, $2.0 billion ex-vessel**
- Processed output **2.3 billion lb, $5.2 billion first-wholesale** — **processing adds $3.2 billion of value,
  more than the harvest itself is worth**
- 48,000+ direct workers; **seafood processing is 66% of Alaska's entire manufacturing employment**
- ~1.3% of global seafood harvest

**Dutch Harbor / Unalaska: #1 US port by volume for the 26th consecutive year — 780.1 million pounds,
$224.5 million ex-vessel (2023).** **Kodiak fell out of the nation's top five ports in 2022 for the first time
in 37 years.**

Two numbers worth noticing: **Naknek lands a third of Dutch Harbor's tonnage but is worth more money**
(sockeye, $298.5 M on 234.5 M lb), and **⚑Sitka lands almost nothing by weight yet clears $77.5 M**
(halibut/sablefish — extreme value density, air-freightable).

## Walleye Pollock — the volume engine

One stock, industrially concentrated, and the default white fish of the Western diet. 2024 US landings: **over
3 billion pounds, ~$432 million.**

**The 2-million-ton ceiling:** the BSAI groundfish FMP sets an optimum yield range of **1.4–2.0 million metric
tons** for the sum of all BSAI groundfish TACs, and NMFS set it at **exactly 2,000,000 mt for 2025, 2026 and
2027** — managers hit the ceiling every year. **Pollock alone is ~69% of that cap.**

Bering Sea pollock TAC has run **1.30–1.43 million mt** since 2018, with a dip to 1,111,000 mt in 2022 when the
ABC fell below the usual TAC. **2025 actual catch: 1,324,556 mt against a 1,389,000 mt TAC (~95%).** EBS survey
biomass and abundance **fell 30% and 36% between the 2024 and 2025 surveys**, recruitment has been below
average since 2018, and the stock is projected to decline in 2026.

**Gulf of Alaska pollock: 2026 TAC cut 25% to 139,498 mt.** **The cause was administrative, not biological** —
a six-week federal government shutdown ending 12 November 2025 prevented completion of the survey and stock
assessment, so the SSC rolled forward old projections and set a precautionary number. (Worth noting for a
collapse story: the management system itself is a dependency.)

**Season structure:** A season (roe season) **20 January – 10 June**; B season **10 June – 1 November**; Bering
Sea directed allowance splits **45% A / 55% B**. Product mix: **~25% surimi, ~20% roe, ~50% fillets.**
**72% of fillet production and 88% of surimi production is exported.**

**American Fisheries Act allocation** of the non-CDQ TAC: **50% inshore catcher vessels / 40% offshore
catcher-processors / 10% motherships**, with **10% off the top to CDQ groups.** So **roughly half the largest
US fishery by volume never touches shore.**

## Salmon — and the hatchery dependency

**2025 statewide commercial harvest: 194,805,726 fish, 792,777,134 lb, $541.4 M.** **2024 (even year):
101,295,384 fish, 450,073,696 lb, $304.0 M.** Statewide volume nearly doubles between even and odd years
because of pink salmon. Prices are brutal: **pink at $0.30/lb, chum $0.60, sockeye $1.17.**

**Bristol Bay sockeye — the standout:**

| Year | Inshore run | Commercial harvest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 67.7M | ~40M+ | Record at the time |
| 2022 | **79.2M** | **60.5M** | **All-time record run; largest harvest since 1893** |
| 2023 | 54.5M | 40.6M | |
| 2024 | 51.6M | 31.6M | Price crashed to **$0.89/lb** |
| 2025 | 56.7M | 41.3M | $1.03/lb |
| 2026 forecast | 45.32M | 32.26M | 26% below the 10-year average |

Long-term average run (1963–2025) is **37.40 million**, so even the "bad" 2026 forecast is 21% above the
historical mean. **The 10-year average (61.0M) is 63% above the long-term average.**

### HATCHERY VS WILD — the load-bearing fact

From the **Alaska Salmon Fisheries Enhancement Annual Report, 2025** (ADF&G RIR 5J26-04):
https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/FedAidPDFs/RIR.5J.2026.04.pdf

**Statewide, hatchery fish were 34% of the total 2025 commercial salmon harvest by numbers and 26% of
ex-vessel value.** But the average conceals everything:

| Species | Hatchery share |
|---|---|
| **Chum** | **83%** |
| **Pink** | **40%** |
| Chinook | 34% |
| Coho | 28% |
| **Sockeye** | **1%** |

By region it is starker:
- **Prince William Sound: 84% of the entire commercial catch is hatchery fish** — 85% of pink, 95% of chum.
  PWS produced a **44.4 million fish** hatchery return in 2025.
- **Kodiak: 27% overall.**
- **⚑ Southeast: 38% overall — 94% of chum.**
- **Bristol Bay: 0%. Entirely wild.** Confirmed — zero hatchery fish in the Bristol Bay commercial harvest.
  Same for Chignik/Alaska Peninsula and the entire Arctic-Yukon-Kuskokwim region.

**The system's physical footprint:** **28 operating salmon hatcheries** (25 private nonprofit, of which 11 are
state-owned but PNP-operated at no cost to the state). 2025: **2.1 billion eggs taken, 1.7 billion juveniles
released.** Total 2025 hatchery return: **70.97 million adult salmon.** Of that, 50.6M went to common-property
commercial fisheries, **15.6M was taken as "cost recovery"** — fish the hatcheries catch and sell to pay their
own operating bills — and 3.4M was held as broodstock.

**Named facilities (Alaska Republic side):** PWSAC operates **Armin F. Koernig, Cannery Creek, Gulkana, Main
Bay, Wally Noerenberg (Esther Island)**; **Solomon Gulch (Valdez)** is run by Valdez Fisheries Development
Association. Kodiak: **Kitoi Bay, Pillar Creek**. Cook Inlet: **Trail Lakes**. Interior: **Ruth Burnett
(Fairbanks)**. Anchorage: **William Jack Hernandez**. (The large Southeast cluster — SSRAA, NSRAA, Macaulay,
Snettisham, Sheldon Jackson, Tamgas Creek — is **⚑ Cascadian**.)

**What this means for a collapse scenario:** roughly a third of Alaska's commercial salmon — and the
overwhelming majority of its chum, plus a big minority of its pink — exists only because incubation facilities,
water systems, power, feed and staff keep running. **A hatchery system is a continuously maintained industrial
process with a 2–5 year lag built in:** the fish returning in any year were released 2–5 years earlier. Shut
the hatcheries and the harvest does not fall immediately — it falls on a delay, and then keeps falling.
**Bristol Bay would be untouched, because it has no hatchery component at all. That asymmetry is probably the
single most useful fact in Part 2.** The cost-recovery mechanism is fragile in a distinct way too: hatcheries
fund themselves by *selling fish into the same market that would be collapsing.*

## The Yukon River Collapse

**2025 chinook:** Eagle sonar (US/Canada border passage) recorded **23,863 chinook — about 49% below the
historical average and the third-lowest on record**, against a border passage objective of **71,000**. Missed
by two-thirds. **Chinook fishing was closed all season throughout the Yukon Area.** Chena River: 1,247 fish.
Salcha River: 1,832.

**2025 fall chum:** drainagewide run **184,000 fish**, escapement ~180,000 against an SEG of **300,000–600,000**
— the **fifth-lowest on record and the sixth consecutive low year.** Mainstem escapement into Canada at Eagle:
**19,600 — second lowest since 1980**, against a goal of 70,000–104,000. **Neither of the two Yukon River
treaty obligations with Canada was met in 2025.**

**2026 forecasts:** Canadian-origin chinook **22,000–33,000** against the 71,000 objective. ADF&G states
flatly: *"Harvest of Yukon River Chinook salmon is not expected during the 2026 season in any fishery."* Fall
chum point estimate 311,000 against an average run of 950,000 — and the assessment warns its own
spawner-recruit model has **overestimated fall chum for eight straight years.**

**2025 Yukon River: no commercial harvest at all**, summer or fall.

**The 2024 agreement:** a Canada–ADF&G agreement on Canadian-origin Yukon chinook was **signed 1 April 2024**
under Chapter 8 of the Pacific Salmon Treaty, with a rebuilding plan in development. ⚠️ **The widely reported
seven-year term and the exact harvest scope could not be verified against a primary source.** What *is*
verifiable and more useful: **the fishery is already, functionally, under a total moratorium.**

**For the interior villages (Emmonak, Galena, Tanana, Fort Yukon) and Yukon Territory First Nations upstream,
the single most important subsistence species has been unavailable for years running.**

## Crab — the snow crab collapse

| Season | Snow crab | Bristol Bay red king crab |
|---|---|---|
| 1991 | **>320 million lb** (peak) | — |
| 2020/21 | 45 million lb TAC | open |
| 2021/22 | **5.6 million lb** (−88%) | **CLOSED** |
| 2022/23 | **CLOSED — first ever** | **CLOSED** |
| 2023/24 | **CLOSED** | REOPENED |
| 2024/25 | REOPENED, 4.72 million lb | 2.3 million lb |
| 2025/26 | **9.3 million lb** | ~2.7 million lb |

**Roughly 10 billion snow crab disappeared from the Bering Sea over about four years.** NOAA's 2025
assessment: overfished but rebuilding, target date 2029.

**The cause was not fishing.** A NOAA study attributes the collapse to the **2018–2019 Bering Sea marine
heatwave causing mass starvation** — warmer water raised crab metabolic demand while their cold-water habitat
shrank. Researchers explicitly tested and **ruled out** directed fishing, trawl bycatch, bitter crab syndrome,
cannibalism and Pacific cod predation.

**Acidification hits unevenly:** snow crab are relatively resilient; **red king crab and Tanner crab are not.**

**A new wrinkle:** for 2025/26 ADF&G for the first time reserved ~**11% of the snow crab TAC (~1 million lb)
for snow-Tanner hybrids**, after surveys found an "unprecedented" number — a concrete sign of species ranges
overlapping where they previously did not.

**St. Paul Island — the key human case.** Population ~300 (the Pribilofs), host to **Trident's plant, the
largest snow crab processor in the nation.** After the crash the city declared an emergency: ~**$3 million/year
in lost tax revenue, ~25 city employees lost over three years.** Critically, **even when the fishery reopened
in October 2024, Trident did not open the plant** — the TAC was too small to justify the fixed cost of opening
the doors, and the company could no longer cross-subsidize from pollock, salmon and cod because those were also
doing badly. **All the 2024/25 crab went to Unalaska instead**, and St. Paul went a third and then fourth
consecutive year with no crab landed.

**That is a clean illustration of a second-order collapse: the resource partially recovered and the community
still got nothing, because the processing infrastructure had a minimum viable scale and the resource came back
below it.**

**Fleet:** ~60 vessels in 2022 and shrinking; in January 2025, **nine vessels** were actively fishing snow
crab. A **$144 million federal disaster package** covers the closures — Unalaska accepted its $3 M+ in June
2025, *three years after the crash*, and crew applications for the 2021/22 and 2022/23 seasons only opened in
**November 2025**.

## Halibut, Sablefish, Cod, Herring

**Pacific halibut:** 2024 ~**20 million lb, $78 million**; ~95% of the US catch comes from Alaska. Managed by
the **IPHC** under **IFQ** plus **CDQ**. ⚠️ Area-by-area limits (2C, 3A, 3B, 4A–4E) **not verified.**

**Sablefish / black cod:** 2024 **64 million lb, $96 million** — **the highest-value finfish per pound in
Alaska**, primary market Japan, ~90% longline. **GOA TAC roughly 2.5× between 2016 and 2023** (9,087 → 23,201
mt) — the flip side of the collapse story, a stock that exploded. The catch: the surge is composed of small
fish, which crashed the per-pound price.

**Pacific cod — the clearest marine-heatwave signature in the dataset.** Gulf of Alaska TAC:

| Year | TAC (mt) |
|---|---|
| 2015 | 75,202 |
| 2017 | 64,442 |
| **2018** | **13,096** ← an 80% cut in one year |
| 2020 | **6,431** ← the floor |
| 2024 | 23,766 |
| 2026 | 21,826 |

That is the 2014–2016 "Blob" arriving in the management numbers with a lag. **The GOA cod fishery has recovered
to roughly a third of its pre-heatwave level and stalled there.** BSAI cod held up far better — a decline, not
a collapse.

**Herring:** **Togiak (Bristol Bay) has not opened since 2022** — the Asian roe market's demand fell away. The
**Bering Sea bait herring market collapsed with the crab fisheries**, because crab pots were its main customer.
**Important distinction: in Alaska right now this is mostly a collapse of a herring *market*, not a herring
*stock*.**

## Processing Infrastructure — the real chokepoint

**In a collapse scenario the plants are the load-bearing wall, not the boats.** Recall: harvest $2.0 B at the
dock, $5.2 B after processing; **66% of Alaska's entire manufacturing employment.**

**Known plants (Alaska Republic side):** **Unalaska/Dutch Harbor** — UniSea (largest local), Westward Seafoods,
Alyeska Seafoods. **Akutan** — **Trident's plant, described as the largest seafood processing plant in North
America.** **Kodiak** — Trident's **"Star of Kodiak,"** one of the largest buildings in downtown Kodiak,
**capable of processing over one million pounds of pollock per day.** **Bristol Bay** — Trident Naknek
(including the historic Diamond NN Cannery), Silver Bay, former Peter Pan Dillingham and Port Moller. **Alaska
Peninsula/Aleutians** — False Pass, King Cove, Sand Point, Chignik, Valdez. **St. Paul Island** — Trident.
(Ketchikan, Petersburg, Wrangell, Sitka are **⚑ Cascadian**.)

**Dependencies — verified:**
- **Fuel is a service plants provide, not just consume.** In these communities the processor *is* the fuel
  infrastructure.
- **Air access is fragile and seasonal.**
- **Labor is overwhelmingly imported.** *"For 100 years, people have been coming from somewhere else to process
  fish in Alaska."* Alaska's local pool is far too small for seasonal peaks.
- **The foreign-worker channel just closed.** UniSea began using **H-2B visas in 2019**; at peak **as many as
  half of UniSea's workers were foreign**, mostly from Eastern Europe. In **January 2026** UniSea announced a
  return to a 100% domestic workforce, citing cost and visa-lottery unpredictability.
- **Plants are single points of failure for whole towns.** King Cove, pop. ~800, "relies on the processing
  facility as its main economic engine."
- **COVID showed the vulnerability precisely:** a single outbreak at Akutan reached **266 cases** and closed
  the largest plant in North America for a month.

**Recent consolidation — the structural change.** **Trident** announced in December 2023 it was **selling about
a third of its Alaska plants** with no advance warning to fleets or municipalities. **Peter Pan Seafoods ceased
operations entirely in April 2024** — announced via a Friday-night Facebook post, facing legal claims from
fishermen over unpaid deliveries. **Silver Bay Seafoods**, which is fishermen-owned, absorbed plants from both
in a single year. Underlying drivers: declining demand, very large harvests, foreign competition, imported
fish, inflation and trade sanctions, producing "a massive drop in the market value of Alaska's fish."

⚠️ **Unverified:** ammonia refrigeration tonnages, can/tinplate sourcing and whether any canmaking happens in
Alaska, Alaska Dept. of Labor nonresident-worker percentages (the commonly cited ~70–75% figure is
**unverified**), diesel volumes and barge logistics, water and wastewater.

## Subsistence — the carrying-capacity numbers

From **"Subsistence in Alaska: A Year 2017 Update," ADF&G Division of Subsistence:**
https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/subsistence/pdfs/subsistence_update_2017.pdf

**Pounds of usable weight per person per year:**

| Region | lb/person/yr |
|---|---|
| **Arctic** | **402** |
| **Western** | **379** |
| **Rural Interior** | **293** |
| Southwest–Aleutians | 210 |
| ⚑ Rural Southeast | 186 |
| Kodiak Island | 159 |
| Rural Southcentral | 145 |
| **Rural subtotal** | **276** |
| **Urban subtotal** | **19** |
| **Alaska total** | **62** |

**The rural figure of 276 lb/person/year is about 0.75 lb per person per day. It supplies 176% of the rural
population's protein requirement (~81 g/day against a 46 g requirement) but only 25% of caloric requirement
(~518 kcal/day against 2,100).** That protein-rich, calorie-poor profile is critical: **subsistence harvest
covers protein completely and calories not at all**, which is precisely why fat sources (seal oil, fish oil)
matter so much in these diets.

**Composition of the rural harvest:** salmon 32.3%, land mammals 21.9%, other fish 21.4%, marine mammals 14.4%,
wild plants 4.1%, shellfish 3.1%, birds and eggs 2.8%. **Fish altogether ~54% by weight.** 95% of rural
households *use* fish, 83% *harvest* it; in the Western region it is 100% and 98%.

**The Yukon decline in per-capita terms — the most vivid number here.** For Kusilvak Census Area (Lower Yukon)
communities, subsistence salmon harvest fell from **98 lb/person/year (2000–2008) to 71 lb/person
(2009–2017)**, and **chinook specifically from 45 lb/person (2000–2004) to just 7 lb/person (2013–2017)** — an
84% decline, *before* the total closures of 2021–2026.

**Scale check:** subsistence harvest is **0.9% of all fish and game taken in Alaska annually; commercial
fisheries are 98.6%.** The entire subsistence economy of rural Alaska runs on under one percent of total
extraction — which means **subsistence needs are trivially small relative to commercial volumes. The constraint
is never total biomass; it is local access and the presence of the run in a particular river.**

⚠️ **Preservation techniques — drying, smoking, fermentation, salting, ice cellars, seal oil, the historical
sled-dog share of the salmon catch — were NOT researched.** Real gap. Starting points: the ADF&G Technical
Paper Series and the Community Subsistence Information System.

## Climate vs. Fishing Pressure

**In the North Pacific, ocean conditions drive stock abundance far more than fishing pressure does.** Three
independent cases:

1. **Snow crab** — ~10 billion animals lost; fishing, bycatch, disease, cannibalism and predation all
   explicitly tested and rejected.
2. **GOA Pacific cod** — TAC fell 64,442 → 6,431 mt following the Blob. **Managers cut quota in response to the
   collapse; they did not cause it.**
3. **Bristol Bay sockeye** — record runs in 2021 and 2022 under the same management regime and the same warming
   ocean that was killing crab and cod.

**That third point is the one most worth carrying into fiction: warming does not uniformly reduce abundance. It
redistributes it, violently and unpredictably, creating windfalls and collapses side by side. Snow crab crashed
while sockeye boomed. The same ocean did both.**

# PART 3 — POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

⚠️ **Partial.** A full assessment requires the agriculture, energy, transport and population research that was
never done. What follows is derived from Parts 1 and 2 only, and is flagged as reasoning rather than sourced
fact where it is reasoning.

## (a) Survives regardless — geology, fish stocks, climate

- **Placer gold at Nome, Fairbanks and the Klondike.** Recoverable with water, gravity and hand tools. The
  Klondike is *still* worked this way; Nome's beach sands were worked by men with rockers in 1899. This is the
  territory's one genuinely collapse-proof extractive industry, and it is also a *currency* industry, which
  matters for trade with neighbors.
- **Sand, gravel and building stone.** Ubiquitous, no technology required.
- **Wild salmon runs with no hatchery component — above all Bristol Bay**, plus Chignik, the Alaska Peninsula,
  and the entire Arctic-Yukon-Kuskokwim region. These are self-sustaining biological systems.
- **Nearshore fish, shellfish, marine mammals and the subsistence complex generally.** Requires boats and
  knowledge, not industry. And subsistence needs are <1% of commercial volumes.
- **Coal at Healy.** Shallow, strippable, sub-bituminous, and DGGS rates the resource as effectively unlimited.
- **The ore bodies themselves.** Red Dog's zinc, Donlin's gold, Ambler's copper, Casino's copper, Pebble's
  copper do not go anywhere. Whether anyone can *reach* them is a different question.

## (b) Depends on infrastructure — repairable, with effort

- **Usibelli coal as an energy source.** It survives *if* the Parks Highway and Alaska Railroad survive. Of all
  Alaskan industrial assets this has the best ratio of value to required technology.
- **Keno Hill silver (Yukon).** Road-accessible, 440 t/day mill, extremely high grade. High-grade ore is
  exactly what a low-technology operation can work, because you get useful metal from small tonnage.
- **Minto copper (Yukon).** Idled but **intact**, with its 4,100 t/day mill standing and under local Indigenous
  ownership — the most plausible restart candidate in the territory.
- **Fish processing — but only at reduced scale, and this is the crux.** The plants are the load-bearing wall,
  not the boats: processing adds more value than the harvest is worth and is two-thirds of Alaska's
  manufacturing employment. But the industry's minimum viable scale is high, its labor is imported, and its
  fuel is the same fuel the communities depend on. **St. Paul is the cautionary case: the resource came back
  and the community still got nothing, because the plant could not open below scale.**
- **Small-scale, locally-run processing — drying, smoking, salting, cold-smoking — is a different matter
  entirely**, and would almost certainly replace the industrial model. ⚠️ Not researched.

## (c) Vanishes — artifacts of global trade and industrial supply chains

- **Red Dog.** This is the clearest case in the file. It is not a mine so much as a logistics system: a
  **52-mile private haul road** running 100-ton trucks **every 15 minutes around the clock**, feeding a
  **shallow-water port** that can only load **seasonally**, shipping concentrate to **smelters that are not in
  Alaska**, with its germanium refined **in Canada**. Every link is severable, and its reserves deplete in the
  **early 2030s** regardless.
- **Fort Knox.** A 36,000 t/day mill processing 0.3–0.4 g/t ore by heap leach. The economics only work at
  enormous scale with cheap energy and cyanide. At 0.4 g/t you move 2.5 tonnes of rock per gram of gold.
- **Pollock, and the entire at-sea fleet.** Factory trawlers, surimi lines, 72%-of-fillets-exported. Roughly
  half of it never touches shore.
- **The hatchery system, on a 2–5 year delay.** ~34% of commercial salmon statewide, **83% of chum, 84% of the
  entire Prince William Sound catch.** It is a continuously maintained industrial process, and it funds itself
  by selling fish into the market that would be collapsing. **Prince William Sound's fishery effectively ends;
  Bristol Bay's does not.**
- **Imported seasonal processing labor.** Thousands of people flown in for the A season.
- **The Ambler, Donlin, Graphite One and Casino projects.** All require roads or ports that do not exist.
- **Cement and structural iron.** No verified cement plant, no iron ore resource. **Iron comes from scrap** —
  the railroad, the pipeline, Anchorage, and abandoned military and mining infrastructure.

## (d) The territory's structural position

**It is not food-poor — that is the correction the fisheries research forces.** The instinct for a northern
territory is "resource-rich, food-poor," and the second half is wrong here. The fish are real, enormous, and in
Bristol Bay's case entirely wild and self-sustaining. **The Alaska Republic's food problem is not protein; it
is calories** — 276 lb/person/year of subsistence harvest covers 176% of protein need and 25% of caloric need.
**Fat and carbohydrate are the constraint, not meat.** [Derived from ADF&G data; the conclusion is mine.]

**Its industrial problem is real and severe:** no iron ore, no verified cement, germanium refined abroad, and
every large mine dependent on a road, a port, or a mill it cannot rebuild.

**And losing the Panhandle costs it more than a coastline.** It loses the Inside Passage, the largest primary
silver mine in the US, and the best-documented limestone. Its ports face the Gulf of Alaska and the Bering Sea
— open, violent water — rather than protected channels.

**The most useful single framing:** this is a territory that can **feed itself protein, mine its own precious
metals with hand tools, and heat itself with Healy coal** — while being unable to make steel, cement, or
anything requiring a smelter. It is rich in exactly the things that do not need industry, and poor in exactly
the things that do. [Reasoning, not a sourced claim.]

  $4.8 B (all mined commodities incl. coal) — different baskets, not contradictions, but quoted interchangeably
- **Barite:** 23.2 Mt statewide (DGGS Table 5) vs. 1.1 billion short tons at Anarraaq alone
- **2026 GOA pollock TAC:** 139,498 mt vs. 134,118 mt (139,498 favored on internal consistency)
