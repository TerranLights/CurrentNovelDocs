# PART 1 — OIL AND GAS

**This is the largest gap in the base file and it is treated first and at the greatest length.** Oil is not one
industry among several in Alaska — it is the state's fiscal foundation, its largest single piece of
infrastructure, and the thing that makes the rest of the modern Alaskan economy financially possible.

## The North Slope

**Prudhoe Bay was confirmed on 12 March 1968** by the Prudhoe Bay State #1 well, drilled by Atlantic Richfield
(ARCO) and Humble Oil (later Exxon), with ARCO as operating partner. It remains **the largest oil field in
North America**, covering **213,543 acres (86,418 ha)** and originally holding roughly **25 billion barrels of
oil in place**. Production could not begin until the pipeline was finished: **first oil 20 June 1977.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prudhoe_Bay_Oil_Field

- **Producing formation:** the Triassic Ivishak sandstone of the Sadlerochit Group, a stacked fan-delta and
  alluvial-fan complex. Early in field life the oil-bearing sandstone was **up to 600 feet thick**; the average
  oil zone is now about **60 feet.**
- **North Slope oil production peaked in 1988 at about 2 million bbl/d**, of which Greater Prudhoe Bay was
  roughly **1.5 million bbl/d.** Prudhoe itself was down to **~285,000 bbl/d by December 2006** and around
  **319,000 bbl/d in early 2023.**
- **Cumulative:** roughly 11 billion barrels produced from the field 1977–2005; ~12 billion barrels as of 2013.
  BP's 2006 estimate put **~2 billion barrels of recoverable oil remaining** with then-current technology.
- Prudhoe also holds an enormous **associated gas cap — on the order of 26 trillion cubic feet recoverable.**
  **Critically, this gas has never been commercially exported.** Alaska's largest single hydrocarbon resource
  is, functionally, stranded, because no gas pipeline or LNG line to market has ever been built. ⚠️ **The
  specific claim that the gas is stripped of liquids and reinjected to maintain reservoir pressure is
  standard industry description but was not verified against a primary source in this pass.** What *is*
  verified is that the Alaska Department of Revenue excludes "major gas sales" from its forecast entirely,
  pending a final investment decision that has never come.
- **Ownership consolidated dramatically:** BP sold all of its Alaska assets to **Hilcorp Energy** in a deal
  that closed in 2020, including BP's **49.1% share of TAPS.** Partners are ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips.

collapse scenario, the question is whether the collapse arrives before or after they come online — and the
answer changes the pipeline's margin above its shutdown threshold by a factor of roughly two.

## The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS)

TAPS is the single largest piece of built infrastructure in the territory and the most consequential thing in
this file. It is privately owned by the **Alyeska Pipeline Service Company**, formed in 1970 and now
majority-owned by Hilcorp.

**Physical description** (Alyeska primary sources: https://www.alyeska-pipe.com/taps-facts/ and the 2024 TAPS
Factbook, https://www.alyeska-pipe.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/24-APSC-0352-FACTBOOK-UPDATE-FOR-WEB-FINAL1_AA.pdf):

- **800 miles (1,288 km) of 48-inch welded carbon steel pipe**, Prudhoe Bay to Valdez, plus several hundred
  miles of North Slope feeder lines, 12 designed pump stations, and the Valdez Marine Terminal.
- **The straight-line distance is only 639 miles.** The line was deliberately built "11 miles too long" and
  laid in a zig-zag, because air temperature along the route swings **over 150 °F between winter and summer**
  and an 1,800-foot section of empty pipe could shift **12 feet.** The pipe **rests on its supports and is not
  welded or bolted to them.**
- **Design modes: 420 miles aboveground, 376 miles conventional burial, about 4 miles "special burial."** Where
  the ground was thaw-stable, the line was simply buried. Where the permafrost was thaw-unstable, burying a
  pipe carrying 120 °F oil would have melted the ground out from under it — so it was insulated and raised on
  **more than 78,000 vertical support members (VSMs)** designed to resist frost-jacking. Many VSMs carry
  **thermosyphon heat pipes** — passive devices that pull heat out of the ground and radiate it from finned
  tops, keeping the permafrost frozen with no power input.
- **178 mainline valves. 34 major river or stream crossings and nearly 500 minor ones.** Highest point
  **Atigun Pass, 4,739 feet.** Steepest grade **145% (55°) at Thompson Pass.** Pipe wall thickness 0.462 in
  over 466 miles and 0.562 in over 334 miles. **Linefill: 9,059,057 barrels** — that is how much oil is sitting
  inside the pipe at any moment.
- **Route towns:** Deadhorse, Coldfoot, Livengood, Fox, Fairbanks, North Pole, Delta Junction, Glennallen,
  Valdez, running alongside the Dalton, Elliott and Richardson Highways.

**Caribou crossings — an actual engineered feature, not a talking point.** The Factbook counts approximately
**579 animal crossings** on the mainline: **554 elevated** (minimum 10 feet of clearance under the pipe),
**23 buried** sections of about 200 feet each, and **2 buried and actively refrigerated** crossings at MP 645
and MP 649. TAPS crosses the range of the **Central Arctic Herd** on the North Slope and the **Nelchina Herd**
in the Copper River Basin. A further refrigerated buried crossing carries the line under the Glenn Highway at
Glennallen, and two insulated buried sections (~1 mile) at Atigun Pass protect against rockslides and
avalanches.

**Earthquakes.** TAPS is designed to survive **magnitude 8.0 at the Denali Fault** and between 5.5 and 8.5
elsewhere on the route. At the Denali crossing the line sits on **slider supports** rated for **20 feet of
lateral and 5 feet of vertical** ground movement. This was tested for real: the **magnitude 7.9 Denali
earthquake of 3 November 2002** ruptured directly beneath the pipeline, moving the ground **14 feet
horizontally and 2.5 feet vertically.** The pipe did not break. Some slider supports were damaged and the line
was shut down for **just over 66 hours** as a precaution. It is a genuinely well-engineered object.

**Pump stations.** The original design was 12 stations with four pumps each. **PS 11 was never built.** Eight
were running at start-up (1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12), rising to 11 by 1980 as throughput climbed. As throughput
fell they were shut down one by one:

| PS 5 | Operating as a relief station |
| PS 2 | Ramped down 1 July 1997 |
| PS 6 | Ramped down 8 August 1997 |

**Stations 1, 3, 4 and 9 have been converted from diesel to electric drive and are automated**, controlled
remotely from the Operations Control Center in Anchorage. PS 1 draws power from the North Slope Central Power
Facility; **PS 9 buys its power from Golden Valley Electric Association**, the Fairbanks-area utility — i.e.
the pipeline is a customer of the Railbelt grid. PS 5 and PS 7 still run legacy equipment (diesel/solar
turbine generators). Crews are typically **6 to 25 people per station** on one-week-on/one-week-off or
two-weeks-on/two-weeks-off rotations. Permanent living quarters exist only at PS 4, 5 and 6. Crude storage:
**PS 1 holds 420,000 bbl, PS 5 holds 150,000 bbl, all others 55,000 bbl.** PS 1, 3, 5 and 6 sit on
**refrigerated foundations.**

**Valdez Marine Terminal.** **Eighteen storage tanks totaling 9.18 million barrels**, each 250 feet in diameter
and 63.3 feet tall, typically kept about **85% full (~7.8 million barrels).** Three on-site power plants of
**12.5 MW each.** Four berths for mooring plus two loading berths. **More than 19,000 tankers loaded since
1977.**

**Maintenance is pig-driven.** Scraper pigs remove wax from the pipe walls; magnetic-flux and ultrasonic
"smart" pigs look for corrosion; geometry pigs look for bending and buckling. Pig launcher/receiver facilities
are at **PS 1, PS 4, PS 9 and the Valdez terminal**, plus a "pig washing" station added at PS 8. The line is
also surveyed by air several times a day.

whole low-flow problem in one sentence.

## TAPS Minimum Viable Throughput — the collapse threshold

**This deserves its own section because it is a genuine engineered cliff, not a gradual economic squeeze.**

**The mechanism.** TAPS was built to move *warm* oil through an Arctic environment. Oil leaves the reservoir at
about **120 °F** and has cooled to roughly **111 °F** by the time it reaches Pump Station 1. Heat is lost to
the ground and air along the way, and the only thing that keeps the line warm is the oil's own thermal mass
plus friction. Cut the flow rate and three things happen at once:

1. **Transit time lengthens** — 4.5 days at peak flow, 18 days now — so the oil has far longer to shed heat.
2. **Water drops out.** Crude carries small amounts of entrained water. At low velocity the flow is no longer
   turbulent enough to keep it suspended, so it separates and pools at low points in the line. That water is
   corrosive. In cold weather, and especially during shutdowns, **it can freeze.** Ice in the line can jam a
   pig, and a jammed pig forces a shutdown and physical intervention on an 800-mile line in winter.
3. **Wax precipitates.** Alaska North Slope crude contains **up to 2% wax by volume.** Two separate failure
   modes: wax crystals deposit on the pipe wall whenever **the wall is below about 70 °F and colder than the
   oil**, and wax drops out of the oil itself as it cools and settles on the bottom of the pipe. Both get worse
   with lower velocity, less turbulence and colder crude. Heavy wax loading makes cleaning pigs harder to run
   and raises the risk of plugging.

**The numbers, from Alyeska's own Flow Assurance overview** (April 2021),
https://www.alyeska-pipe.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Flow-Assurance-April-2021.pdf:

> - Earlier flow assurance research examined operational issues **at flow rates above 300,000 bbl/d.**
> - Research continues on operating **below 300,000 bbl/d.**
> - Analysis "to date suggests that **with additional investment** it may be technically possible to safely
>   operate down to annualized throughput rates **as low as 200,000 bbl/d**."
> - And the crucial qualifier, in Alyeska's own framing: **"Technical capability does not necessarily equate to
>   economic viability; the long-term sustainability of TAPS may ultimately be limited by per barrel
>   transportation costs."**

**Other published thresholds, which genuinely conflict and should not be smoothed over:**

- **Alyeska's 2011 "Low Flow Impact Study" (LoFIS)** concluded the minimum for the pipeline **as it then
  existed** was **300,000–350,000 bbl/d in winter** — winter being the binding case.
- **An internal BP study** argued that with heaters installed along the route the floor could go as low as
  **70,000 bbl/d.** In late 2011 the **Alaska Superior Court ruled Alyeska's low-flow study invalid and found
  for the BP figure** — a ruling that **increased the taxed property value of the pipeline ninefold.**
- A **Natural Resources Defense Council** analysis cited in the same litigation put the shoring-up cost at
  about **$0.8 billion** to extend the line's life enough to recover an additional ~$28 billion of oil from
  existing wells, and stated plainly that it distrusted LoFIS's assumptions.
- Other studies quoted in the literature give **70,000–100,000 bbl/d** as the floor for the line as built;
  replacing the 48-inch pipe from Prudhoe to Fairbanks with a **20-inch line** and railing the oil from there
  would allow as little as **45,000 bbl/d.**

**⚠️ Read the conflict correctly.** The minimum-flow number is **legally and financially contested, not
settled engineering** — the taxable value of the pipeline depends on how long it can be operated, so the owner
has an incentive to state a *high* floor and the tax authority an incentive to state a *low* one. The honest
position is:

- **Without active intervention, the line has problems below roughly 300,000–350,000 bbl/d in winter.**
- **With sustained investment — heaters, freeze depressants, corrosion inhibitors, redesigned pigs, added wax
  management facilities — the operator's own current estimate is about 200,000 bbl/d.**
- **Claims down to 70,000 bbl/d assume a continuously funded, continuously maintained, continuously staffed
  heating and chemical-treatment program.**

**Mitigations already in place, all of which are things a collapse removes:**

- **Added heat.** Crude is recirculated at **Pump Stations 3, 4, 7 and 9** purely to generate frictional heat.
  A **mainline heating system at Milepost 238** started up in 2020, and **skid-mounted mobile heaters** are
  staged at two other locations. **PS 8 no longer pumps at all — it exists to heat crude.**
- **A modified pigging program** — pigs run less often, new pig designs, a pig-washing station at PS 8.
- **Contingency plans for freeze depressants** and extended use of corrosion inhibitors in the mainline.
- **High-definition video cameras inside the line** (e.g. at PS 9) to measure at what velocities wax and water
  actually settle out.

**What this means for a collapse story, stated plainly.** TAPS is not a passive tube. At today's flow rates it
is an **actively thermally managed system** that requires electricity, gas or diesel for heating, a chemicals
supply chain, a pigging program, and several hundred trained people. **It has a hard floor, and the floor
rises as the maintenance falls away.** Strip the heaters and the chemicals and the floor goes back up toward
300,000+ bbl/d — which is **below the 2026 throughput of ~458,000 bbl/d, but not by much**, and it is a
number the North Slope will reach on its own decline curve within decades. And the failure mode is not a
graceful ramp-down: it is wax and ice accumulating until a pig jams or the line gels, in winter, somewhere in
the Brooks Range.

**One more detail that matters enormously: the line is full.** **Linefill is 9.06 million barrels.** A
permanent, unplanned shutdown does not leave an empty pipe — it leaves nine million barrels of waxy crude
cooling to ambient inside 800 miles of steel. ⚠️ **What actually happens to TAPS in an uncontrolled long-term
shutdown — whether it can ever be restarted, and over what timescale it becomes permanently plugged — was not
found in any source in this pass. It is the single most important unanswered question in this file.** By
regulation Alyeska is required to *remove* the pipeline at end of life, funded by a levy on shippers; as of
2004 those funds were commingled and the required amount was disputed.

## Cook Inlet — the other basin, and the one that is actually running out

Cook Inlet is Alaska's *first* producing province, in production since the late 1950s and early 1960s — older
than the North Slope, far smaller, and physically separate. It has never fed TAPS. Its oil goes to the
refinery at Nikiski; its gas heats and powers Southcentral Alaska.

**Oil is now negligible.** The Alaska Department of Revenue's Spring 2026 forecast puts **Cook Inlet crude at
7,600 bbl/d in FY 2026**, declining to about **4,500–5,000 bbl/d across the 2030s** — against 459,200 bbl/d
for the North Slope. It rounds to noise in the state's oil numbers.
https://tax.alaska.gov/programs/documentviewer/viewer.aspx?2001r

**Gas is the thing that matters, and the gas is running short.** From ENSTAR Natural Gas — the utility itself,
which is the most direct primary source available on this:
https://www.enstarnaturalgas.com/cook-inlet-natural-gas-supply/

- Southcentral Alaska has run on Cook Inlet gas for **more than 60 years**. ENSTAR alone serves **more than
  156,000 homes and businesses.**
- **Production has been declining for years**, and ENSTAR states that producers "have indicated they are
  unable or unwilling to commit additional firm supplies needed to meet future demand." The gas is not gone;
  the *cheap, contractible, already-drilled* gas is.
- **Winter 2025–26: ENSTAR drew on stored gas more heavily than at any point in company history**, during an
  extended cold snap.
- Mitigations already taken: an expanded storage agreement with **Cook Inlet Natural Gas Storage Alaska
  (CINGSA)** — a **$67 million** expansion adding **2 billion cubic feet** of storage — and a **five-year gas
  purchase agreement with Furie / HEX Operating** beginning in 2026. ENSTAR says it spent **$4.6 million** on
  engineering studies, demand forecasts and global RFPs evaluating every option.
- **The near-term answer is importing LNG into the state that exports it.** ENSTAR has partnered with
  **Glenfarne** to build an **LNG *receiving* terminal on the Kenai Peninsula, first deliveries targeted
  2029** — deliberately designed so it can be switched over to North Slope gas if the Alaska LNG pipeline is
  ever built.
- **The long-term answer is the Alaska LNG Project** — a gas pipeline from the North Slope to tidewater, which
  has been proposed in one form or another for fifty years and has never reached a final investment decision.
  The state's own revenue forecast explicitly excludes it from projections "to be conservative."
- **About 80% of an ENSTAR customer's bill is the cost of the gas itself**, so a supply squeeze passes straight
  through to households.

**The Alaska Energy Authority frames the same fact from the electricity side:** the Cook Inlet basin "has
supported 80% of the power generation" for the Railbelt plus most of the heating load, and local supplies are
"getting harder to find" with volumes "behind pipe and available for market consumption... dwindling."
(*Statewide Railbelt Energy Vision*, AEA, 19 February 2026.)

## Refining — Alaska can turn its own crude into fuel, and this matters enormously

**This was flagged as a critical question and the answer is yes, with a large and important caveat.** From
EIA's *Refinery Capacity Report*, Table 3, capacity of operable refineries as of **1 January 2026**:
https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/refinerycapacity/table3.pdf

| Refiner | Location | Atmospheric crude distillation, bbl/calendar day | bbl/stream day |
|---|---|---|---|
| ConocoPhillips Alaska | Prudhoe Bay | 15,000 | 16,000 |
| Hilcorp North Slope | Prudhoe Bay | 6,500 | 8,000 |
| Petro Star | North Pole (Fairbanks) | 25,000 | 25,600 |
| Petro Star | Valdez | 62,500 | 65,000 |
| Tesoro Alaska (Marathon) | Kenai / Nikiski | 68,000 | 72,000 |
| **Alaska total** | | **177,000** | **186,600** |

**177,000 bbl/d of crude distillation capacity against roughly 460,000 bbl/d of throughput** — Alaska can
process about **38% of what it produces.** By US standards these are small plants, but for a territory of
740,000 people that is a very large amount of fuel.

**The caveat, and it is the whole story:** look at the downstream columns in the same table.

- **Vacuum distillation: 26,000 bbl/d — all of it at Kenai.**
- **Catalytic cracking: zero, statewide.** Delayed coking, fluid coking, visbreaking: **zero.**
- **Catalytic hydrocracking: 13,500 bbl/d — all at Kenai. Catalytic reforming: 13,500 bbl/d — all at Kenai.**
- **Diesel desulfurization: 23,500 bbl/d — 11,500 at Kenai and 12,000 at Valdez.**

**Translation: only the Kenai refinery is a conversion refinery.** It has the reformer that makes gasoline
octane and the hydrocracker that upgrades heavy fractions. **The North Slope and Petro Star plants are
topping refineries.** They take crude — in Petro Star's case, crude drawn *off TAPS* — boil off the jet fuel,
diesel and heating-oil cuts, and **return the unwanted residual to the pipeline.** That model is elegant and
cheap and it depends entirely on the pipeline continuing to run.

**Consequences, stated plainly for the collapse case:**

1. **Diesel, jet fuel and heating oil can be made in Alaska at several sites.** These are the fuels that
   actually matter for survival: generators, boats, aircraft, heating stoves, trucks.
2. **Gasoline effectively comes from one plant, at Nikiski on the Kenai Peninsula.** Lose Kenai and the
   territory loses its domestic gasoline supply. ⚠️ How much of Alaska's gasoline is *currently* imported
   rather than refined at Kenai was **not verified.**
3. **Petro Star's two refineries are parasitic on TAPS.** Valdez (62,500 bbl/d, the second-largest in the
   state) sits at the pipeline terminus; North Pole sits beside it near Fairbanks. If TAPS stops, they have no
   feedstock — and they also lose their disposal route for the residual they do not want.
4. **Ownership is worth noting:** Petro Star is a subsidiary of **Arctic Slope Regional Corporation (ASRC)**,
   the North Slope Iñupiat regional corporation — i.e. two of Alaska's five refineries are Alaska
   Native-owned. ⚠️ **Confirm the ownership against an ASRC primary source before relying on it.**
5. **One refinery has already closed in living memory.** The **Flint Hills refinery at North Pole** does not
   appear in the 2026 EIA table; it ceased refining in 2014. ⚠️ The closure and its sulfolane groundwater
   contamination are widely reported but were **not verified against a primary source in this pass.**

## The Alaska Permanent Fund and state revenue — and a correction to the obvious story

**The obvious story is "oil pays for Alaska." That was true, and it is now only half true**, which is a more
interesting fact for a collapse scenario than the cliché.

**The fund.** Established 1976 by Article 9, Section 15 of the Alaska Constitution under Governor Jay Hammond;
managed since 1980 by the **Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation (APFC)**. Seeded with an initial **$734,000** in
1977. Current value, from APFC's own reporting:

**Total fund value $87,898,600,000 as of 31 July 2026**, split between a constitutionally protected principal
(**$59.4 billion of permanent deposits plus $14.3 billion of unrealized gains**) and an Earnings Reserve
Account (**$5.7 billion uncommitted, $4.1 billion committed to the POMV draw, $2.7 billion unrealized gains,

Permanent Fund is not a stockpile of anything. It is $88 billion of claims on companies, governments and
buildings that are mostly not in Alaska.** In a genuine systemic collapse it is the single largest asset in
the territory and also the most completely worthless.

## The Matanuska-Susitna Valley

The Mat-Su, about 45 miles northeast of Anchorage around Palmer and Wasilla, is Alaska's agricultural core —
glacial outwash soils, a maritime-moderated climate, and the only sizable block of arable land within reach of
a port, a railroad and a highway.

**The Matanuska Colony, 1935 — the New Deal resettlement.** Created by the **Federal Emergency Relief
Administration** as one of several rural rehabilitation colonies. **203 families** were recruited from
**Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan** — chosen for their supposed fitness for a subarctic climate, and in
practice chosen because they were on relief. Each family was to get a **40-acre plot**; **241,332 acres** were
initially set aside under Executive Order 6957, with 7,780 more added later. Land sold from **$5 per acre**
uncleared on a **30-year schedule at 3% interest**. The government built houses and barns and shipped the
families north aboard the **USMS *North Star***. Total cost roughly **$5 million.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matanuska_Valley_Colony

**It largely failed, and the reasons are the reasons Alaska agriculture is still small.**

- Colonists arrived in **May 1935** to a tent city and no permanent buildings; a **measles epidemic** swept
  the camp within weeks.
- The land was worse than surveyed: coarse glacial gravel, rock outcrops, uneven terrain, so little arable
  ground in some parcels that they had to be doubled in size.
- **Short growing season, high freight costs, distant markets.** That is the whole of it.
- **Over half the colonists had left by 1940. Only 31% of the originals remained in 1948. By 1965 there were
  20 families still farming.** Many who stayed in Alaska switched to fishing, mining, trade or wartime
  military construction.

**The giant vegetables are real and are a genuine consequence of latitude.** At the summer solstice the
Matanuska Valley gets on the order of **19 hours of direct sunlight plus long twilight**, and Fairbanks
nearly 22 — so cool-season crops (cabbage, kale, pumpkins, rutabaga, chard) that keep photosynthesizing at
low light accumulate extraordinary mass in a season of only about 100 frost-free days. The Alaska State Fair
in **Palmer** has produced repeated world records for cabbage and other giant vegetables. ⚠️ **Specific record
weights and dates were not verified in this pass — do not quote a number without checking it.** The
underlying mechanism (long photoperiod, cool nights, cool-season species) is sound; the record-keeping is not
sourced here.

## Delta Junction and the Barley Project

**The Delta Barley Project is Alaska's clearest example of an agricultural plan defeated by transport rather
than by agronomy.** In the late 1970s and early 1980s the state designated the Tanana Valley east of Delta
Junction as an agricultural development area and sold large tracts to prospective farmers. The plan:
**grow barley, move it by a planned rail extension to Valdez, and export it to Asia.**

**Farms were established. A grain storage facility was built at Valdez. The railroad was never built.** With
no bulk transport to tidewater, the export premise collapsed; political support and funding ended with a
change of governors. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Junction,_Alaska

**What the land does now:** mostly pasture and hay, with barley, potatoes, carrots and some wheat grown at
limited commercial scale. Some farmers turned to cattle — supported by a local abattoir, **Delta Meat and
Sausage Company** — and the area now also produces **yak, bison and elk.** The **90,000-acre Delta Junction
Bison Range**, established in the early 1980s and managed by ADF&G, exists specifically to hold bison off the
farm fields in autumn; the herd is kept at several hundred animals by hunting permit.

**The lesson generalizes, and it is the lesson of Alaska agriculture entire: the land can grow the crop. The
problem is always getting it to a market, and the market is always somewhere else.**

## Reindeer herding on the Seward Peninsula

**Reindeer in Alaska are an introduced, deliberately established industry, not a native one.** Caribou are
native; domesticated reindeer were imported.

- **The Alaska Reindeer Service** was established by Congressional action on **3 March 1893**, with a first
  appropriation of **$6,000**, following a private subscription of **$2,146** that bought **16 deer in 1891
  and 171 in 1892.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Reindeer_Service
- **1,280 reindeer were shipped from Siberia to Alaska between 1892 and 1902**, aboard the revenue cutter
  *Bear*, until the Russian government withdrew the permit.
- **Port Clarence on the Seward Peninsula was the landing point**, and the **Teller Reindeer Station** became
  the distribution base from which herds were driven out to establish new centers across northern Alaska.
- **Sámi herders were recruited from Norway** to teach herding. The program ran through the Bureau of
  Education as a vocational-training scheme: Iñupiat and Yup'ik apprentices served four-year terms, receiving
  animals each year, and graduated to herder status with their own herds and their own apprentices.
- The Canadian government bought **3,000 Alaska reindeer in the 1920s** and drove them from Nome to Reindeer
  Station, NWT — a drive that took **five years.**

⚠️ **The modern industry was not researched.** Current herd numbers, the number of active herders, the effect
of the Western Arctic Caribou Herd absorbing domestic reindeer (a well-known and repeatedly documented
problem — wild caribou passing through draw domestic reindeer away with them), meat production volumes, and
the role of the **Reindeer Herders Association** and the **UAF Reindeer Research Program** are all **gaps.**
Starting points: the University of Alaska Fairbanks Reindeer Research Program and the USDA/BIA reindeer
program records.

## How much of Alaska's food is imported?

**The commonly cited figure is that Alaska imports about 95% of its food. ⚠️ THIS COULD NOT BE VERIFIED IN
THIS PASS and should be flagged wherever it is used.** It traces to Alaska food-security literature of the
2010s (most often to Ken Meter and Megan Phillips Goldenberg's work for the state, and to Alaska Food Policy
Council material), but no primary source was retrieved here.

**What can be said on verified arithmetic instead, which is stronger:**

- **Total Alaska farm-gate output is $90.85 million a year (2022 Census), of which roughly $21.7 million is
  ornamental and roughly $43.5 million is mariculture.** Terrestrial food agriculture is on the order of
  **$25 million.**
- Statewide **vegetable acreage is 745 acres**, potato acreage **321 acres**, and protected food-growing
  space about **eleven acres.**
- Statewide grain production is **147,812 bushels of barley and 62,964 bushels of oats** — roughly 3,500 and
  1,000 tons respectively, or about **12 pounds of grain per Alaskan per year, before milling losses, and most
  of it is feed barley.**
- Cattle inventory statewide is **16,840 head.** At a generous 500 lb of retail beef per animal and a
  sustainable offtake of perhaps 25% of the herd, that is on the order of **2 million pounds of beef a year,
  or under 3 pounds per Alaskan.** [Arithmetic is mine.]

**Those verified numbers make the conclusion inescapable without needing the 95% figure: Alaska's terrestrial
food production is a rounding error against its consumption.** The food that is *not* imported is
overwhelmingly **fish and wild game**, which is the subject of the base file, not this one.

**The honest framing for the author:** the "95% imported" figure is about **groceries**, and is probably
roughly right for the retail food supply. It is **not** a statement about total calories consumed, because it
excludes subsistence harvest, which in rural Alaska supplies **276 lb per person per year** (base file,
ADF&G). The correct way to hold both is: *the commercial food system is almost entirely imported; the
non-commercial food system is almost entirely wild.*

## Soil, permafrost and the physical limit

⚠️ **This subsection is reasoning from the Census data plus the general literature, not from a retrieved soil
survey. Treat it as framing, not fact.**

- **Alaska's arable land is not distributed like its landmass.** The cropland is essentially three pockets:
  the **Matanuska-Susitna Valley**, the **Tanana Valley** (Fairbanks and Delta Junction), and the **Kenai
  Peninsula** — all on glacial or river deposits, all within the road and rail corridor.
- **Permafrost is the hard boundary.** North and west of the Interior, discontinuous and then continuous
  permafrost makes tillage agriculture impossible at scale; you cannot deepen a root zone that is frozen 18
  inches down. This is why the agricultural map and the road map are the same map.
- **The growing season is roughly 100–120 frost-free days in the Mat-Su and shorter in the Interior**, with
  intense photoperiod compensation. ⚠️ Specific frost-date data **not sourced here.**
- **Alaska soils are typically acidic, low in organic matter in the Interior, cold, and slow to warm in
  spring.** Cold soils delay germination and suppress nitrogen mineralization — which is why Alaska yields
  (barley at 32 bu/acre) run well below temperate norms even where the season is long enough.

## The Railbelt — the one real grid

**The Railbelt is named after the Alaska Railroad, whose route it largely follows.** It runs from **Homer on
the Kenai Peninsula, through Anchorage and the Mat-Su, north to Fairbanks and southeast to Delta Junction**,
and it serves **about 75% of Alaska's population** (the AEA figure; Wikipedia says two-thirds — both are in
the right range). It was assembled out of three originally separate local systems — Kenai, Anchorage/Mat-Su,
and Healy-to-Fairbanks — and it shows: it is a long, thin, radial system with limited redundancy.

**Six owners along the line:** the **Alaska Energy Authority**, **Chugach Electric Association**, **Golden
Valley Electric Association** (Fairbanks), **Homer Electric Association**, **Matanuska Electric
Association**, and **Seward Electric Association**. Source: AEA, *Statewide Railbelt Energy Vision*,
19 February 2026,
https://www.akenergyauthority.org/Portals/0/Home/2026.02.19%20AEA%20Statewide%20Railbelt%20Energy%20Vision%20(Optimized%20Final).pdf

AEA's own assessment: *"Alaska lacks sufficient electrical transmission infrastructure to provide robust
reliable, redundant, and affordable power distribution in the Railbelt."* The **Alaska Intertie** links the
Anchorage and Fairbanks halves. A **Railbelt Transmission Organization (RTO)** was created by HB 307 to
recover backbone transmission costs system-wide and enable economic dispatch.

**Major Railbelt plants** (capacities from
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Alaska):

- **George M. Sullivan Generation Plant 2**, Anchorage — **346.9 MW**, combined and simple cycle gas,
  1979/2017 and 1984.
- **Beluga**, west side of Cook Inlet — **312.4 MW**, six simple-cycle gas turbines, 1968–1978.
- **Southcentral Power Project**, Anchorage — **203.9 MW**, 3×1 combined cycle gas, 2013.
- **Eklutna Generation Station**, Chugiak — **171 MW**, ten reciprocating gas engines, 2015.
- **Bernice Lake**, Nikiski — 76.7 MW, three simple-cycle gas units, 1971–81.
- **Healy Power Plant** (coal, Usibelli-fed) — **75 MW** (Unit 1, 25 MW, 1967; Unit 2, 50 MW, 1998).
- **Chena Power Plant**, Fairbanks (Aurora Energy, coal) — 25.7 MW, units dating from 1952 and 1975.
- **Golden Valley BESS**, Fairbanks — **40 MW battery**, 2003, one of the first grid-scale batteries anywhere.

**The nation's only new coal plant built since 2015 is the University of Alaska Fairbanks combined heat and
power plant, which started up in February 2020.** That is a fair emblem of the Interior's energy position.

**Bradley Lake** is the anchor. AEA-owned, **$400 million**, completed **1991**; a **125-foot concrete-faced
rockfill dam**, four diversion structures, a **3.5-mile power tunnel and vertical shaft**, powerhouse and
substation, and about **20 miles of transmission line.** **From 1995 through 2020 it averaged 392,000 MWh a
year at about $0.04 per kWh** and supplies roughly **10% of all Railbelt power.** Day-to-day operation is run
by a Bradley Lake Project Management Committee of the participating utilities under a Power Sales Agreement.
https://www.akenergyauthority.org/What-We-Do/Railbelt-Energy/Bradley-Lake-Hydroelectric-Project

**⚑ CASCADIAN — Snettisham (78.2 MW) serves Juneau and is a loss to the Alaska Republic**, as are Blue Lake
(Sitka) and Swan Lake and Tyee Lake (Ketchikan/Wrangell). **The Panhandle is where Alaska's best hydrology
is** — high precipitation, steep coastal mountains, short penstocks — and all of it goes to Cascadia. What
the Alaska Republic keeps is Bradley Lake, Eklutna, Cooper Lake, Solomon Gulch, and Terror Lake on Kodiak.

**Projects in the pipeline:**

- **Bradley Lake Expansion (Dixon Diversion)** — diverting additional water into the existing reservoir,
  expected to **raise output by about 50%** and offset an estimated **1.5 billion cubic feet of natural gas a
  year.** Requires the Railbelt to be upgraded to **230 kV** to move the power north.
- **Cook Inlet PowerLink (CIPLink)** — a **high-voltage DC** link **Nikiski to Beluga**, about **40 miles of
  subsea cable plus 8 miles overland** with converter stations, giving **200 MW of bidirectional flow**, N-1
  redundancy, grid-forming and black-start capability. AEA calls it "shovel-ready."
- **Susitna-Watana** — **the Railbelt's largest hydroelectric potential and it has not started.** A remote dam
  site on the **Susitna River, about 80 river miles upstream of Talkeetna and 30 miles above Devils Canyon**
  (which is a natural anadromous fish barrier, an important siting argument). **Three turbines, 459 MW
  nominal, 2,800 GWh a year — more than 50% of current Railbelt demand.** Two transmission corridors, one
  north to the Denali Highway and one west to the Alaska Intertie, one of which would also be the access
  road. AEA was authorized to seek a FERC license in 2011; licensing was **suspended in 2017** for fiscal
  reasons after FERC's updated Study Plan Determination on 58 required environmental studies; the project was
  put in abeyance, taken out of abeyance in 2019, and Railbelt utilities have formally asked to resume
  federal licensing. **Still: not started.**

## Wind, solar, geothermal, biomass

**Wind is small and, remarkably, shrinking.** 123,366 MWh in 2024, down from 151,957 MWh in 2014. The main
installations:

- **Eva Creek**, near Ferry (Golden Valley) — **24.6 MW, 12 turbines, 2013.** The largest in Alaska.
- **Fire Island**, off Anchorage — **18 MW, 11 turbines, 2012.**
- **Pillar Mountain**, Kodiak Island — **9 MW, 6 turbines, 2009 and 2012.**
- **Kotzebue** — **4.5 MW across 19 turbines**, installed in stages from **1997 through 2012.** Kotzebue
  Electric Association was the first utility in Alaska to run utility-scale wind and is the reference case
  for Arctic wind-diesel hybrids.
- Numerous small village turbines (Gambell, Quinhagak and others), typically 0.4–1 MW.

**Kodiak is the standout success and deserves its own paragraph.** **Kodiak Electric Association runs an
isolated island grid on which Terror Lake hydro supplies roughly 85% of generation**, with the balance from
Pillar Mountain wind plus a **3 MW battery** and flywheel storage; KEA's stated target is to hold **98% of
energy sales** on renewables. https://www.kodiakelectric.com/ **A community of ~12,400 people on an island
runs essentially without fossil generation, using hydro plus wind plus storage.** That is the single most
useful Alaskan precedent in this file for a post-collapse energy system — and notably, both components are
mechanically simple and locally maintainable.

**Solar** is newly nonzero — 7,770 MWh in 2024, about 0.1% — from rooftop installations and the Mat-Su
utility-scale farm. Obviously seasonal to the point of uselessness in December.

**Biomass** is 43,004 MWh and falling; wood-fired district heat is used in some Interior communities.

⚠️ **Geothermal was not verified.** Alaska has real geothermal potential along the Aleutian arc (Makushin
Volcano near Unalaska, Mount Spurr) and operates a small low-temperature binary plant at **Chena Hot Springs**
near Fairbanks. **No capacities, generation figures or project statuses could be confirmed in this pass.**
Treat all geothermal claims as unsourced.

## Diesel dependence in the villages — the crux

**This is the most important section in Part 3 and the numbers are unusually good, because the state runs a
subsidy program that forces every village utility to report its fuel consumption every month.**

The **Power Cost Equalization (PCE)** program, established **1984**, reimburses rural utilities for credits
extended to residential and community-facility customers so that rural rates come down toward what Anchorage,
Fairbanks and Juneau pay. It is administered by AEA with rate-setting by the Regulatory Commission of Alaska,
and is funded from the earnings of the **PCE Endowment Fund** (5% of a three-year average market value) —
which, note, **is managed by the Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation.**
https://www.akenergyauthority.org/PCE

**Program scope:** **82,000 Alaskans in 193 communities** (AEA's current figure) — **81,996 people in 188
communities served by 82 utilities** in FY 2023, and about **81,000 in 188 communities** in FY 2025, with
roughly **$46 million disbursed** that year.

**The eligibility rule is itself a fact worth having:** to qualify, a utility must have used **diesel-fired
generators for more than 75% of its electrical consumption** in 1984. **PCE is, by definition, the diesel
village program.**

**The formula:** PCE pays **95% of eligible costs per kWh between a floor of 19.58 cents and a ceiling of
$1.00.** Costs below 19.58¢ and above $1.00 are not covered. **Maximum PCE credit: 75.97 cents per kWh.**
Residential customers get the credit on up to **750 kWh per month**; community facilities get up to **70 kWh
per month per resident** of the community.

**The **Alaska Village Electric Cooperative (AVEC)** alone serves **58 communities.** AEA also runs an
**Alaska Bulk Fuel Infrastructure Partnership** rebuilding **tank farms in ten rural communities**, which is a
reminder that the diesel system's weak points are not only the generators: they are the barge landing, the
tank farm, the fuel line, and the pumps.

**The logistics, which is the part that breaks first in a collapse.** Village fuel arrives either by **coastal
and river barge during an open-water season of a few months**, or, for communities without navigable water,
**by air.** A village fills its tank farm once or twice a year and lives off it. That means:

1. **There is a physical buffer** — a full tank farm is months of autonomy, not days.
2. **There is a hard annual deadline.** Miss the barge window (low water on the Yukon or Kuskokwim, early
   freeze-up, a missing vessel) and the alternative is air freight at several times the price, or nothing.
3. **The resupply chain terminates in a refinery.** In Alaska's case it can terminate at **Kenai, Valdez or
   North Pole** — which is a genuinely important resilience fact and is developed in Part 5.

## SETTLED — the capital is Whitehorse

**Author decision, 2026-09-22.** The section below framed the question; this is the answer.

**Whitehorse**, not Anchorage or Fairbanks, despite being far smaller than either. Three reasons, in order of
weight:

1. **It is the hinge of the country.** The only route between the Republic's eastern and western halves that
   never leaves national territory runs Inuvik → Dempster Highway → Dawson City → Klondike Highway →
   **Whitehorse**. Anchorage and Fairbanks both sit *beyond* that junction, at the far western end of a nation
   extending to Baffin Island. Whitehorse is the only serious candidate that can physically reach both halves
   of its own territory. See `Yellowknife - City Study and Intra-Republic Connections.md` and
   `Alaska Republic - Transport and Connectivity (supplement).md`.
2. **It is the only candidate that is not a nuclear target.** Anchorage has Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson;
   Fairbanks has Eielson, Fort Wainwright and Clear. Whitehorse has no major military installation — it was a
   civilian territorial capital in what had been Canada. In a 2083 counterforce exchange the two larger cities
   are struck and Whitehorse is not. ⚠️ **This reasoning is not sourced** — military infrastructure is on the
   open-items list as never researched — but it is the load-bearing argument and should be verified.
3. **It gives the capital an in-world history rather than a design rationale.** The Republic is not
   administered from Whitehorse because anyone selected it, but because **it is what survived.** A nation run
   from its third city because the first two are craters reads as consequence, not authorial convenience.

**What this commits the setting to**, and what each alternative would have meant instead: the three candidates
were not interchangeable. **Anchorage** would have made a maritime, Pacific-facing republic dependent on
seaborne trade — and on a Gulf of Alaska approach, since the Inside Passage is Cascadian. **Fairbanks** would
have made a continental one running on the Healy-coal-and-railroad loop, having given up on the sea. Choosing
**Whitehorse** makes it an *interior corridor* state: oriented along its own internal road network, closer to
the Cascadian border than to its own ports, and defined by the problem of holding together a territory whose
regions can only reach each other by water, ice, or a neighbor's roads.

⚠️ **The one gap that bears directly on this decision:** **Yukon electricity was never researched** — the
Whitehorse Rapids, Aishihik and Mayo hydro systems. Whether the capital has its own generation or is a diesel
dependency is exactly what separates a viable seat of government from a vulnerable one. **This is now the
highest-priority open research item for the Alaska Republic.**

## The capital problem — a live worldbuilding consequence

**⚑ Juneau — the state capital, seat of the legislature, and home of the Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation —
is Cascadian in this fiction. The Alaska Republic therefore has no capital, and no obvious one.**

This is not a cosmetic problem. Juneau holds the legislature, the governor's office, the state court system's
supreme seat, the state archives and library, and APFC. **All of it is on the wrong side of the line.** The
candidates and their arguments:

- **Anchorage (289,221)** — the only real city; 43% of the population; the airport, the port, the military
  installations, the Operations Control Center for TAPS, the utilities' headquarters. The obvious answer, and
  it makes the republic a city-state with a very large hinterland. Also the most exposed: it sits on Cook
  Inlet at the end of the gas supply that is running out, and on an active seismic zone.
- **Fairbanks (31,068 city / 97,083 borough)** — the Interior anchor, the university, the rail and highway
  hub, close to Usibelli coal and to two refineries, and the point where the road network branches toward
  Canada and the North Slope. Continental rather than coastal; defensible; cold.
- **Valdez (3,743)** — ice-free deep-water port, the TAPS terminus, a 62,500 bbl/d refinery, 9.18 million
  barrels of crude storage, and a road to the Interior. Tiny, but strategically the most valuable square mile
  in the territory.
- **Whitehorse (28,201)** — the Yukon's capital and the largest city in northern Canada, on the Alaska
  Highway, with existing territorial government machinery. Also the only candidate that is not in Alaska,
  which may be exactly the point in a federation that includes the Yukon, NWT and Nunavut.
- **Palmer or Wasilla (5,950 / 9,724)** — the agricultural valley, symbolically loaded (the Colony), and
  physically adjacent to Anchorage without being Anchorage.

**The genuinely interesting version of this problem is that the two best candidates optimize for different
republics.** Anchorage is the capital of a republic that is mostly a coastal city and its supply lines.
Fairbanks is the capital of a republic that is mostly an interior landmass with a road to Canada and coal in
the ground. Whitehorse is the capital of a republic that takes its Canadian half seriously. **Choosing one is
choosing what the Alaska Republic is.** [This is worldbuilding reasoning, not sourced fact — but the
underlying geography, populations and infrastructure are all sourced above.]

# PART 5 — POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

⚠️ **This Part is reasoning built on the sourced material above, plus the minerals and fisheries findings of
the base file. Where a claim is inference rather than citation it is marked. Arithmetic is shown so it can be
checked and disagreed with.**

## (a) Survives regardless

- **The oil is still in the ground.** Prudhoe, Kuparuk, Alpine, Willow, Pikka. Roughly 2 billion barrels of
  recoverable oil remained at Prudhoe on BP's 2006 estimate, and the fields have produced continuously since
  1977. Nothing about a collapse removes the resource. It removes the ability to lift, move and refine it.
- **Cook Inlet oil and gas** — small, shallow, near a refinery, near a city, and on a road. Structurally the
  most accessible hydrocarbons in the territory even though they are nearly exhausted commercially.
- **Hydroelectric water.** Bradley Lake's catchment, Eklutna's, Cooper Lake's, Terror Lake's, Solomon Gulch's.
  Rain and snow do not stop.
- **The Mat-Su, Tanana and Kenai arable soils.** 72,708 acres of cropland, glacially deposited, already
  cleared, already on the road and rail network. Small, but real, and the Matanuska Colony proved it can be
  farmed by people with hand tools and horses if they are willing to stay.
- **The photoperiod.** Nineteen to twenty-two hours of summer light is a permanent physical asset and it is
  why cool-season crops do extraordinary things in the Mat-Su.
- **The fish**, per the base file: Bristol Bay sockeye with zero hatchery component, the AYK runs, nearshore
  species, marine mammals.
- **Reindeer and caribou.** The reindeer herds on the Seward Peninsula are a domesticated meat and hide supply
  requiring no fuel, no infrastructure, and no imports.

## (b) Depends on maintained infrastructure — repairable with effort

- **The refineries, and this is the most important finding in this file after the TAPS threshold.** Alaska has
  **177,000 bbl/d of crude distillation capacity at five sites**, of which **Kenai (68,000 bbl/d) is a full
  conversion refinery** and the rest are topping plants. Topping plants are, by refinery standards, *simple*:
  a furnace, a distillation column, some heat exchange and pumps. **A society that can keep a coal-fired
  boiler and a pump running can keep a topping refinery running.** It cannot keep a catalytic reformer running
  without catalyst supply, hydrogen and precise process control.
  - **Therefore: diesel, jet fuel, kerosene and heating oil are recoverable. Gasoline probably is not.**
    That is an enormous distinction for a post-collapse society, because it pushes everything toward
    compression-ignition engines, boats, and heating stoves, and away from cars.
- **Village diesel, and the arithmetic is much friendlier than it first looks.** All 188 PCE communities burn
  **27,248,271 gallons of diesel a year** for electricity. That is **648,768 barrels a year, or about 1,780
  barrels a day.** The Petro Star refinery at **Valdez alone runs 62,500 bbl/d.** **The entire rural Alaskan
  electricity supply is under 3% of one refinery's throughput.** [Arithmetic mine.] Even at a modest diesel
  yield, a single topping plant fed by a trickle of crude could keep every village generator in Alaska
  running. **The binding constraint on village diesel is not refining capacity and never was — it is barge
  logistics, tank farms, and the ability to pay.**
- **Bradley Lake and the other hydro plants.** A 1991 concrete-faced rockfill dam with two turbines is a
  hundred-year asset if the powerhouse is maintained. **Eklutna has been running since 1955; Cooper Lake since
  1961.** Hydro generation is the most collapse-resistant electricity in the territory, and it is also the
  cheapest (~$0.04/kWh at Bradley Lake).
- **Kodiak as the working model.** An isolated island grid of ~12,400 people running on **85% hydro plus wind
  plus batteries and flywheels**, targeting 98% renewable. Mechanically simple, locally maintainable, already
  built. If any Alaskan community keeps the lights on indefinitely, it is Kodiak.
- **The Railbelt as a physical asset.** Transmission line, substations, and four large gas plants. The gas is
  the problem, not the wires.
- **The Mat-Su farm system.** 401 farms, 39,532 acres, already producing 48% of state agricultural sales.
  Under collapse, output per acre would fall (no fertilizer, no fuel, no imported seed) but acreage under
  cultivation could rise a great deal — there are 241,332 acres of Colony land, most of it never farmed.
- **Usibelli coal** (base file) — on the Parks Highway and the Alaska Railroad, shallow and strippable.

## (c) Vanishes entirely

- **The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System, and with it the North Slope.** Developed below.
- **The Alaska Permanent Fund.** $87.9 billion of public equities, bonds, private equity, real estate and
  infrastructure, managed from a city that is in another country in this fiction, invested in economies that
  are mostly not in this hemisphere. **It funds 59% of the state government today and it is worth exactly
  nothing in a systemic collapse.** The Permanent Fund Dividend — the mechanism that has underwritten the cash
  economy of rural Alaska for forty years — ends at the same moment.
- **The natural gas supply to Southcentral Alaska.** Cook Inlet is already short; the plan of record is to
  **import LNG from overseas beginning in 2029.** An LNG import terminal is exactly the kind of asset that has
  no post-collapse function at all. **Anchorage's heating and its power generation both rest on a supply that
  the utility itself says cannot be sustained from local production.**
- **Imported food.** With terrestrial food farming at roughly $25 million of farm-gate output for 739,000
  people, essentially the whole commercial food supply is imported and essentially all of it stops.
- **Air resupply.** Every village that is served by aircraft rather than barge loses its supply line first,
  because aviation fuel and airframe maintenance are the most import-dependent parts of the system.
- **The 2029 LNG terminal, the Alaska LNG pipeline, Susitna-Watana, CIPLink, the Bradley Lake Expansion, and
  the Railbelt 230 kV upgrade.** Every one of them is a capital project that has not started.

## The TAPS threshold, stated as a scenario

**This is the mechanism the story was looking for, and it is real, engineered and quantified.**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Peak throughput (1988) | **2,032,928 bbl/d** |
| 2025 throughput (all-time low) | **462,821 bbl/d** |
| 2026 throughput, Jan–Aug | 458,570 bbl/d |
| Transit time, 1988 → today | **4.5 days → ~18 days** |
| Linefill (oil inside the pipe at any moment) | **9,059,057 barrels** |
| Alyeska/LoFIS minimum **without new investment**, winter | **300,000–350,000 bbl/d** |
| Alyeska's current estimate **with additional investment** | **~200,000 bbl/d** |
| BP study / Alaska Superior Court finding, **with heaters installed** | **70,000 bbl/d** |
| Studies for the line **as built** | 70,000–100,000 bbl/d |
| With a 20-inch replacement line to Fairbanks and rail onward | 45,000 bbl/d |

**The physics, restated:** less oil means slower oil means colder oil. Below roughly 70 °F at the pipe wall,
wax deposits out of a crude that is **up to 2% wax by volume.** At low velocity, entrained water separates and
pools; in winter, or during any shutdown, it can freeze. Ice jams pigs; wax plugs the line. Alyeska already
compensates by **recirculating crude at Pump Stations 3, 4, 7 and 9 purely to make frictional heat**, by
running a **mainline heater at Milepost 238**, by keeping **skid-mounted mobile heaters** staged, and by
having repurposed **Pump Station 8 into a crude heating facility.**

**Three scenario-relevant consequences:**

1. **The margin is about 25–35%.** Throughput today is ~458,000 bbl/d; the unmaintained winter floor is
   300,000–350,000. That is not a lot of room, and it is the *maintained* system that has that much room.
   **Strip the heating program, the chemical supply and the pigging regime and the effective floor rises
   toward and above today's flow.** The pipeline does not need the oil to run out; it needs the maintenance to
   run out.
2. **The failure is seasonal and it is a winter failure.** Every source that gives a floor gives a *winter*
   floor. A collapse that arrives in May has months of grace. One that arrives in October does not.
3. **A shutdown leaves nine million barrels of waxy crude in the pipe.** ⚠️ **What actually happens then —
   whether the line can be flushed, whether it can ever be restarted, and on what timescale it becomes
   permanently plugged — could not be sourced in this pass and is the single most important open question in
   this file.** It is also, for a novelist, the most usable: nobody in the real world has an answer, which
   means the author is free to decide, and the decision is load-bearing.

**And if TAPS stops, what stops with it:** the North Slope fields (they have no other export route), Pump
Station 9's load on the Fairbanks grid, **the Petro Star refineries at Valdez and North Pole** (which draw
their feedstock off the line and return residual to it), the Valdez Marine Terminal, the tanker trade out of
Prince William Sound, and **the oil revenue that is 28% of the state's unrestricted general fund.** What does
*not* stop: **Cook Inlet oil, and the Kenai refinery that processes it.** That asymmetry — the Interior and
the North lose their fuel supply while Southcentral keeps a small one — is probably the most useful political
geography in this file.

## Carrying capacity — how many people can this territory feed?

⚠️ **All of the following is arithmetic, not citation. The inputs are sourced; the conversions (calories per
pound, edible yield fractions) are standard rules of thumb and are not.** Treat the orders of magnitude, not
the digits.

**The base file established the shape of the problem: rural subsistence harvest of 276 lb/person/year supplies
176% of protein requirement and 25% of caloric requirement (~518 kcal/day against 2,100). Protein is solved.
Fat and carbohydrate are not.** This file can now put numbers on the other side.

**Carbohydrate — the real gap, and it is catastrophic.**

- Alaska's entire grain crop is **147,812 bushels of barley and 62,964 bushels of oats** — roughly **7.1
  million and 2.0 million pounds**, about **9.1 million pounds of grain.**
- At roughly 1,600 kcal per pound, that is about **14.6 billion kcal.**
- One person needs 2,100 kcal/day, or **766,500 kcal/year.**
- **14.6 billion ÷ 766,500 ≈ 19,000 person-years.** Against a population of 668,504, **Alaska's entire grain
  harvest is about ten days of calories for the territory** — and most of it is feed barley.
- Potatoes at 321 acres add, very roughly, another 2,000–3,000 person-years. Vegetables at 745 acres add
  negligible calories (they are water and micronutrients).

**Fish — the surplus, and it is enormous.**

- 2025 statewide commercial salmon harvest (base file): **792,777,134 lb round weight.** At roughly 60% edible
  yield and roughly 700 kcal/lb averaged across species, that is on the order of **330 billion kcal**, or
  **~430,000 person-years of complete caloric requirement.**
- **Bristol Bay sockeye alone** — 41.3 million fish in 2025, entirely wild, zero hatchery component, caught
  with gillnets from small boats — is on the order of **175,000 person-years.**
- And the base file's scale check: **subsistence harvest is 0.9% of all fish and game taken in Alaska;
  commercial fisheries are 98.6%.** There is no biomass constraint. There is only an access, preservation and
  distribution constraint.

**Putting it together:**

1. **Protein: solved, with a very large margin.** The fish alone are several times what the population needs.
2. **Fat: probably solved.** Salmon (especially sockeye and chinook), marine mammals, seal and fish oil.
   This is exactly what the traditional diets of the region are built around, and it is not an accident.
3. **Carbohydrate: not solved, not close, and not solvable by expanding Alaskan agriculture within any
   plausible timeframe.** Ten days of grain. This is the constraint.
4. **The realistic ceiling is therefore not set by food energy in aggregate — it is set by (a) how much fish
   can be caught and *preserved without refrigeration*, and (b) how the caught fish moves from Bristol Bay and
   the Bering Sea to Anchorage and Fairbanks, where 60% of the people are.**

**The distributional problem is the real one and it deserves emphasis.** The food is in the west and the
people are in the southcentral and the interior. **Anchorage, the Mat-Su, Fairbanks and the Kenai hold 565,000
of 668,500 people and produce almost none of the wild harvest.** Moving Bristol Bay sockeye to Anchorage
currently requires refrigerated vessels, a processing plant, and either the Gulf of Alaska shipping lanes or
air freight — all of which are in category (c). **A collapse does not starve the Yukon-Kuskokwim delta. It
starves Anchorage.** [Reasoning mine; the populations and harvest figures are sourced.]

**A defensible estimate, offered as a range and not a number:**

- **The rural, coastal and riverine population of ~88,000 off-road Alaskans is the part of this territory best
  positioned to survive**, because they are already harvesting 276 lb/person/year of wild food, already
  operating boats and nets, and already living within a day of the resource. Their constraint is fuel,
  ammunition, netting, outboard motors and preservation — not food.
- **The Railbelt population of ~565,000 has no local caloric base at all** beyond 72,708 acres of cropland and
  whatever fish can be landed at Homer, Seward, Kenai, Whittier and Valdez. **This is the population that a
  collapse actually threatens**, and the number that can be sustained there depends almost entirely on whether
  the fishing fleet and the road and rail links keep working.
- **The territory as a whole could, in principle, feed something on the order of its present population, and
  cannot in practice, because the food and the people are in different places and the machinery that connects
  them is the first thing to fail.** [Conclusion mine.]

## The three most useful facts for the author

1. **TAPS has a hard, engineered floor of roughly 300,000–350,000 bbl/d unmaintained, against present
   throughput of ~458,000 bbl/d — and the floor rises as maintenance falls away.** It is a threshold, it is
   seasonal, it is in winter, and it takes the whole North Slope down with it.
2. **Alaska can refine its own diesel and heating oil but probably not its own gasoline**, because only the
   Kenai plant has a reformer. The other four are topping plants, two of them fed directly off TAPS. And the
   entire rural electricity diesel demand — 27.2 million gallons a year — is under 3% of one refinery.
3. **The food problem is carbohydrate and distribution, not protein or biomass.** The grain crop is ten days
   of calories; the salmon harvest is several hundred thousand person-years; and the people are in the wrong
   place relative to the fish.

## Method note
**The session's WebSearch budget was exhausted before this research began** (200 of 200 calls used), so
everything here was obtained by direct `curl` against primary sources, `pdftotext`, the Wikipedia API, and
WebFetch. That turned out to be an advantage: almost every figure in this file comes from a government or
operator primary source rather than from journalism. Sites that refused: **pce.akenergyauthority.org**
(geo-blocked), **alaskabeacon.com** and **alaskapublic.org** search (403), **dog.dnr.alaska.gov/CIGasDashboard**
(JavaScript app), **EIA state-profile narrative pages** (JavaScript shells — the underlying data files work).
The **FY2025 PCE Statistical Report** is a PDF whose per-community pages use per-page subsetted fonts with no
usable ToUnicode map, so its text extracts as sequential-substitution gibberish; **FY2023 extracts cleanly**
and was used instead.
