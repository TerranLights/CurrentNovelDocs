**Compiled:** 2026-09-22

**Purpose.** A companion to `Cascadia - Real-World Material Base.md`, filling the two research streams that
file left open. Everything here is real-world fact about the present-day territory; nothing is invented. It

## A. Industry and Manufacturing

### A.1 Aerospace — Boeing and the Puget Sound supplier base

Boeing is the largest manufacturing employer in this territory and the only world-scale heavy manufacturer
anywhere on the Pacific coast of North America. Two plants carry it.

**Everett (Paine Field, Snohomish County) — the largest building in the world by volume.**
**472,370,319 cubic feet (13,376,038 m³)**, recognized by Guinness World Records; footprint **98.3 acres
(398,000 m²)** on a complex of roughly **1,000 acres**; 114 feet high; six hangar doors each 82 feet tall and
300-350 feet wide; 26 overhead cranes on 31 miles of track; assembly lines that creep forward at 1.5 inches a
minute. About **30,000 employees as of 2022**, the largest employer in Everett and Snohomish County. Over
**5,000 widebody aircraft** built there since 1967.

What Everett builds has contracted sharply. The **747 line ended with the final rollout in December 2022**
after 55 years, and **787 assembly moved entirely to Charleston, South Carolina by February 2021**. What
remains is the **767** (freighter and KC-46 Pegasus tanker) and the **777** family (777-9, 777F) — plus, from
**July 2026**, a new **737 MAX "North Line"**, described in trade coverage as Boeing's largest assembly
reshuffle since 1970. The building is therefore now substantially larger than the program load it carries.

**Renton (Renton Municipal Airport, built on land reclaimed from Lake Washington in 1916).** About
**1,100,000 square feet**, building the **737 MAX** and the **P-8 Poseidon** maritime patrol aircraft.
Cumulative output from this single plant is the best available illustration of what uninterrupted industrial
continuity actually produces: **1,119 B-29s, 943 C-97s, 803 KC-135s, 865 707s, 1,832 727s, 1,049 757s, and
10,963 737s across four generations as of March 2022.**

**The rail detail is the one to carry into Part B.** Renton has **its own rail spur served by BNSF**, and 737
fuselages arrive on flatcars — they are built by **Spirit AeroSystems in Wichita, Kansas**, which is not in
this territory. A Cascadia holding Renton but not Wichita holds an assembly hall, not an aircraft industry.
**[UNVERIFIED]** the exact BNSF routing of the fuselage train through the mountains; it reaches Puget Sound
from Spokane, which means either Stevens Pass or the Columbia Gorge (see B.3). The Wichita origin and the
rail mode are well attested — three fuselages famously ended up in the Clark Fork River in Montana in 2014.

**Rates and restructuring.** After the January 2024 door-plug accident the FAA capped 737 MAX output; by
**May 2026 the FAA had approved an increase to 47 aircraft a month**, and Boeing was hiring **100-140
production workers a week** to support it. As of **16 September 2026** Boeing's chief executive publicly
acknowledged that stabilizing the rate was taking longer than expected, and analyst coverage the following
day said the target rate had still not been reached. **[UNVERIFIED]** the scale of the 2024 IAM 751 strike
and the subsequent company-wide layoffs (widely reported as roughly 33,000 machinists out for about seven
weeks, followed by a ~10% workforce reduction on the order of 17,000 jobs) — no primary source retrieved.

**The supplier base, and why it does not save you.** Boeing's own Washington fabrication network includes
**Auburn** (machining and sheet-metal fabrication, one of the largest such plants anywhere), **Frederickson**
(composite wing skins and empennage structure), and **Portland/Gresham, Oregon** (machined parts and
gears). **Toray Composites America** at Frederickson makes carbon fiber prepreg. **[UNVERIFIED]** employment
and output at each of these; Boeing does not publish site-level figures and none were retrieved here. Beyond
that the tree runs straight off the map: 787 wings are built by Mitsubishi in Japan, fuselage barrels in
Kansas and Italy, and — the decisive one — **no large commercial turbofan engine is manufactured anywhere in
this territory.** CFM, GE, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce all build elsewhere. **[UNVERIFIED]** as a
categorical statement, but no West Coast large-engine plant is known to exist.

**Post-collapse reading.** Aerospace is the clearest Tier C industry in the file. An airliner needs titanium
forgings, carbon fiber, precision five-axis machining, avionics, and turbofans, drawn from four continents.
What survives at Everett and Renton is **floor space, cranes, runways, rail spurs, and machinists** — which
is a great deal if what you want to build is trucks, turbines, boilers, locomotives or ships, and nothing at
all if what you want is a 737. The Puget Sound aerospace workforce is a metalworking workforce first.

### A.2 Shipbuilding and ship repair

For a country that is physically held together by saltwater, this is the most strategically important industry
in the file — more important than aerospace, and the parent file's Section 4 does not touch it.

#### A.2.1 Puget Sound Naval Shipyard and Intermediate Maintenance Facility, Bremerton

The single most capable heavy-marine industrial site on the Pacific coast of North America.

- **179 acres** on Sinclair Inlet, Bremerton; the **Pacific Northwest's largest naval shore facility**.
- About **15,000 employees** — **the largest public shipyard in the United States by personnel assigned**.
- **Six drydocks.** Dock 6 (completed 1962) is **1,151 ft 11 in long × 180 ft wide × 53 ft 2 in deep** — large
  enough for a nuclear aircraft carrier. Dock 1 (completed **1896**) is 638 ft 11 in × 108 ft × 30 ft 2 in and
  is still in the inventory, which is itself a useful fact: a graving dock cut into rock in the 1890s is still
  doing work in the 2020s.
- Work: maintenance, modernization, and technical and logistics support. Roughly **25% of the workload is
  inactivation, reactor compartment disposal, and ship recycling**. It is **the only US facility certified to
  recycle nuclear-powered ships** and has scrapped **more than 125 submarines** plus cruisers.
- A **250-foot green hammerhead crane built in 1933**, rated 250 tons, still stands over the yard.

Associated naval infrastructure in the same waters — **[UNVERIFIED]** in detail, but structurally certain:
Naval Base Kitsap at **Bangor** (Pacific Trident ballistic-missile submarine base), Naval Station **Everett**,
and the Naval Magazine at **Indian Island**. Whoever holds Bremerton holds the only place on this coast where
a capital ship can be lifted out of the water and rebuilt.

#### A.2.2 Vigor — the working commercial yard network

Now **Vigor Marine Group**; about **2,500 employees as of 2025**. Facilities:

| Site | Size | Docks / piers / cranes |
|---|---|---|
| **Portland, Oregon (Swan Island)** | 60 acres | 3 drydocks, 12 piers, 13 cranes |
| **Seattle, Washington** (the former Todd Shipyards) | 27 acres | 3 drydocks, 4 piers, 12 cranes |
| **Vancouver, Washington** | 7 acres | aluminum fabrication |
| San Diego, California | 31 acres | 0 drydocks, 2 piers, 4 cranes |
| Norfolk / Portsmouth, Virginia | 33 / 4.5 acres | outside the territory |

The Portland floating drydock **Vigorous**, bought for **$40 million** and delivered in 2014, is **960 feet
long** and was claimed at the time to be **the largest in North America**. Vigor builds **Washington State
Ferries**, US Army **Maneuver Support Vessel (Light)** landing craft, barges, tugs, and aluminum workboats,
and repairs naval and commercial vessels. Ownership has been financial rather than industrial: Lone Star
Funds sold the business to **Antin Infrastructure Partners in February 2026**. **[UNVERIFIED]** — Vigor has
also operated the state-owned **Ketchikan Shipyard** in the Alaska Panhandle, which would be the only
significant repair yard in the northern third of the country; the source consulted here did not list it.

**Washington State Ferries** is the customer that keeps this capability alive and is itself a chokepoint
question: **21 vehicle-carrying vessels, 8 routes, 20 terminals, 20.1 million riders in 2025** (10.7 million
walk-on passengers, 9.4 million in vehicles) — **the largest ferry system in the United States**. Its Jumbo
and Olympic class hulls were ordered from **Todd Shipyards in Seattle**, the yard that is now Vigor Seattle.
Puget Sound's road network has water in the middle of it, and the ferries are the road.

#### A.2.3 British Columbia — Seaspan and the National Shipbuilding Strategy

**Seaspan ULC**, North Vancouver: **2,800 employees**, revenue **$493.8 million (2025)**, three yards.

- **Vancouver Shipyards** (North Vancouver) — new construction; steel forming shop, fabrication hall, enclosed
  paint facility, a **1,200-tonne SyncroLift**, and a land-based systems integration test site.
- **Victoria Shipyards** (Esquimalt) — the largest ship repair company on Canada's Pacific coast. It operates
  the **Esquimalt Graving Dock**, owned by Public Services and Procurement Canada, which is **the largest
  non-military hard-bottom drydock on the west coast of the Americas**, takes ships **larger than Panamax**,
  and handles vessels **up to 100,000 DWT**.
- **Vancouver Drydock** (the former Burrard Dry Dock site) — two floating docks, 36,000-tonne Panamax
  capacity and a 30,000-tonne deployable dock.

Seaspan also runs tugs, ferries, and **rail-car barges**, moving forest products, minerals, railcars, and
supplies along the coast — which is the coastal-trunk-line function the parent file's Section 0 identifies as
the thing actually holding the country together.

**Canada's National Shipbuilding Strategy** (announced 19 October 2011) split the national build between
**Irving Shipbuilding** in Halifax (combat package, originally $25 billion, now **over $80 billion**) and
**Seaspan** in North Vancouver (non-combat package, originally $8 billion, now roughly **$26 billion**), with
**Davie Shipbuilding** of Lévis, Quebec added as a third yard in **April 2023** for icebreakers. Seaspan's
book: **2 Protecteur-class Joint Support Ships, 3 Offshore Fisheries Science Vessels, 1 Offshore
Oceanographic Science Vessel, up to 16 Multi-Purpose Icebreakers, and 1 Polar Icebreaker.** As of **May
2026**, the fisheries and oceanographic vessels were in service; the supply ships and polar icebreakers were
still under construction.

**Why the author should care about the NSS specifically.** It is a real, documented, twenty-year national
program to **rebuild a shipbuilding industry that a country had allowed to die**, complete with cost
escalation of roughly three times the original estimate and delivery slippage measured in years. That is the
best available real-world calibration for how long and how expensively a reconstruction-era Cascadia would
take to get back to building its own ships — and Canada attempted it with an intact economy, intact
suppliers, and a functioning global market for steel and diesel engines.

#### A.2.4 The rest of the yards, and the WWII precedent

**[UNVERIFIED]** in detail, but the second tier is real and worth naming: **Nichols Brothers Boat Builders**
(Freeland, Whidbey Island), **Dakota Creek Industries** (Anacortes), **Gunderson Marine** (Greenbrier,
Portland — barges and railcars), **Foss Maritime** (Seattle and Rainier, Oregon), **Everett Ship Repair**,
and **BAE Systems San Francisco Ship Repair**. **[UNVERIFIED]** the current status of **General Dynamics
NASSCO at San Diego**, which the parent file flags as the surviving major West Coast builder of large
ocean-going commercial ships — the Wikipedia title consulted was a disambiguation page and no figures were
retrieved. Check the National Steel and Shipbuilding Company article or nassco.com.

**The precedent that matters most is eighty years old.** In the Second World War the **Kaiser Shipyards**
built **1,490 ships** across seven yards — four at **Richmond, California** (747 ships), two at **Portland,
Oregon** (Oregon Shipbuilding Corporation, 455 ships, plus Swan Island), and one at **Vancouver,
Washington** — which was **27% of all US Maritime Commission construction**. Liberty ships were being
completed in **a little over two weeks** each; the *SS Joseph N. Teal* was built at Portland in **ten days**
in the fall of 1942, and the *SS Robert E. Peary* at Richmond in **under five days**. Kaiser's ships cost
about a **quarter** of the all-yards average.

This is the single most useful fact in Part A for a reconstruction plot. Welded steel merchant hulls are not
high technology. They were mass-produced on tidal mudflats by a workforce recruited from farms, largely women
and first-time industrial workers, using prefabricated sections and jigs. The **sites are still there** —
Richmond, Swan Island, Vancouver, Bremerton, Esquimalt, North Vancouver. What a collapse takes away is the
steel mill upstream (see the parent file's §4.4 on Kaiser Fontana) and the marine diesel plant, not the
ability to lay a hull.

### A.3 Semiconductors and electronics

The parent file's §4.4 says, correctly, that leading-edge fabrication has largely left California. It does not
say where it went, and the answer is inside this territory: **Oregon**.

#### A.3.1 Intel in Oregon — the crown jewel, and the most fragile object in the country

**Intel's Oregon operation is its largest anywhere in the world**, and its **Ronler Acres** campus in Hillsboro
(opened 1994) is described as its most advanced facility anywhere. Oregon is where Intel does **process
development** — the new node is proved out in Hillsboro and then copied to volume fabs elsewhere. Four
campuses: **Aloha** (1979, Intel's first Oregon site), **Jones Farm** (1982, next to Hillsboro Airport),
**Hawthorn Farm**, and **Ronler Acres**.

**[SOURCES DISAGREE]** on headcount, because the figures measure different geographies and different years:
about **17,000 in Oregon in late 2012**, about **22,000 in Oregon by 2022**, while the City of Hillsboro's
2022 financial report gives roughly **20,000** Intel employees in the city (30.6% of the top-employer total)
and another passage puts the Hillsboro sites at **about 16,000**. Take Oregon-wide as ~20,000-22,000 at the
2022 peak and treat any current figure as unverified — Intel has been through repeated restructuring since.

Recent, and worth having: in **July 2026** trade and local coverage reported **ASML equipment shipments to
Ronler Acres** and Intel **shipping High-NA EUV silicon** from Hillsboro. **[UNVERIFIED]** — these are trade
and local-press reports retrieved through a news aggregator, not Intel releases, and no 2025-26 Oregon
headcount figure was confirmed in this session.

#### A.3.2 The rest of the Silicon Forest

Oregon and southwest Washington hold a real, if second-tier, fab cluster: **Qorvo** at Hillsboro (the former
TriQuint — 100 mm and 150 mm wafers, gallium arsenide power amplifiers), **Analog Devices** at Beaverton (the
former Maxim "MaxFabNorth"), **Microchip Technology** at Gresham (the former Fujitsu fab), **onsemi**,
**Lattice Semiconductor**, **Silicon Labs**, and — across the river in **Camas, Washington** — **WaferTech**,
a **TSMC** subsidiary. **nLIGHT** at Vancouver, Washington has made laser diodes there since 2001. Add the
instrument and display companies that gave the region its name: **Tektronix**, **Planar Systems**,
**Pixelworks**, **InFocus**, plus HP, Xerox and Epson printer operations, and the tool vendors **Tokyo
Electron America** and **Synopsys**.

Scale check: Oregon high-tech employment peaked at **almost 73,000 in 2001** and was **57,000 by spring
2012**. **[UNVERIFIED]** current figure.

#### A.3.3 California's remaining fabs, and Micron

Fabrication in California is now a residue. The plants confirmed in this session are **Tower Semiconductor
Fab 3** at Newport Beach (200 mm, 500-130 nm, foundry/SiGe work) and an **Apple**-owned fab at San Jose, the
former Maxim/Samsung site (600-90 nm). Both are **trailing-edge**. **[UNVERIFIED]** — other California fabs
almost certainly operate (Bosch at Roseville and Skyworks at Newbury Park are commonly cited) but were not
confirmed from the source consulted. What California has is **design**: Santa Clara County holds the
architecture, EDA tools, and IP, and the silicon is made in Taiwan, Korea, Arizona, Oregon, and Israel.

**Micron is not in this country.** Micron Technology is headquartered in **Boise, Idaho**, with US fabs at
Boise, Manassas (Virginia), and the announced New York complex — **all outside the territory on the author's
map**. **[UNVERIFIED]** as a categorical claim, but no Micron fab in California, Oregon, Washington, BC, or
Baja was found. The author should not assume Cascadia holds a memory industry. It holds a **logic process
R&D** industry, which is a different and narrower thing.

#### A.3.4 Why a fab is the first thing to die — the three dependencies

This is the part that matters for the collapse analysis, and each leg is independently fatal.

1. **Ultrapure water.** A leading-edge fab consumes water measured in **millions of gallons a day**, and it
   must be ultrapure — deionized far past drinking standard, with organics, particles, and dissolved gases
   stripped out. That is itself an industrial plant requiring membranes, resins, and power. **[UNVERIFIED]**
   Intel's specific Oregon withdrawal figures; Intel and the City of Hillsboro publish water-use data and the
   author should pull it. Note the geography, though: Hillsboro sits in the **Tualatin Valley**, a modest
   basin in the rain-shadowed lee of the Coast Range, not on a great river.
2. **Uninterrupted power.** Tens of megawatts, continuously. The critical detail is not the quantity but the
   **quality**: a voltage sag of a fraction of a second ruins every wafer in process across the fab, and a
   wafer takes **weeks to months** to work through several hundred process steps. A grid that browns out is
   not a grid a fab can use, regardless of how much total energy it delivers.
3. **Imported chemicals, gases, wafers, and above all machines.** Photoresists and high-purity hydrogen
   fluoride come predominantly from Japan; polished silicon wafers from Shin-Etsu and SUMCO; specialty gases
   from a handful of global suppliers. And the lithography: **extreme-ultraviolet scanners are built by
   ASML, in the Netherlands, and by nobody else on Earth.** The July 2026 report of ASML shipments into
   Ronler Acres is the whole dependency in one sentence. **[UNVERIFIED]** in the specific percentages, but
   the single-vendor EUV fact is not in dispute.

**Post-collapse reading.** Semiconductor fabrication is the purest Tier C item in the entire file — more
purely Tier C than aerospace, because at least an airframe can be repaired with hand tools. A fab that loses
power for a week loses its work in progress; a fab that loses its chemical supply for a quarter is scrap. The
buildings survive as enormous, very clean, very well-serviced warehouses. What a reconstruction economy could
plausibly rebuild toward is not this: it is **discrete devices, vacuum tubes, relays, wire, transformers and
electric motors** — which is a 1920s-1950s electrical industry, and which the region's remaining metalworking
and winding capability could actually support.

### A.4 Forestry, pulp, paper, and lumber

The parent file covers the **Tongass** collapse in §4.6 and should be read first; this section covers the
living industry south of it. The short version: the forest is the one industrial resource in this territory
that is renewable, distributed along the whole length of the country, and **already in structural decline for
reasons that have nothing to do with any collapse**.

#### A.4.1 British Columbia — a supply shock that already happened

**The mountain pine beetle epidemic is the single largest disturbance event in this file.** More than
**160,000 km² (40 million acres)** of BC forest was affected. By **November 2008 roughly half the province's
lodgepole pine was dead — about 135,000 km² (33 million acres)** — in an outbreak described as **an order of
magnitude larger than any previously recorded**. It crossed the continental divide into Alberta in 2007. The
dead standing pine was salvage-logged on an emergency basis, which temporarily **raised** the allowable cut
and kept mills running on borrowed time; the bill came due afterward.

**[UNVERIFIED] and important:** BC's **annual allowable cut** and actual harvest volumes in million cubic
meters were not confirmed from a primary source in this session, and they are the numbers the author most
needs. News coverage through 2025-26 is uniformly bleak — *Business in Vancouver* headlining "another tough
year" for the sector (28 January 2026), *Resource Works* describing a "breaking point" (27 August 2026), *The
Tyee* a "reckoning" over logging licenses (2 June 2026), and **Canfor announcing the closure of its 100 Mile
House sawmill** (reported by CFJC Today, 8 November 2025) with union coverage in July 2026 describing workers
"reeling" from shutdowns. Pull the actual figures from the **BC Ministry of Forests Harvest Billing System**
and the ministry's AAC determinations before relying on any number.

**The two BC majors, and where their capacity actually is:**

- **Canfor** — as of 2016, annual capability of **5.9 billion board feet of lumber**, **1.1 million tonnes of
  northern softwood market kraft pulp**, about **220,000 tonnes of BCTMP** and **140,000 tonnes of kraft
  paper**; roughly **6,331 employees (2017)**. BC mill towns named in the source include Prince George, Elko,
  and Wynndel. **[UNVERIFIED]** current mill count and closure list.
- **West Fraser Timber** — founded at **Quesnel, BC**, headquartered in Vancouver, **the largest lumber
  manufacturer in North America**, about **50 locations (2018)** and **10,800 employees (2023)**, of which
  roughly **5,000 were in Western Canada in 2018**. The trend the author should note: both companies have
  spent two decades shifting capacity to the **US South**, where the fiber is fast-growing planted pine and
  the politics are simpler. **[UNVERIFIED]** the BC-versus-elsewhere capacity split.

#### A.4.2 Washington and Oregon — the federal harvest already stopped once

**The Northwest Forest Plan (1994)** governs about **ten million hectares** of federal forest in western
Oregon, western Washington, and a small slice of northern California. It was adopted after northern spotted
owl litigation and **"called for strongly decreased timber yields within National Forests."** **[UNVERIFIED]**
— the specific before-and-after federal harvest figures, mill closure counts, and job-loss numbers were not
retrieved; they are well documented in USFS and Oregon Department of Forestry publications and the author
should pull them. The qualitative outcome is not disputed: federal-land logging in the Pacific Northwest fell
by roughly an order of magnitude in the 1990s, private industrial timberland became the harvest base, and
dozens of small mill towns lost their single employer.

**Weyerhaeuser** is the anchor and its history is a worldbuilding gift on its own. Headquartered in
**Seattle**, it owns about **10.4 million acres** of US timberland and manages about **14 million more under
long-term license in Canada**, with **9,440 employees (2024)**. It exists because in **1900** Friedrich
Weyerhäuser and fifteen partners bought **900,000 acres of Washington timberland from James J. Hill of the
Great Northern Railway** — the railroad's federal land grant, converted directly into a private forest
empire. In **1929** it built what was then **the world's largest sawmill at Longview, Washington**, adding a
pulp mill in 1931. The point for the author: **the Pacific Northwest forest estate was assembled as a
by-product of building a railroad**, and the land title pattern still follows the old grant checkerboard.

**[UNVERIFIED]** — current mill counts for Oregon and Washington, and **log export** volumes. Raw-log export
to Asia from Washington and BC ports is a real and politically charged trade (US federal and state lands
restrict it; private lands do not), but no primary figure was retrieved here. Check the Washington DNR, the
Oregon Department of Forestry harvest reports, and BC's log export permit data.

#### A.4.3 Why forestry is the industry that comes back first

Unlike a fab or an airframe, a sawmill is a machine a reconstruction economy can rebuild. The historical
sequence on this coast is instructive and runs in exactly the right direction: **water-powered sash mills →
steam-powered circular mills → band saws → electric mills**. Each step is achievable with the metalworking
base described in A.2. The **log itself is transported by water** — rafted, boomed, and towed down the
Columbia, the Fraser, and the Inside Passage — which means forestry is the one heavy industry whose logistics
match the parent file's Section 0 conclusion that the sea is the real trunk line.

The constraint is not the trees. It is **pulp and paper**, which is chemistry: the kraft process needs sodium
hydroxide and sodium sulfide, a recovery boiler, and continuous high-pressure steam. Salt from Guerrero Negro
(parent file §1.6) is the feedstock for the chlor-alkali half of that. Sawn lumber survives a collapse;
**bleached kraft pulp does not**, and neither does the newsprint or the cardboard box.

### A.5 Food processing

This is the most **post-collapse-relevant** manufacturing in the territory, for the reason the parent file
gives in §4.4: the inputs are local and most of the technology is 19th- and 20th-century. It is also, right
now, enormous.

**Frozen potatoes — the Columbia Basin's export industry.** **Lamb Weston** is one of the world's largest
frozen-potato processors: **$6.45 billion revenue and 10,100 employees (2025)**. Its plant list is the
Columbia Basin written as a factory map — **Washington: Connell, Pasco, Paterson, Quincy, Richland, Warden;
Oregon: Boardman East, Boardman West, Hermiston; Idaho: American Falls, Twin Falls**, plus Louisiana,
Minnesota, Canada, Australia, and China. Note that the **headquarters is in Eagle, Idaho** — outside the
territory — but essentially all of the Pacific Northwest processing capacity is inside it. Read this against
the parent file's §2.2.2: the Columbia Basin Project lifts water 280 feet with 600 MW of electricity to grow
164,500 acres of potatoes at the highest yield in the United States, and these plants turn them into frozen
fries for Asia. **Every link in that chain is electrical.**

**Central Valley canning and tomato processing.** **The Morning Star Company** of Woodland, California
processes roughly **40% of California's processing tomatoes** and supplies about **10% of the world's
ingredient tomato products** (as of October 2023) from three plants — **Los Banos (1990), Williams (1995),
and Santa Nella (2002)** — with capacity **over 3,000 tons of tomatoes an hour** and roughly **5 million tons
a year**. Revenue exceeds **$1 billion**; **550 full-time employees and over 2,500 seasonal workers**. One
privately held company in one California county is a measurable fraction of the world's tomato paste.
**[UNVERIFIED]** — the rest of the Central Valley pack (Del Monte, Pacific Coast Producers, Seneca, and the
fruit canneries) was not researched in this session, and Del Monte's California plant closures are a live
story worth checking.

**Wine.** California **produces about 90% of the American wine supply** and is the **fourth-largest wine
producer among the world's independent nations**, with **4,864 wineries as of January 2025** and **477,475
acres of planted vineyard** — and it is contracting: growers pulled out **nearly 40,000 acres between October
2024 and August 2025**. The **Central Valley alone produces nearly 75% of California's wine grapes** (Gallo,
Franzia, Bronco), which is the opposite of the region's prestige image; Napa and Sonoma are the North Coast
and are a small share of volume. Washington is the second US wine state and Oregon the Willamette Valley
Pinot noir region (parent file §2.2, §2.3), and Baja's Guadalupe Valley is Mexico's principal wine region
(§2.5).

**Wine is the single best Tier B/Tier A hybrid in the file.** Fermentation is biology; it needs no grid, no
imported chemicals, and no refrigeration if you accept the vintage the weather gives you. Distillation needs
only a still and fuel. Vineyards are perennial and survive neglect for years. The industrial parts — stainless
tanks, temperature control, cork and glass, and the export market — are the fragile parts. A collapsed
Cascadia keeps its wine, in roughly the form 18th-century Europe had it.

**Seafood processing.** **Trident Seafoods**, headquartered in **Seattle**, runs catcher and catcher-processor
vessels plus processing plants at **twelve coastal locations in Alaska**, with about **9,000 employees**, two
shipyards of its own, an R&D center, and sales in over 55 countries. Shore plants at Ketchikan, Petersburg,
and Sitka are named in the parent file's §2.1; **Ensenada** is Mexico's principal Pacific fishing port
(§2.5); **Pacific Seafood** of Clackamas, Oregon is the other large regional processor **[UNVERIFIED]**.

**[UNVERIFIED] — Pacific Northwest frozen vegetables.** Washington and Oregon are a major frozen-vegetable
region (peas, corn, carrots, green beans, broccoli), historically through **NORPAC** (an Oregon growers'
cooperative that went bankrupt in 2019), **Twin City Foods**, **National Frozen Foods**, and **Seneca Foods**.
No plant counts or volumes were confirmed in this session.

**The analytic point.** Sort food processing into three shelves and it separates cleanly:

- **Survives on its own** — fermenting, salting, smoking, drying, and root-cellaring. Needs salt (Guerrero
  Negro), sun, and skill. This is what the whole territory falls back to.
- **Survives with modest industry** — canning in glass or tinplate, milling, pressing oil, brewing and
  distilling. Needs a boiler, a foundry, and a glassworks, and note that **borosilicate and container glass
  need the Boron borates in the parent file's §1.5**.
- **Vanishes** — the frozen chain. Freezing is not a preservation technique so much as a **permanent
  electricity subscription**. Every frozen fry in Warden, every frozen pea in Skagit, and every frozen fillet
  in Ketchikan is only food for as long as the power stays on, and thaws into a disposal problem the week it
  doesn't.

## B. Ports, Rail, and Chokepoints

### B.1 The ports

North to south. The parent file's §5.5 covers Los Angeles and the other California ports, §5.6 covers Baja,
and §5.7 covers the Alaska Panhandle; this section fills in everything between and adds Long Beach, which the
parent file names but does not quantify.

#### B.1.1 Prince Rupert, British Columbia — the geography prize

- **Draft depth 16 meters (52 feet).** Described as **the deepest ice-free natural harbor in North America
  and the third-deepest natural harbor in the world.**
- **The shortest great-circle distance to the Far East of any Pacific Northwest port** — it is the first
  inbound and last outbound port of call on the Asia-North America rotation, which cuts a day or more off
  transits to the Midwest.
- **Fairview container terminal:** Phase 1 rated **500,000 TEU**, Phase 2 planned at **2 million TEU**.
- Actual throughput: **2017 — 927,000 TEU / 24.2 million tonnes; 2018 — 1,036,000 TEU / 26.7 Mt; 2019 — 1.21
  million TEU / 29.84 Mt.** **[UNVERIFIED]** anything after 2019; the port publishes annual statistics.
- Also a **grain terminal at Ridley Island**, coal, wood pellets, and bulk. **Served exclusively by CN Rail.**

**Why this port is disproportionately interesting for the author.** Everything that makes Prince Rupert
valuable is **geology**: depth, shelter, and its position on the globe. None of it was built and none of it
can be destroyed. What *was* built is the single railway that connects it to anything (B.2.3), and that is
the fragile half.

#### B.1.2 Vancouver, British Columbia — Canada's largest port

- **141 million metric revenue tonnes** a year and **3.5 million TEU** — **the largest port in Canada and the
  fourth largest in North America by tonnage.**
- **29 deep-sea and domestic marine terminals** across five sectors: automobiles, break-bulk, bulk,
  containers, and cruise. Commodities: **grain, coal, potash, logs, cement, steel, autos, containers.**
- Economic weight: **115,300 jobs, $7 billion in wages, $11.9 billion in GDP**, enabling roughly **$240
  billion in annual trade**.
- Navigation limits on the **Fraser River** arm: maximum **294 m length overall, 38 m beam, 11.5 m draft with
  tidal assistance**, air draft **53 m**.

#### B.1.3 The Northwest Seaport Alliance — Seattle and Tacoma

Formed **4 August 2015** as a joint port authority; the two harbors are **32 miles apart**.

- **10 container terminals, 23 berths, 47 cranes**, plus **7 non-container terminals** for break-bulk and
  roll-on/roll-off.
- **2016: 3.6 million TEU and 28 million metric tons.** It was the third-largest US container gateway at
  formation and had slipped to **seventh busiest by 2023 (Lloyd's List)**.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** current volume. *WorldCargo News* reported on **24 July 2026** that the NWSA was running a
  **12.4% year-to-date TEU decline**. Pull the annual figures from nwseaportalliance.com.

The structural point: the NWSA is the **third node** in a West Coast container system whose first two nodes
are eight times its size, and it has been losing share for a decade. In a collapse scenario this matters
less than what sits beside it — **Bremerton, Bangor, Everett, and the Vigor Seattle yard** (A.2).

#### B.1.4 Portland, Oregon — an ocean port 100 river miles inland

| **T-5** | 159 acres | **grain**, minerals, warehousing |
| **T-6** | 419 acres | containers, autos, steel, break-bulk, rail yard |

**Portland's container business is a cautionary tale the author should use.** In **March 2015 Hanjin — 78% of

infrastructure; it is a **position in somebody else's network**, and it can be withdrawn in a month.

**The river constraint defines the port:** the Columbia navigation channel is maintained to a **43-foot
draft**, and **air draft is capped at 196 feet by the Astoria-Megler Bridge**. The Port of Portland runs its

facts — see Part C.

### B.2 Rail

#### B.2.1 BNSF — the northern system

- **Over 33,400 miles of track** in 28 US states and western Canada; **over 24,000 route miles** directly
  owned, more than 50,000 miles including secondary main and yard trackage.
- The **Northern Transcon** runs **Seattle to Chicago**, assembled from the old **Great Northern, Northern
  Pacific, and Spokane, Portland & Seattle** railroads. BNSF's Northwest Division, headquartered in Seattle,
  covers **British Columbia, Idaho, Montana, Oregon, and Washington**.
- **BNSF reaches Vancouver, British Columbia** over roughly **30 miles of line from the US-Canada border** —
  so the coastal north-south railroad is continuous from Vancouver BC through Bellingham, Everett, Seattle,
  and Tacoma to Vancouver, Washington and Portland.
- Two Cascade crossings, both covered in B.3: **Stevens Pass** (the Cascade Tunnel) and **Stampede Pass**.
  BNSF's predecessor **abandoned Stampede in August 1984 in favor of Stevens**, then rehabilitated and
  **reopened it in December 1996** to relieve congestion on Stevens.
- Intermodal: among the largest intermodal carriers in North America; the **Seattle International Gateway**
  is its Puget Sound hub.

#### B.2.2 Union Pacific — the southern system

**8,300 locomotives over 32,200 route miles in 23 states west of Chicago and New Orleans.** **[UNVERIFIED]**
in detail — the Wikipedia article consulted gave no subdivision-level description, so the following is
general railroad geography rather than a sourced list, and the author should confirm it before using route
names:

- UP holds the **Oregon side of the Columbia River Gorge** (BNSF holds the Washington side) — this much *is*
  confirmed, including by the **2016 Mosier derailment**, in which an oil train spilled **42,000 US gallons
  (160 m³) of Bakken crude** in the Gorge near Mosier, Oregon.
- The through freight route south from Portland runs **Portland - Eugene - Klamath Falls - Dunsmuir -
  Sacramento**, crossing the Cascades inland rather than following I-5 over Siskiyou Summit.
- South of Sacramento, the San Joaquin Valley line runs to Bakersfield and over the **Tehachapi** crossing
  into the Los Angeles Basin, with the **Coast Line** as the secondary route through San Luis Obispo and
  Salinas. **Cajon Pass** is the eastward exit. Parent file §5.5 covers Tehachapi and Cajon.

**The confirmed and important fact about the Oregon-California rail connection:** the old Southern Pacific
**Siskiyou Line** over Siskiyou Summit, now the **Central Oregon and Pacific Railroad**, **closed in 2008 and
reopened in 2015**. A Class I main line between two states went out of service for seven years in the real
world, in peacetime, for want of maintenance money. Keep that number.

#### B.2.3 Canadian National — the line to Prince Rupert

CN runs a core freight network **Halifax to Chicago and Toronto to Vancouver and Prince Rupert.** The Prince
Rupert line is the former **Grand Trunk Pacific**, built about **1910-1913**, crossing the Rockies at the
**Yellowhead Pass (1,131 m / 3,711 ft)** — a low, gently graded pass, which is precisely why the route
exists and why it can pull heavy trains efficiently — then descending the Fraser and **following the Skeena
River to tidewater**. CN bought **BC Rail** from the province in **2004**, taking the operating assets while
**the province retained ownership of the track and right-of-way** (a detail the author may find useful: the
public kept the land and sold the operation).

**A detail worth the author's attention: CN ran a rail-barge service, the Aquatrain, between Prince Rupert
and Whittier, Alaska from 1963 until 2021.** That was the only rail connection of any kind between the North
American network and Alaska, it worked by floating railcars across open water, and **it ended in 2021**. In a
reconstruction scenario, restarting a rail barge is a far more plausible project than building a railroad up
the Panhandle, which nobody has ever managed.

#### B.2.4 Where there is no railroad at all

- **The Alaska Panhandle** — nothing but the **White Pass and Yukon Route** narrow-gauge line out of Skagway
  (parent §5.7). The Alaska Railroad, 600 miles north, is not connected to the continental network either.
- **The Baja peninsula** — nothing but the short **Tijuana-Tecate** line in the far north (parent §5.6).
  Ensenada, Baja's only deep-water port, has **no rail connection** (B.1.7).
- **Between Prince Rupert and Prince George** there is one railway and one highway (16), in the same valleys.

**The summary the author needs:** this country has **four** rail spines, and they do not form a network.
BNSF and UP run north-south on the US side in a single coastal corridor; CN and CPKC run **east-west** in
British Columbia, out of the country. There is **no rail connection at all** in the northern quarter and the
southern quarter. A 4,000 km nation is served by one continuous freight railroad from Vancouver BC to San
Diego, and by nothing above or below that.

### B.3 Chokepoints — the enumerated list the parent file promises

**This is the section the parent file's Section 0 promises twice and does not deliver.** Its claim, verbatim,
is that "north-south land transport is a chain of chokepoints, not a network... Sever any one and the country
becomes two countries." The research below **substantiates the chokepoint list in full and corrects the
conclusion**, and the correction is worth more to the author than the confirmation. Read B.3.9 last.

#### B.3.1 Dixon Entrance and the Inside Passage — the northern seam

The Panhandle has no road and no railroad (parent §5.7). Its only connection to the rest of the country is
the sheltered channel from Puget Sound and the Strait of Georgia north through the BC coast and the Alexander
Archipelago. **Sever it and you do not isolate a region, you isolate everything north of Dixon Entrance
absolutely** — Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan, Petersburg, Wrangell, and Greens Creek mine with it. Note that this
is the one chokepoint on the list that is **pure geography with no built component at all**: no lock, no
bridge, no tunnel, nothing to bomb. It can only be closed by a ship.

#### B.3.2 The Skeena corridor — Prince Rupert's single thread

One railway (CN) and one highway (16) run together down the Skeena valley from Prince George to Prince
Rupert, for hundreds of kilometers, in the same narrow river canyon. **Sever the Skeena and the third-deepest
natural harbor in the world becomes a fishing village.** The port has no hinterland of its own; its entire
value is that a train can reach it. And since the **Aquatrain rail barge ended in 2021** (B.2.3), it no
longer even has a marine rail outlet.

#### B.3.3 The Fraser Canyon — the only low route from the BC coast to the interior

- **Highway 1 (the Trans-Canada) is carved out of the rock faces**, through **seven tunnels built 1957-1964**
  — Yale, Saddle Rock, Sailor Bar, Alexandra, Hells Gate, Ferrabee, and China Bar — the longest about **610
  meters (2,000 ft)**.
- **Both railways are in the canyon with it**: CP historically on the west side south of Siska, CN on the
  east, crossing over each other — the CP on a **160-metre (520 ft) truss bridge**, the CN on a **250-metre
  (810 ft) steel arch above it**.
- At **Hells Gate** the walls rise about **1,000 meters (3,300 ft)** above the rapids. In **1913 a rockslide
  caused by railway blasting** diverted the river and blocked the salmon run so badly that **fishways** had
  to be built beside it — a permanent, still-visible example of a transport corridor destroying a food
  supply.

Two railroads and a highway, in one canyon, on unstable rock, above a river. There is no redundancy here;
there is only proximity.

#### B.3.4 The Coquihalla — the modern bypass, and it is worse

**BC Highway 5**, the Coquihalla section, runs **185.55 km from Hope to Kamloops** over a summit of **1,244 m
(4,081 ft)**, opened in two phases (**16 May 1986** and **September 1987**), requiring **31 bridges and
underpasses and over 3.7 million tonnes of gravel**. It is **the shortest land connection between Vancouver
and Edmonton** and carries the freight. It also gets **snowfall exceeding 10 cm per hour**, and recorded
**32 fatal crashes between 2004 and 2013** with 400-500 accidents per winter season.

#### B.3.5 November 2021 — the thesis, proven, by weather, in four days

**This is the most valuable single event in the entire supplement, because it is the author's scenario
performed in reality with cameras running.** Over two days in mid-November 2021, about **200 mm (7.9 in) of
rain** fell on southwestern British Columbia. The result:

- **Highway 1, Highway 3, Highway 5 (Coquihalla), Highway 7, and Highway 99 were all severed.** The combined
  closures **cut off road traffic between Metro Vancouver and the rest of Canada.**
- **Both CN and CP were severed at multiple locations, with no rail connection between Kamloops and
  Vancouver.**
- **Forty vessels sat waiting off the Port of Vancouver**, through which roughly **75% of Canada's grain
  exports** move.
- Up to **630,000 farm animals died** on the flooded Sumas Prairie.
- Insured damage **at least CDN $450 million**; total economic damage estimated at **US $2.5-7.5 billion**.
- Reopening: Highway 7 to limited traffic **17 November**; Highway 99 to essential traffic **20 November**;
  **the Coquihalla to essential traffic 20 December 2021, to regular traffic 19 January 2022, and to full
  completion only in September 2022.**

Read that timeline twice. **A single storm closed every land route into Canada's largest Pacific port for
about a week, and full repair of one highway took ten months** — with a functioning federal government, an
insurance industry, a heavy construction sector, and no opposition.

#### B.3.6 The Strait of Juan de Fuca — the highest-value chokepoint in the country

- **96 miles (154 km) long, 12 to 25 miles wide, averaging about 100 meters deep.**
- **The Salish Sea's only connection to the Pacific Ocean.** Beyond its eastern end lie Haro Strait, San Juan
  Channel, Rosario Strait, and Puget Sound.
- The **international boundary runs down the middle of it** in the real world. On the author's map both
  shores are Cascadian, which is a genuine and rare strategic simplification.
- **More than 500 oil tankers a year pass through Rosario Strait** alone, serving the Washington refineries.

**What is behind this door:** the Port of Vancouver (141 Mt, 3.5M TEU), the Northwest Seaport Alliance,
**Puget Sound Naval Shipyard and its six drydocks**, Naval Base Kitsap, Naval Station Everett, the Esquimalt
Graving Dock, Seaspan's three yards, Vigor Seattle, the Boeing Everett complex's marine access, and **four of
Washington's five refineries.** One strait, 12 miles wide at its narrowest, in front of all of it.

#### B.3.7 The Cascade passes — and the rail situation is far worse than the road situation

**Roads:**

| Pass | Route | Elevation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Snoqualmie** | I-90 | **3,015 ft** | **29,000 vehicles/day**, **420 inches of snow a year**; the lowest of Washington's three year-round crossings and **the only divided highway crossing the state east-west**; "the primary commercial artery between Seattle and points east" |
| Stevens | US 2 | — | year-round, higher, narrower |
| White | SR 410 | — | year-round |
| **Siskiyou Summit** | I-5 | **4,310 ft** | **the highest point on Interstate 5**; ~6% grade; winter closures; winds to 50 mph; the natural summit was cut down 49 feet to build the road |
| **Tejon Pass (the Grapevine)** | I-5 | **4,144 ft** | second-highest point on I-5; a **continuous 5% grade for 5 miles** |

**Rail — this is the finding that surprised me, and the author should not miss it.** Washington's entire
east-west rail capacity consists of two tunnels, and both are constrained:

- **The Cascade Tunnel (Stevens Pass)** — **7.80 miles (12.55 km)**, maximum elevation **2,881 ft**, opened
  **12 January 1929**, BNSF's Scenic Subdivision. It is **limited to 28 trains a day** by ventilation: an
  automated door system and **two 800-horsepower electric fans** need **20-30 minutes to clear exhaust
  between trains**, and crews carry portable respirators. It was the longest railroad tunnel in the United
  States until the Mount Macdonald Tunnel in BC opened in 1989. It exists because the **1910 Wellington
  avalanche** killed **96-101 people** near the old tunnel's west portal — **the deadliest avalanche disaster
  in US history**.
- **The Stampede Tunnel (Stampede Pass)** — **1.86 miles**, pass elevation **3,672 ft**, and its clearance is
  **22 feet, insufficient for double-stack intermodal cars**. Mothballed **August 1984**, reopened **5
  December 1996**.

**So: the main Cascade rail crossing is a 1929 bore that can pass 28 trains a day only because two electric
fans blow the diesel smoke out, and the alternate crossing cannot carry a modern container train at all.**
That is not a network. That is a pair of single points of failure, one of which depends on continuous
electricity to be usable by trains that do not otherwise need electricity.

#### B.3.8 The Columbia River Gorge — the most concentrated chokepoint in the territory

**Over 80 miles (130 km) long and up to 4,000 feet (1,200 m) deep**, and — in the source's own framing —
**"the only navigable route through the Cascades and the only water connection between the Columbia Plateau
and the Pacific Ocean."**

Count what is inside that one canyon:

1. **Interstate 84** and **US 30** on the Oregon bank.
2. **Washington State Route 14** on the Washington bank.
3. **Union Pacific's main line** on the Oregon bank.
4. **BNSF's main line** on the Washington bank.
5. **The navigable channel itself** — the only barge route from the interior to the sea (B.4).
6. **[UNVERIFIED] but near-certain:** the region's trunk **electricity transmission** and **natural gas
   pipeline** corridors. The parent file's §3.1.6 covers transmission; the author should confirm the Gorge
   routing before asserting it.

Two interstate-class highways, two Class I railroads, a waterway, and the utility corridors, in a canyon a
few miles wide, with **routine 35 mph winds** funneled by the pressure difference across the range. The
**2016 Mosier oil train derailment** (42,000 US gallons of Bakken crude) happened here and is the proof of
concept.

**No substitute exists, and none can be built.** Every other crossing of the Cascade Range is a pass over the
top. The Gorge is the only place the range is cut to sea level, and it is cut to sea level because a very
large river spent a very long time doing it. Sever the Gorge and the Pacific Northwest loses its sea-level
connection to the interior permanently — not until repairs, **permanently**, because the alternatives are
3,000 feet higher.

#### B.3.9 So which points, if severed, actually split the country?

**Ranked, with the honest answer at the end.**

**Tier 1 — cuts with no substitute at all:**

| Point | What it severs | Substitute |
|---|---|---|
| **Strait of Juan de Fuca** | Vancouver, Victoria, Seattle, Tacoma, Bremerton, Everett, 4 of 5 WA refineries — simultaneously | **None.** It is the only door. |
| **Columbia River Gorge** | The entire Pacific Northwest interior from tidewater, at sea level | **None.** Every other route is 3,000 ft up. |
| **Dixon Entrance / Inside Passage** | The whole Alaska Panhandle | **None.** There is no road. |
| **Fraser Canyon + Coquihalla together** | The BC coast from the Canadian interior | Proven in November 2021: **none for a week**. |

**Tier 2 — genuine north-south cuts, but all three have a sea bypass:**

| Point | What it severs | Sea bypass |
|---|---|---|
| **Siskiyou Summit (I-5) + the Klamath Falls rail line** | Oregon from California | Coos Bay, Eureka, Crescent City |
| **Tejon Pass + Tehachapi** | Southern California from everything north | San Pedro Bay, Port Hueneme |
| **The Transpeninsular Highway's Vizcaíno middle** | Baja Sur from Baja Norte | Already the only real option (parent §5.6) |

**The correction, and it is the most useful sentence in this file.** Look at the Tier 1 list. **Three of the
four are east-west cuts, and the fourth is a sea lane.** The Gorge, the Fraser Canyon, Snoqualmie, Stevens,
Stampede, Tehachapi and Cajon all separate the **wet coast** from the **dry interior** — which is exactly the
"Left Coast versus Far West" fault line the parent file identifies in §0.4 and calls the internal cultural
problem. The passes are not where the country splits north from south. **They are where the country splits
west from east, and that split is the same one the politics already runs along.**

Meanwhile every genuine north-south pinch point — Siskiyou, Tejon, the Vizcaíno — sits within reach of a
harbor. **You cannot cut this country in half by taking a mountain pass. You can only cut it in half by
taking the sea.**

So the parent file's §0 is right that the sea is the trunk line and slightly wrong about the mechanism. The
sharpened version:

> **Cascadia is not a chain. It is a ladder.** The north-south rail is saltwater and is effectively
> uncuttable by anyone without a navy. The east-west rungs — passes, canyons, the Gorge — are cheap to cut,
> cut themselves regularly without help (November 2021), and each one connects the populated wet side to the
> food, water, power and minerals of the dry side. **A hostile navy splits Cascadia lengthwise. A dozen
> people with explosives, or one atmospheric river, starve it crosswise.** The second threat is enormously
> cheaper than the first, and it is the one the interior controls.

### B.4 Inland waterways — the Columbia-Snake system

#### B.4.1 The system

Slackwater barge navigation runs from the Pacific **465 river miles (750 km)** inland to the **Port of
Lewiston, Idaho**, at the Snake-Clearwater confluence, roughly **745 feet above sea level**. Lewiston is
**"the farthest inland port linked to the Pacific Ocean"**, the most inland seaport west of the Mississippi
system, Idaho's only seaport, and the eastern terminus of **Marine Highway M-84**. The first barge left on
**9 August 1975**, carrying wheat to Portland.

**Eight locks stand between Lewiston and the sea**, four on each river:

| River | Lock and dam | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Columbia | **Bonneville** | **River Mile 146.1** |
| Columbia | **The Dalles** | |
| Columbia | **John Day** | **River Mile ~216**; **110-foot lift — the highest of any lock in the United States**; chamber **86 × 675 ft** |
| Columbia | **McNary** | **River Mile 292**; chamber **86 × 683 ft**; average lift **75 feet** |
| Snake | **Ice Harbor** | |
| Snake | **Lower Monumental** | |
| Snake | **Little Goose** | |
| Snake | **Lower Granite** | completed 1975, about 30 miles below Lewiston |

**Bonneville's lock is the one with the history.** The original **1938** lock was **the highest single-lift
lock in the world at 60 feet**. It was replaced by a new chamber **86 ft wide by 676 ft long**, built
**1987-1993 at a cost of $341 million**, with a transit time of about **30 minutes**.

**Note the standardization, because it is the operative engineering fact: every chamber is 86 feet wide.**
That single number fixes the maximum tow size for the entire 465-mile system. A tow built for the Columbia
fits every lock on it and nothing else. And because the locks are in **series**, not parallel, **any one of
the eight out of service closes the whole river.**

#### B.4.2 What moves on it

- About **10% of all US wheat exports transit the Port of Lewiston.**
- Per the parent file §2.2.1: roughly **90% of Washington wheat is exported, and over 60% of that moves by
  barge** down this system to the Portland and Vancouver terminals.
- Also fuel, pulses (the Palouse lentil and pea crop), paper, lumber, and containers.
- The **four lower Snake dams are the subject of a live removal debate** on salmon grounds. If they go,
  Lewiston ceases to be a seaport and the Palouse harvest shifts to rail and truck — a real-world,
  in-progress test of exactly the scenario this file is built to support.

#### B.4.3 Where navigation stops, and why Grand Coulee is a wall

**[UNVERIFIED] as a categorical statement, but strongly supported:** above McNary the Columbia is a staircase
of **dams without navigation locks** — Priest Rapids, Wanapum, Rock Island, Rocky Reach, Wells, Chief Joseph,
and **Grand Coulee**. McNary's reservoir, **Lake Wallula, extends 64 miles up the Columbia to the Hanford
site** and up the Snake to Ice Harbor, and that is effectively the end of the line on the Columbia. No lock
is described at any dam above it.

The consequence deserves stating plainly, because it inverts an intuition: **the Columbia is not a
north-south route.** It is a route from the sea **eastward** to the Tri-Cities and then up the Snake into
Idaho. The upper Columbia — the Big Bend, the Arrow Lakes, the Canadian reach — is **unreachable from the
ocean**, and the parent file's "single most important structure in the territory" (§3.1.2, Grand Coulee) is
also the structure that guarantees the river cannot be used to connect the country lengthwise.

#### B.4.4 The channel and the bar — both are maintained artifacts

- **Channel:** a **43-foot draft** is maintained up to Portland and Vancouver, Washington; **air draft is
  capped at 196 feet by the Astoria-Megler Bridge.** The Port of Portland runs its own dredge, the *Oregon*.
- **The 1980 Mount St. Helens eruption temporarily reduced the shipping channel depth by 26 feet** — a single
  volcanic event nearly closed the river to ocean shipping.
- **The Columbia Bar** — about **3 miles wide and 6 miles long**; the navigation channel is **2,640 feet wide
  at its western end, narrowing to 600 feet inside the jetties**. The river runs out at **4 to 7 knots**
  against Pacific swell and westerly wind, throwing up large standing waves; conditions go from calm to fatal
  in minutes. **Roughly 2,000 large vessels have sunk there since 1792 and over 700 people have drowned.**
  It is called the Graveyard of the Pacific for cause.
- **The bar is only usable because it is engineered.** The **south jetty (1884)**, the **north jetty (1914)**,
  and **Jetty A (by 1939)** train the current; the entrance is **dredged to 55 feet across the northern
  three-quarters and 48 feet across the southern quarter**; and the jetty system needed a **repair program
  running 2016-2025** after a century of service. Ships cross with one of about **16 bar pilots**, paid
  roughly **$180,000 a year**, who frequently board **by helicopter** because the sea state will not permit a
  boat transfer.

**Hold that last image for Part C.** The entrance to the Pacific Northwest's only great river is kept open by
three stone jetties, a dredging program, and sixteen people who arrive by helicopter.

## C. What Survives a Collapse

This mirrors the parent file's Section 7 tiering and should be read as an extension of it, not a replacement.

### C.1 Tier A — geography, and therefore permanent

**Natural deepwater harbors.** The distinction that organizes this entire section is the difference between a
harbor that is deep **because of geology** and one that is deep **because of dredging**.

- **Prince Rupert** — **16 meters (52 feet) of natural draft**, ice-free, the **third-deepest natural harbor
  in the world**. Nothing about that depth was built, so nothing about it can stop working.
- **Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia, Burrard Inlet, Esquimalt, Sitka Sound, San Diego Bay, Bahía de Todos
  Santos** — glacially carved or tectonically formed deep water, sheltered, and self-maintaining.
- **San Francisco Bay** is the mixed case: naturally deep at the Golden Gate, dredged at the berths.
- **San Pedro Bay is the counter-example, and it is the biggest port in the country.** Long Beach alone is
  **3,520 acres of land in a 4,600-acre harbor with channel depth over 50 feet** behind a breakwater. Almost
  none of that is natural. It is a harbor made of a century of money.

**The rule, stated the way the author asked for it: a natural deepwater harbor remains useful with nothing
but a rowboat. A dredged channel silts up.** On that test, Prince Rupert, Puget Sound and Esquimalt keep
their entire value through any collapse, and San Pedro Bay — twenty million containers a year — loses most of
its physical advantage along with all of its commercial reason.

**Sea lanes.** The Inside Passage, the Strait of Juan de Fuca, Haro and Rosario Straits, Puget Sound, the
Strait of Georgia, and the open Pacific. These are the parent file's "real trunk line" (§0) and they need no
maintenance whatsoever.

**Mountain passes.** A pass is a low place in a mountain range. The **pavement** over Snoqualmie (3,015 ft),
Stevens, White, Siskiyou (4,310 ft), Tejon (4,144 ft), Coquihalla (1,244 m), and Yellowhead (1,131 m) is Tier
B; **the low place is Tier A.** Every one of these was a foot or pack trail before it was a road, and the
parent file's §1.1 supplies the precedent directly: the **Chilkoot** and **White Pass** out of Skagway were
walked by tens of thousands of people carrying a year's supplies on their backs.

**The Columbia Gorge as a gap.** Strip out I-84, SR-14, both railroads, and the locks, and the Gorge is still
**the only place on the entire Cascade Range where you can travel from the Pacific to the interior plateau
without climbing**. That was true for the peoples who used it as a trade corridor for thousands of years and
it will be true afterward.

**Rivers in their natural state.** The lower Columbia below the Cascades rapids, the Willamette below the
falls at Oregon City, the Fraser below Hope, and the Sacramento are all navigable to small craft with no
engineering at all — which is exactly how the fur trade and the overland migrations used them.
**[UNVERIFIED]** the specific natural heads of navigation; check the pre-dam Army Corps surveys.

### C.2 Tier B — survives only if maintained, ranked by how fast it fails

1. **Lock gates and dredged channels — first to go, in seasons rather than decades.** Eight locks in
   **series** between the sea and Lewiston; any one out of service closes all 465 miles. The Columbia's
   43-foot channel, the bar entrance at 55/48 feet, and the Fraser's tidally assisted 11.5 m all depend on
   continuous dredging. **[UNVERIFIED]** siltation rates, but the direction is not in doubt: the 1980 Mount
   St. Helens eruption took **26 feet** off the shipping channel in a day.
2. **The Cascade Tunnel's ventilation — fails the moment the grid does.** Two 800-horsepower electric fans,
   20-30 minutes between trains, 28 trains a day. No power, no fans, no diesel trains through a 7.8-mile
   bore. Worth noting for reconstruction: the original Cascade route was **electrified**, and an electric or
   steam-free traction system is the classic answer to an unventilated tunnel.
3. **Bridges, on a clock that is already running.** The Interstate Bridge lift spans, the Marquam, the
   Astoria-Megler, the Fraser Canyon rail crossings (a 160 m truss and a 250 m steel arch, one above the
   other), and every I-5 crossing. Behind them sits the **Cascadia subduction zone**: a **1,000 km (620-mile)
   fault** that produced a **magnitude 9.0 earthquake at about 9 p.m. on 26 January 1700**, with recurrence
   estimates of roughly **500 years for M8+** or a **243-year average across 41 events in 10,000 years**.
   Probability estimates run **10-14% for M9+ within 50 years (2009)** and **up to 37% for M8+ (2010)**.
   FEMA projects about **13,000 dead, 27,000 injured, a million displaced, and 2.5 million needing food and
   water.** The author does not need to invent an infrastructure catastrophe for this coast. One is scheduled.
4. **Tunnels and rock cuts.** The seven Fraser Canyon highway tunnels (1957-64), Stampede, Mount Macdonald,
   and the Gorge benches. Rock stays put; a tunnel face does not, and scaling loose rock is a permanent job.
5. **Railroads.** Ties, ballast, washouts, and slides. The **November 2021 benchmark**: about a week to
   restore any road link at all, a month to essential traffic on the Coquihalla, **ten months to full
   completion** — with a functioning federal government and a heavy construction industry.
6. **Jetties.** The Columbia's south jetty dates from **1884**, the north from **1914**, Jetty A from **1939**
   — and they needed a **repair program running 2016 to 2025**. A century of service, then a decade of work,
   in a country that could afford it.
7. **Winter road maintenance.** Snoqualmie takes **420 inches of snow a year** and carries 29,000 vehicles a
   day. Plowing is a permanent industrial commitment; without it, every Cascade pass reverts to **seasonal**,
   which is what all of them were within living memory of the railroads.
8. **Ferries.** 21 vessels, 20 terminals, 20.1 million riders. Steel hulls and marine diesels, and a yard to
   dock them in. Puget Sound's road network has water in the middle of it.
9. **Pipelines and transmission.** The **Olympic Pipe Line** (upwards of 90% of Oregon's fuel), **Trans
   Mountain** (890,000 b/d), and the Gorge utility corridor. The parent file's §3.6 supplies the perfect
   miniature: a **44-mile transmission line** from Snettisham, cut by avalanche, drops a **state capital**
   onto diesel generators.
10. **Drydocks — the slowest-failing Tier B item, and the one worth fighting for.** **Puget Sound Naval
    Shipyard's Dock 1 was completed in 1896 and is still in the inventory.** A graving dock is a stone-and-
    concrete hole in the ground with a gate and a pump; it is repairable with pre-industrial materials and
    rebuildable with 19th-century ones. **PSNS (six docks, the largest 1,152 feet), the Esquimalt Graving
    Dock (the largest non-military hard-bottom dock on the west coast of the Americas), and Vigor's floating
    docks at Portland and Seattle** are, tier for tier, the most durable heavy-industrial assets in this
    entire file.

### C.3 Tier C — simply vanishes

- **Container logistics.** Roughly **20 million TEU a year across San Pedro Bay**, 3.5 million at Vancouver,
  3.6 million at the NWSA (2016), 1.21 million at Prince Rupert (2019). Those numbers do not describe
  harbors. They describe gantry cranes, chassis pools, on-dock rail, terminal operating software,
  14,000-TEU ships, and a planetary manufacturing system. **The container is a system, not a box.** Portland
  proves the point without any collapse at all: 78% of Terminal 6's traffic left in a single month in 2015
  for purely commercial reasons and took three years to partially return.
- **Just-in-time supply chains.** Their real resilience margin was measured in November 2021: **forty ships
  at anchor off Vancouver within days.**
- **Semiconductor fabrication** (A.3.4) — gone within weeks of the first extended outage, and irrecoverable
  without ASML.
- **Large commercial aerospace** (A.1) — gone with Wichita, the Japanese wing suppliers, and the engine makers.
- **The frozen food chain** (A.5) — freezing is a permanent electricity subscription, and it thaws into a
  disposal problem the week the power fails.
- **Bleached kraft pulp, paper, and cardboard** (A.4.3) — the trees survive; the recovery boiler does not.
- **Refining at scale** (A.6) — about **2.0 million b/d of capacity** fed by crude from **Alberta and the
  Alaska North Slope, both outside the territory**.
- **Maquiladora assembly and cruise tourism** (parent §4.5, §4.6).
- **Helicopter bar pilotage** — sixteen pilots at roughly $180,000 a year, boarding ships from the air.
  Note the split, because it is the whole logic of this file in one example: **the helicopter is Tier C, and
  the pilotage is Tier A.** The bar does not go anywhere. Somebody will learn it again from a boat, exactly
  as people did between 1792 and the invention of the helicopter, and the knowledge will be worth a fortune.

### C.4 The map redraws itself

Sort the territory by tier and the center of gravity moves north. Today it is San Pedro Bay, which is Tier C
sitting on Tier B. Afterward, value concentrates wherever three Tier A conditions coincide: **naturally deep
sheltered water, rain that falls without pumping, and a river that reaches the sea without a lock.**

That describes **Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia and Burrard Inlet, the lower Columbia below Bonneville,
and Prince Rupert.** It describes almost nothing south of Cape Mendocino, and nothing at all in Baja. The
parent file's §7.4 arrives at a similar conclusion from water and food; ports, passes and harbors arrive at
it independently, which is the strongest kind of agreement two separate research streams can produce.

**The sentence to keep:** a natural deepwater harbor is worth *more* after the collapse than before it,
because everyone else's harbor stopped working.
