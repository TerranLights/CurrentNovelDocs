# The Colorado Republic — Real-World Material Base

**Compiled 2026-09-22.** This file is *not* fiction. It is a factual, sourced baseline of what the real-world
territory underlying the neo-country "the Colorado Republic" actually contains — its water, rock, energy,
soil, cities, and roads — so that the post-2083 extrapolation rests on real numbers rather than on vibes.
Everything below is present-day (or clearly dated historical) fact with citations. The single speculative
section is **Part 7, the Post-Collapse Assessment**, which is explicitly labeled as reasoning *from* the facts
above, not as fact itself.

**Territorial scope.** Per the project's own cross-reference work
(`../American_Nations_to_New_North_America_Cross-Reference.md`), the Colorado Republic maps closely onto Colin
Woodard's **Far West**. This file treats the core territory as approximately **Colorado, Utah, Nevada,
Wyoming, Idaho, and Montana**.

**The borders are the author's to set, and two overlaps are genuinely open questions:**

- Woodard's Far West extends **east** into the western Dakotas, western Nebraska, and western Kansas —
  territory the project's own map assigns to the **Prairie Federation**. This is exactly where the Ogallala
  aquifer lives, so the eastern border decision directly determines whether the Colorado Republic controls a
  fossil-water breadbasket or not.
- The Far West also extends **west** into interior Washington and interior Oregon. **Settled 2026-09-23: both
  states are wholly Cascadian**, so the Columbia Plateau and the lower Snake are **not** Colorado Republic
  territory. The map originally split them at the Cascade crest; the author adopted whole-states-only as an
  absolute rule and redrew. Woodard's cultural boundary still runs through there — it is simply an internal
  Cascadian fault line now rather than an international border.
- Woodard also puts the **Texas panhandle** in the Far West. Almost certainly not Colorado Republic territory,
  but noted for completeness.

**The organizing fact of this entire territory: it is mineral-rich and water-poor.** Woodard's own reading of
the Far West is that it is the one North American region shaped less by any settler population's values than
by terrain and aridity — it was opened by outside corporate and federal capital (railroads, mining
corporations, Reclamation water projects) because individual homesteaders could not make it work at the
prevailing scale. That dependency on outside capital and outside engineering is not a historical footnote for
this project. It is the load-bearing fact of the post-collapse assessment: **most of what makes this territory
habitable at 2026 population levels was built by an external power that will not exist in 2233.**

## Part 1 — Water (the defining constraint)

### 1.1 The aridity baseline

Nevada is the driest state in the United States, at roughly **9.5 inches** of precipitation a year. Utah runs
about **12.2 inches**, Wyoming about **13 inches**. For comparison, the United States east of roughly the 98th
meridian averages over **30 inches** a year — the conventional dividing line between country that farms on
rainfall and country that does not.
([WorldAtlas driest states](https://www.worldatlas.com/climate/the-driest-states-in-the-united-states.html);
[US rainfall climatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_rainfall_climatology))

**Flagged:** these state-average precipitation figures come from a secondary aggregator, not directly from
NOAA normals. The ordering of the driest states below Nevada (Utah, Wyoming, Arizona, New Mexico) shifts
depending on which 30-year normals period is used. The *magnitude* — high single digits to low teens of inches
— is not in dispute; the exact ranking is.

The cause is rain shadow. Pacific moisture is lifted over the Sierra Nevada and the Cascades, drops its water
on the western slopes, and arrives in the interior warm and dry. The Rockies cast a second shadow further
inland. State averages also badly understate the variation: the high country gets mountain snowpack measured
in feet while the basins below it get desert rainfall. **Nearly all usable water in this territory is
manufactured in the mountains and consumed in the lowlands, which means nearly all of it has to be moved.**

### 1.2 The Colorado River system

The Colorado drains roughly 246,000 square miles across seven US states and into Mexico. Its headwaters are in
the Colorado Rockies and the Wind River Range in Wyoming; the Green River, its largest tributary, rises in
Wyoming and runs through Utah. **Four of the six core Colorado Republic states (CO, UT, WY, and NV) are
Colorado River basin states, and three of them are Upper Basin states — meaning the Colorado Republic would
sit on the river's *source*, not its mouth.** That is a strategically enormous fact and is developed in
Part 7.

The legal architecture splits the basin at Lee Ferry, Arizona, into an Upper Basin (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming,
New Mexico, and a sliver of Arizona) and a Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada).

### 1.3 The Compact and its chronic over-allocation

This is the single most useful real-world fact in the entire file, because it is a case of a civilization
writing a legal promise its physical world could not keep, and then building cities on the promise anyway.

- The **1922 Colorado River Compact** divided the river by giving the Upper Basin and the Lower Basin
  **7.5 million acre-feet (MAF) per year each**, 15 MAF total. A later treaty obligation to Mexico
  (1.5 MAF/yr) pushes total legal allocation to roughly **16.5 MAF/yr**.
- The negotiators assumed the river carried about **16.4 MAF/yr**. They were measuring during the 1920s, which
  tree-ring reconstruction later showed to be one of the wettest stretches in centuries — some years ran near
  20 MAF.
- Actual measured flow, 1906–2024, averages roughly **14.6 MAF/yr**. Since 2000 it has averaged roughly
  **12.4 MAF/yr**.
  ([CRS: Colorado River Compact of 1922](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/HTML/IN11982.html);
  [Eos, "Fixing the Flawed Colorado River Compact"](https://eos.org/features/fixing-the-flawed-colorado-river-compact);
  [Water Education Foundation](https://www.watereducation.org/aquapedia-background/colorado-river-compact))

**So the river is legally promised about 16.5 MAF and physically delivers about 12.4 MAF.** The structural
deficit is on the order of **4 MAF/yr** — roughly a quarter of the promised total. That gap has been papered
over for a century by drawing down reservoir storage.

**Flagged:** different reconstructions and different baseline periods produce different "natural flow" numbers
(you will see 13.5, 14.6, 15.0, and 16.4 MAF quoted in different places for different things). The author
should treat "about 15 MAF long-term average, about 12.4 MAF this century, about 16.5 MAF legally promised" as
the honest summary rather than any single number.

**As of 2026 the legal framework itself is in flux.** The 2007 Interim Guidelines and the 2019 Drought
Contingency Plans expired at the end of 2026. The seven states did **not** reach consensus on a successor.
Interior issued a Record of Decision in 2026 imposing a federal Decision Framework for 2027–2036, with
Lower Basin reductions for 2027–2028 and two-year adaptive revisions thereafter.
([Reclamation, Post-2026 Operations](https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/index.html);
[Reclamation decision documents](https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/decision-doc/);
[DLA Piper summary, Aug 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/08/new-federal-framework-charts-the-colorado-rivers-next-decade))

For worldbuilding: **the real world arrived at 2026 unable to agree on how to divide a river it had already
over-promised, and the federal government had to impose terms.** That is a ready-made pre-war political
fracture line, decades before any nuclear exchange.

### 1.4 Lake Mead and Lake Powell

The two great federal reservoirs are the entire buffer between the legal promise and the physical river.

| | Full capacity | Status, Sept 2026 | Dead pool | Min. power pool |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lake Mead** (Hoover Dam, 1936) | 28.23 MAF | 6.85 MAF, elev. 1,038.19 ft, **26.2% full** | elev. 895 ft | elev. ~950 ft |
| **Lake Powell** (Glen Canyon Dam, 1963) | 24.32 MAF | 5.12 MAF, elev. 3,517.46 ft, **22.0% full** | elev. 3,370 ft | elev. 3,490 ft |

Combined, the two hold about **12.05 MAF — roughly 24.4% of combined capacity.** Reclamation's July 2026
24-Month Study projects Mead at 1,037.31 ft on 31 Dec 2026 (below the July 2022 record low) and Powell
bottoming near 3,491.75 ft in April 2027 — **1.75 feet above minimum power pool.**
([Reclamation July 2026 24-Month Study](https://www.usbr.gov/uc/water/crsp/studies/24Month_07.pdf);
[ReservoirBench Colorado River system](https://reservoirbench.com/colorado-river/);
[Colorado Sun, Sept 2025](https://coloradosun.com/2025/09/12/experts-low-storage-water-cuts-colorado-river-2026/))

**Flagged:** the live percentage-full and elevation figures come from a tracking aggregator (ReservoirBench)
rather than directly from Reclamation's own daily reports. The capacities and the 24-Month Study projections
are from Reclamation and Wikipedia's dam articles and are solid. If exact numbers matter, pull Reclamation's
own HDB data.

Three elevation thresholds matter and should be distinguished carefully, because they are three different
kinds of failure:

1. **Minimum power pool** — below this the turbines stop. Glen Canyon loses roughly **3 MW of capacity per
   foot** of lake decline. Hoover's nominal capacity is **2,074 MW**, dropping to roughly **650 MW** at
   elevation 950 ft; Glen Canyon's nominal capacity is **1,320 MW**.
   ([Power Magazine](https://www.powermag.com/drought-crippled-hoover-dam-glen-canyon-hydropower-plants-operating-at-substantially-decreased-capacity/);
   [Circle of Blue](https://www.circleofblue.org/2022/world/what-happens-if-glen-canyon-dams-power-shuts-off/);
   [Reclamation Post-2026 DEIS Technical Appendix 15](https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/draft-eis/docs/vol-3/P26-DEIS-TA-15.pdf))
2. **Dead pool** — below this, water physically cannot pass the dam by gravity through the outlet works. At
   Mead that is elevation 895 ft. Below dead pool the river below Hoover Dam effectively stops.
3. **Structural survival of the dam itself** — separate from either. A concrete arch-gravity dam with no
   maintenance does not fail quickly, but its gates, penstocks, turbines, and transformers do.

### 1.5 Snowpack dependence

**Roughly 70–85% of annual runoff in the Colorado River Basin begins as mountain snowmelt**, with the Upper
Basin figure usually given as around 80%. The Upper Basin's seasonal snowpack peaks at an average of about
**18 MAF** of water equivalent — about 70% of Lake Powell's full capacity, rebuilt and released every year.
([USGS snowpack sublimation program](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/co-water/science/snowpack-sublimation-measurements-and-modeling-colorado-river-basin);
[coloradoriverscience.org — Snowpack](https://www.coloradoriverscience.org/Snowpack);
[NRCS Colorado](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/state-offices/colorado/news/colorado-snowpack-approaches-seasonal-peak))

This is worth stating plainly for the author: **the territory's actual reservoir is not Lake Powell. It is the
snowpack.** The mountains are a natural storage and timed-release system that no one built and no one has to
maintain, and it will keep working in 2233 exactly as it works now, modified only by whatever climate the war
leaves behind. The dams merely re-time the delivery from spring to year-round. That distinction — natural
storage that survives, engineered storage that needs maintenance — is the spine of Part 7.

### 1.6 The Great Basin: water that never reaches the sea

The **Hydrographic Great Basin** is roughly **200,000 square miles** of internal (endorheic) drainage covering
most of Nevada and western Utah. Precipitation falling on it never reaches any ocean. It evaporates, sinks, or
collects in terminal lakes — mostly saline ones. The major sinks are the **Great Salt Lake**, **Pyramid
Lake**, and the **Humboldt Sink**. The **Humboldt River** of northern Nevada is the largest river entirely
within the Great Basin; it rises in the northeast, runs west across the state, and simply ends.
([Britannica — Great Basin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Basin);
[Endorheic basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endorheic_basin);
[Utah History to Go](https://historytogo.utah.gov/post/great-basin/))

Consequences the author can use directly:

- **There is no downstream.** A Great Basin settlement cannot be denied water by someone upstream of a
  different watershed, and cannot appeal to one either. Each basin is its own closed hydrological world.
- **Salts accumulate.** Irrigation in a closed basin concentrates salt in the soil over time with no flushing
  outlet. This is a long-horizon agricultural constraint, not a short one, and it has ended irrigation
  civilizations before.
- **The Humboldt is the only natural east–west corridor** across northern Nevada with reliable water. The
  California Trail, the first transcontinental railroad, and I-80 all follow it for the same reason.

**The Great Salt Lake is presently failing.** As of June 2026 it sat at elevation **4,191 ft — three feet
above its November 2022 record low of 4,188 ft.** Utah's 2026 snowpack was under half of normal by early
March. State officials attribute the long decline primarily to **over-consumption of inflow, not to drought
alone**, with roughly **30% more water diverted than is sustainable**. The retreat has exposed on the order of
**800 square miles** of dust-prone playa, upwind of over two million people, with arsenic and other
contaminants identified in lakebed samples.
([HEAL Utah, 2026 update](https://www.healutah.org/gsl2026/);
[Deseret News, Feb 2026](https://www.deseret.com/environment/2026/02/11/why-is-the-great-salt-lake-shrinking/);
[EHN on toxic dust](https://www.ehn.org/as-utahs-great-salt-lake-recedes-toxic-dust-threatens-nearby-communities))

**Flagged — sources genuinely disagree here.** On the health risk from lakebed dust, some panels of officials
have urged caution about claims of "toxic dust," noting that monitoring has not yet shown rising arsenic
levels in nearby communities, while other reporting notes that neither Utah nor the federal government
systematically tracks the frequency or chemistry of these dust storms. The *exposure* of the lakebed is not
disputed. The *measured human health consequence so far* is. Treat the hazard as real and the quantification
as unsettled.

### 1.7 The Snake River Plain and the Eastern Snake Plain Aquifer

Idaho's agricultural heart is the Snake River Plain, a volcanic arc across southern Idaho. Underneath it lies
the **Eastern Snake Plain Aquifer (ESPA)**, roughly **10,000 square miles**, and — critically — it is
**hydraulically connected to the Snake River itself**. Groundwater pumping reduces spring discharge into the
river, which directly injures downstream surface-water rights holders.
([Boise Standard reference compilation](https://boisestandard.org/idaho-reference))

**Flagged as unverified:** I know of a major Idaho water call and curtailment order affecting several hundred
thousand irrigated acres of ESPA groundwater users in 2024, but I exhausted my search budget before I could
verify the specifics (acreage, parties, outcome). The author should check this directly with the Idaho
Department of Water Resources if the detail matters — it is a good real-world example of a modern state
actually ordering irrigation shut off to protect senior rights.

The general principle is verified and is the important part: **in Idaho, groundwater and surface water are the
same water, and pumping one takes from the other.** That is true of a great many western aquifer–river
systems.

### 1.8 The Ogallala / High Plains aquifer (eastern edge only)

This sits at the *eastern* margin of Woodard's Far West and may or may not fall inside the Colorado Republic
depending on the author's border decision (see scope note above).

- The High Plains aquifer held roughly **21.7 billion acre-feet** of water in saturated material as of 1992.
- Pumping since the mid-20th century has removed more than **300 million acre-feet** — about **10% of the
  original volume**, or roughly 85% of the volume of Lake Erie.
- Saturated thickness runs from a few feet to over **1,000 feet** (deepest around 1,200 ft), generally thicker
  in the north. Depth to water ranges from around 400 ft in parts of the north to 100–200 ft across much of
  the south.
- **Two-thirds of the water lies under Nebraska**, with another ~20% under Texas and Kansas. Levels have
  actually risen in places in Nebraska; the serious declines run **from Kansas southward**, where some areas
  have lost more than 100 feet of saturated thickness since 1950.
- Depletion rate is estimated to have peaked around 2006 and to decline thereafter, largely because there is
  progressively less left to pump in the depleted south.
  ([USGS HA 730-D](https://pubs.usgs.gov/ha/ha730/ch_d/D-text2.html);
  [Ogallala Aquifer overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogallala_Aquifer);
  [Peak groundwater depletion in the High Plains Aquifer, 1930–2110](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378377415301220);
  [CU Denver, "The Fate of Ogallala"](https://clas.ucdenver.edu/ges/2025/07/18/fate-ogallala-precious-and-vulnerable-resource))

**Worldbuilding note:** the Ogallala is **fossil water** — recharge is negligible relative to withdrawal. It
is a mine, not a renewable supply. This is a fundamentally different resource from snowpack. Snowpack renews
every year forever. The Ogallala does not renew on any human timescale. A post-collapse society that
rediscovers deep-well pumping on the High Plains is spending a non-renewable inheritance, and a society that
*cannot* pump — because pumping requires energy, casing, and machine shops — simply reverts that land to
dryland wheat and short-grass rangeland.

### 1.9 The plumbing: inter-basin transfers

A very large share of this territory's population lives on the *wrong side of the Continental Divide* from its
water. Colorado's Front Range — Fort Collins through Denver to Pueblo, where the overwhelming majority of the
state's population lives — is in the Missouri/Arkansas/Platte drainage, on the dry eastern side, while the
snow falls on the western side in the Colorado River headwaters. The gap is closed by **transmountain
diversion**: tunnels bored *through* the Continental Divide.

The largest is the **Colorado–Big Thompson Project (C–BT)**, built 1938–1956, which moves Colorado River
headwaters water under the Divide (via the Alva B. Adams Tunnel) to the Big Thompson and Cache la Poudre
drainages, serving over **1.1 million residents and about 615,000 acres of irrigated farmland** in
northeastern Colorado. Seven C–BT hydropower plants generate about **770 million kWh/yr**.
([Northern Water](https://www.northernwater.org/what-we-do/deliver-water/colorado-big-thompson-project);
[Colorado Encyclopedia](https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/colorado-big-thompson-project);
[Roaring Fork Conservancy, transmountain diversion appendix](https://roaringfork.org/media/1554/gdii-appendix-i-transmountain-diversion-projects.pdf))

**Flagged — sources disagree on C–BT's annual volume.** Northern Water gives approximately **215,000
acre-feet/yr**; other sources give approximately **260,000 acre-feet/yr** with a **310,000 acre-feet**
maximum. These are probably measuring different things (long-run average delivery vs. diversion capacity vs.
decreed maximum), but I could not reconcile them. Use "roughly a quarter of a million acre-feet a year" and
cite the range.

Las Vegas is the extreme case. **Nearly 90% of Southern Nevada's water comes from Lake Mead.** Because the
older, higher intakes risked being stranded by falling lake levels, the Southern Nevada Water Authority bored
a three-mile tunnel largely beneath the reservoir to build **Intake No. 3**, whose opening sits at elevation
**860 ft — about 35 feet below Mead's 895-ft dead pool.** It was activated in September 2015. The companion
**Low Lake Level Pumping Station** was completed in 2020 at a cost of $522 million. Together they let Las
Vegas keep drawing water from Lake Mead *after* the reservoir has fallen below the level at which Hoover Dam
can deliver anything to California, Arizona, or Mexico.
([SNWA Intake No. 3](https://www.snwa.com/where-southern-nevada-gets-its-water/our-regional-water-system/intake-no-3.html);
[SNWA Intake 3 & Low Lake Level Pumping Station](https://www.snwa.com/infrastructure-improvements/intake3-pumping-station/index.html);
[Nevada Independent](https://thenevadaindependent.com/article/las-vegas-turns-on-low-level-lake-mead-pumps-designed-to-avoid-a-day-zero))

That is worth sitting with: a city of over two million built a straw under the bottom of the lake so it could
outlive the dam. **It runs entirely on pumps.** No electricity, no Las Vegas — not eventually, immediately.

### 1.10 Who actually uses the water

Agriculture accounts for up to **80%** of consumptive use in the Colorado River Basin (recent research says
about **79%**). Within that:

- About **70%** of agricultural water irrigates **livestock feed** — alfalfa, other hays, corn silage.
- **Cattle-feed crops account for roughly 32% of all water consumed in the basin**, 46% of direct consumption,
  and 62% of all agricultural water.
- **Alfalfa hay alone uses over 5 MAF of Colorado River water — about 26% of everything consumed in the
  basin.** Alfalfa uses more water than every city and every industry in the basin combined.
- In the **Upper Basin specifically, cattle-feed crops consume 90% of all agricultural irrigation water** —
  three times all municipal, commercial, and industrial use put together.
  ([Colorado Sun, Apr 2024](https://coloradosun.com/2024/04/04/research-colorado-river-water-use-cherish-hamburger/);
  [Food & Water Watch report](https://www.foodandwaterwatch.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/RPT2_2306_CORiverWaterUse-WEB.pdf);
  [High Country News](https://www.hcn.org/articles/cattle-are-drinking-the-colorado-river-dry/);
  [NASA Landsat feature](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/landsat/meat-of-the-matter-colorado-river-over-consumed/))

**This is the most important single leverage point in the whole file.** The cities are not the problem. Cities
are a rounding error on this river. The water goes to growing feed for cattle. Which means: a post-collapse
society that abandons industrial beef production — which it will, because feedlot beef is an artifact of cheap
grain, cheap trucking, and refrigeration — **instantly frees up the majority of the water this territory
currently spends.** The Colorado Republic could be far less water-stressed than 2026 Colorado is, at a much
lower population, simply by not doing the thing that consumes four-fifths of the water.

## Part 2 — Minerals and mining

This territory is one of the richest hard-rock mining regions on Earth. Unlike the water, **the rock does not
go anywhere.** Everything in this section still exists in 2233. What changes is whether anyone can reach it,
process it, and move it.

### 2.1 Gold — Nevada

Nevada is the leading US gold-producing state, accounting for roughly **70% of domestic production**. Nevada
produced **3,479,748 troy ounces in 2024** (down 13.7% from 4,030,556 oz in 2023). Total US mine production in
2024 was about **160 metric tons**, valued around **$12 billion**; Nevada's share was about **112 metric
tons**. As a standalone country, Nevada would rank roughly **10th in the world** for gold output.
([USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 — Gold](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-gold.pdf);
[Nevada Division of Minerals](https://data-ndom.opendata.arcgis.com/pages/5501d7879c2343f0975452a7c8b61d08);
[Gold mining in Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_mining_in_Nevada))

**The Carlin Trend** in northeastern Nevada is the largest and most productive concentration of gold deposits
in North America — a northwest–southeast belt roughly **5 miles wide and 40 miles long**. It passed
**50 million troy ounces** of cumulative production in 2002 and has produced more gold than any other US
mining district. The **Goldstrike** mine complex sits on it. The deposit style — "Carlin-type," microscopic
gold disseminated in sedimentary rock — is the key detail: **the gold is invisible.** You cannot pan it, you
cannot see it in hand specimen, and it was not found until the 1960s precisely because it does not look like
gold. Recovery requires chemical processing at scale.
([USGS Carlin deposit record](https://mrdata.usgs.gov/sedau/show-sedau.php?recno=51);
[USGS PP 1267, Geology of the Carlin Gold Deposit](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/1267/report.pdf);
[Goldstrike mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldstrike_mine))

Other historic Nevada districts: the **Comstock Lode** (Virginia City, silver and gold, 1859 onward) — largely
worked out but historically enormous.

### 2.2 Copper — Utah and Montana

**Bingham Canyon (Kennecott), Oquirrh Mountains, Utah.** The largest man-made excavation and deepest open-pit
mine in the world, and believed to have produced more copper than any other mine in history — over
**19 million short tons**. Nominal output is around **300,000 tons of copper/yr**; a concrete recent year
(2021) gives **159,400 tons copper, 139,500 oz gold, 7,600 tons molybdenum, and 2.22 million oz silver**. It
accounts for essentially all of Utah's molybdenum and silver and nearly all its copper and gold. Rio Tinto
operates the mine plus a concentrator, **smelter, and refinery** on site.
([Bingham Canyon Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingham_Canyon_Mine);
[Mining Technology](https://www.mining-technology.com/projects/bingham/);
[NS Energy](https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/projects/kennecott-copper-project/))

**Flagged:** the "300,000 tons/yr" and the 2021 figures differ by nearly a factor of two. The higher figure
appears to be nameplate/historic peak and the lower an actual recent year. Use the 2021 actuals as the
reliable number.

The on-site smelter and refinery matter more than the pit for post-collapse purposes. **A smelter is the
hardest thing on this list to rebuild.** It is the difference between having copper ore and having copper.

**Butte, Montana — the Continental Pit (Montana Resources).** Butte was "the richest hill on Earth," the
Anaconda Company's empire, and the copper that wired the United States. The **Continental Pit** is the only
active operation at Butte today, an open-pit copper–molybdenum mine. Since 1986 it has produced over
**2.5 billion pounds of copper and 300 million pounds of molybdenum**, with reserves projected to support
mining to **2054**. A 2006 reserve estimate gave **364 million tonnes at 0.35% Cu, 0.027% Mo, 2.2 g/t Ag**.
([Montana Resources LLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Resources_LLP);
[Montana Resources operations](https://www.montanaresources.com/operations/);
[Mining Data Online — Continental](https://miningdataonline.com/property/38/Continental-Mine.aspx))

Note for the author: Butte is also the **Berkeley Pit**, a flooded former open pit holding extremely acidic,
metal-laden water requiring perpetual treatment. It is a Superfund site that needs active management forever.
An abandoned Butte is a poisoned Butte.

### 2.3 Molybdenum — Colorado

**Climax mine**, 13 miles northeast of Leadville at the top of Fremont Pass, elevation over 11,000 ft. The
largest molybdenum deposit known, in production from 1918, at one point the largest underground mine in the
world and for many years supplying about **three-quarters of world molybdenum**. Nameplate capacity around
**30 million lb Mo/yr**.

**Henderson mine**, near Empire in Clear Creek County, producing since 1976, working a deposit roughly **60%
the size of Climax's** — North America's largest primary molybdenum producer, capacity about **18 million lb
Mo/yr**.
([Climax mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climax_mine);
[Henderson molybdenum mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson_molybdenum_mine);
[Colorado Encyclopedia — Climax](https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/climax-molybdenum-mine);
[Colorado Encyclopedia — Henderson](https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/henderson-molybdenum-mine))

**Flagged:** Climax has been idled and restarted repeatedly with molybdenum prices; the "30 million lb/yr"
figure is capacity, not a guarantee of current operation. Check Freeport-McMoRan's most recent 10-K for
present status.

**Why molybdenum matters to a reconstruction society far more than gold does:** moly is the principal alloying
element for high-strength, high-temperature, corrosion-resistant steels — pressure vessels, boiler tubes,
turbine components, drill pipe, armor, tool steels. A territory that holds Climax and Henderson holds the
ability to make *good* steel rather than merely steel. That is a genuine strategic asset in a rebuilding
world and it is sitting on a mountain pass in Colorado.

### 2.4 Silver, lead, zinc — Idaho

The **Coeur d'Alene district ("Silver Valley")** in the Coeur d'Alene Mountains of northern Idaho — Kellogg,
Wallace, Osburn, Mullan, along the South Fork of the Coeur d'Alene River — **has produced more silver than any
other mining district in the United States** and ranks historically among the top three silver districts in
the world. It has also been a major producer of **zinc, lead, and copper**.
([Coeur d'Alene Mining District](https://sterlingmining.net/coeur-dalene-mining-district-the-silver-valley/);
[Silver mining in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_mining_in_the_United_States);
[Idaho State University, Northern Idaho and Mining](https://www.isu.edu/digitalgeologyidaho/n-idaho-mining/))

Lead and zinc deserve emphasis over the silver for reconstruction purposes. **Lead is batteries and
radiation shielding. Zinc is galvanizing** — the single cheapest way to stop steel from rusting, which is what
determines whether a rebuilt tool or roof or rail lasts ten years or fifty.

### 2.5 Trona and soda ash — Wyoming

**The Green River Basin of southwestern Wyoming holds the largest known trona deposit in the world**, covering
about **1,300 square miles** in the Wilkins Peak Member of the Eocene Green River Formation. Estimated
resource: **127 billion tons**, of which about **40 billion tons** are mineable by conventional hard-rock
methods. The district supplies about **90% of US soda ash**, producing roughly **17–18 million tons/yr**.
Cumulative production since 1949 is **633.2 million tons**. At current rates the reserves last an estimated
**2,350 years**.
([Wyoming State Geological Survey — Trona](https://main.wsgs.wyo.gov/mineral-resources/industrial-minerals/trona);
[Wyoming Mining Association](https://wyomingmining.org/trona/);
[Wyoming Energy Authority](https://wyoenergy.org/portfolio/trona/);
[Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2023/11/23/producing-90-of-us-trona-theres-a-piece-of-wyoming-in-every-kitchen-in-america/))

**This may be the single most underrated asset in the entire territory for the author's purposes.** Soda ash
(sodium carbonate) is one of the foundational industrial chemicals. It is required for **glass** — every
window, bottle, lens, and laboratory vessel. It is required for **soap and detergent**, for **paper**, for
water treatment, for pH control in essentially every chemical process, and as a feedstock for other sodium
chemicals. Elsewhere in the world soda ash is made synthetically by the Solvay process, which needs salt,
limestone, ammonia, and a lot of energy. Wyoming does not need the Solvay process. **Wyoming digs soda ash out
of the ground as a rock and calcines it.** A society with a pick, a kiln, and a road to Green River has glass
and soap. That is a civilizational threshold. A 2,350-year reserve life at industrial rates means it is
effectively inexhaustible at any post-collapse rate of use.

### 2.6 Uranium — Wyoming and the Colorado Plateau

**Wyoming holds the largest known uranium reserves in the United States** — roughly **350 million pounds** —
and has historically been the top uranium-producing state. In August 2025 Wyoming surpassed Texas in current
production; Q1 2025 output was **92,595 lb U3O8** (vs. Texas's 69,082 lb). There are four active operations —
three in the Powder River Basin and one in the Great Divide Basin. Wyoming's production is almost entirely
**in-situ recovery (ISR)**: rather than digging, operators circulate a leaching solution through the ore body
via wells and pump the loaded solution back to the surface. ISR is cheap precisely because it avoids
excavation, milling, heap leaching, and large-scale reclamation. The **Irigaray central processing plant**
reached first drummed concentrate in February 2025, licensed at **4 million lb/yr**.
([Wyoming State Geological Survey 2024 Uranium Resources Summary](https://www.wsgs.wyo.gov/products/wsgs-2025-uranium-summary.pdf);
[WSGS Uranium Mining](https://main.wsgs.wyo.gov/energy/uranium/uranium-mining);
[Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2025/11/12/uranium-listed-as-critical-mineral-signaling-boost-for-wyoming-mining/))

There is also the **Colorado Plateau uranium province** — the Uravan mineral belt of western Colorado and
eastern Utah, source of the vanadium-uranium ores of the Manhattan Project era. **Flagged as general knowledge
I could not re-verify within budget:** the **White Mesa Mill** near Blanding, Utah is, to my knowledge, the
only operating conventional uranium mill remaining in the United States. The author should verify this before
leaning on it, but if true it is a significant single point of failure and therefore a very usable one.

**Post-collapse note, and be careful with it:** ISR is a *sophisticated* technology. It requires wells, pumps,
chemistry, ion-exchange resin, and power. A collapsed society does not do ISR. It goes back to digging rock,
which in Wyoming's roll-front deposits is possible but much harder. Uranium is only worth anything at all if
someone can still enrich it or build a reactor, which is a very high bar — see Part 7.

### 2.7 Platinum-group metals — Montana

**The Stillwater Complex** in southern Montana — the **Stillwater** and **East Boulder** mines on the **J-M
Reef** — is the **only significant source of platinum-group metals in the United States** and the
highest-grade PGM resource in the world. Output is roughly **78% palladium, 22% platinum**, with minor
rhodium. Historical production figures: about **380,000 oz/yr** from Stillwater and **230,000 oz/yr** from
East Boulder; total Montana mine production was **523,900 oz in 2013**.
([Stillwater Mining Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillwater_Mining_Company);
[Mining Technology — Stillwater and East Boulder](https://www.mining-technology.com/projects/stillwater-and-east-boulder/);
[CLUI — Stillwater PGM Mine](https://clui.org/ludb/site/stillwater-pgm-mine))

**Flagged:** these production figures are from 2013-era sources. Sibanye-Stillwater (the current owner) has
restructured and curtailed Montana operations in recent years, so **current** output is likely materially
lower. The *geology* — a uniquely high-grade PGM reef, the only one in North America — is what matters and is
not in dispute.

PGMs are catalysis. Catalytic converters, yes, but more importantly chemical process catalysts, electrical
contacts that do not corrode, laboratory crucibles, and thermocouples. Small quantities, outsized leverage.

### 2.8 Lithium — Nevada

**Thacker Pass**, in the McDermitt Caldera of northern Nevada, hosts **the largest known measured lithium
resource and reserve in the world** and the largest known US lithium deposit — lithium-bearing clays
associated with an ancient supervolcano.

**Silver Peak** in **Clayton Valley** is the only currently producing lithium operation in the United States,
extracting lithium from brine since the mid-1960s and processing roughly **3.8 billion gallons of brine
annually**. As of September 2024 there were about **23,500 active lithium claims in Nevada**, concentrated
around Clayton Valley.
([Thacker Pass lithium mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thacker_Pass_lithium_mine);
[Nevada Independent](https://thenevadaindependent.com/article/even-as-lithium-prices-drop-industry-expansion-in-nevada-still-underway);
[Eos — Nevada Has Loads of Lithium](https://eos.org/articles/nevada-has-loads-of-lithium-heres-why);
[BLM Record of Decision, Thacker Pass](https://me.smenet.org/blm-issues-record-of-decision-for-thacker-pass-mine-in-nevada/))

**Realism check for the author:** lithium is only useful to a society that can make lithium-ion cells, which
requires cathode chemistry, separator film, electrolyte, dry rooms, and precision assembly. That is a very
late-reconstruction capability. Clay-hosted lithium at Thacker Pass in particular requires acid leaching at
scale. **Lithium is a "century two" resource, not a "decade one" resource.** Lead-acid — from Idaho's Silver
Valley — is the battery a recovering society actually builds.

### 2.9 Rare earths

**Bear Lodge Mountains, northeastern Wyoming** (near Sundance, about 16 km northwest of the Black Hills):
thorium and rare-earth deposits over roughly **16 square kilometers**, one of the largest US resources of both
total rare earths and thorium, and one of the highest-grade REE deposits in North America. The carbonatite
hosts a distribution favoring **neodymium–praseodymium**, the magnet elements. The **Bull Hill Mine** is the
proposed operation; the project is in development, not production.
([USGS PP 1049-D](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1049D);
[USGS MRDATA — Bear Lodge](https://mrdata.usgs.gov/ree/show-ree.php?rec_id=10);
[Bear Lodge Rare Earth Project, federal permitting dashboard](https://www.permits.performance.gov/permitting-project/fast-41-covered-projects/bear-lodge-rare-earth-project);
[Wyoming Energy Authority — Critical Minerals & Rare Earths](https://wyoenergy.org/portfolio/critical-minerals-rare-earths/))

There are also thorium/REE claims at **Lemhi Pass** on the Idaho–Montana border on the Continental Divide.
([High Country News, "Rare-earth reality check"](https://www.hcn.org/issues/43-6/rare-earth-reality-check/))

**Be honest about this one.** Rare earths are geologically common; what is rare is the separation chemistry —
a long cascade of solvent-extraction stages to pull chemically near-identical elements apart. No US rare-earth
project inside this territory is in production. HCN's own framing is a "reality check," and the author should
carry that skepticism forward: **a post-collapse Colorado Republic has rare-earth *rocks*, and almost
certainly not rare-earth *metals*.**

### 2.10 Phosphate, potash, magnesium, salt

- **Phosphate:** Idaho (the Soda Springs area, in the western phosphate field) and Utah are producers, though
  most US phosphate comes from Florida and North Carolina.
- **Potash:** Utah has produced potash since 1917 and holds a state resource of roughly **2.2 billion tons
  K2O** against a US resource of **7.7 billion tons** — i.e., Utah alone holds something like a quarter of
  identified US potash. Sources include Great Salt Lake surface brine, Great Salt Lake Desert subsurface
  brine, Sevier Lake playa brine, and the **Paradox Formation** potash beds near Moab. Recovery is by
  **solution mining plus solar evaporation** — which in this climate is nearly free.
- **Great Salt Lake industrial minerals:** salt, potash, **magnesium metal**, chlorine, and assorted
  potassium and magnesium salts.
  ([Utah Geological Survey — Utah Potash](https://geology.utah.gov/map-pub/survey-notes/utah-potash-resources-production-and-exploration/);
  [UGS — Today's and Tomorrow's Phosphate](https://geology.utah.gov/map-pub/survey-notes/todays-and-tomorrows-phosphate/);
  [USGS MCS 2025 — Potash](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-potash.pdf))

**Rank this very high for the author.** Phosphate and potash are two of the three macronutrients in
fertilizer (the third, nitrogen, is fixed from air and needs energy, not a mine). **A territory holding
Idaho phosphate rock and Utah potash holds two-thirds of the fertilizer problem solved by geology.** And the
recovery method for potash — let the desert sun evaporate brine in ponds — works with no technology at all
beyond shovels, dikes, and patience. **That is a resource a medieval society could exploit.** It is arguably
the most robust mineral asset in this file.

## Part 3 — Energy

### 3.1 Coal — the Powder River Basin

Wyoming is the most prolific coal-producing state in the United States, historically accounting for close to
**40% of all US coal production** — nearly as much as the next seven states combined. **Seven of the ten
largest coal mines in the country operate in the Wyoming portion of the Powder River Basin.**

- **North Antelope Rochelle** (Peabody) has been the largest coal mine in the United States; it produced
  **109.3 million tons in 2015** and still over **62 million tons in 2021**.
- **Black Thunder** produced **62.2 million tons in 2022**.
- The twelve Wyoming PRB mines together produced about **38.6 million tons in Q2 2025 alone**.
  ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=2770);
  [WSGS Coal Production & Mining](https://main.wsgs.wyo.gov/energy/coal/coal-production-mining);
  [North Antelope Rochelle Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Antelope_Rochelle_Mine);
  [Black Thunder Coal Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Thunder_Coal_Mine);
  [USGS EROS — Powder River Basin](https://eros.usgs.gov/earthshots/powder-river-basin-wyoming-usa))

**The critical geological detail for post-collapse purposes: PRB coal is in seams up to ~100 feet thick, close
enough to the surface to be strip-mined, and it is sub-bituminous and low in sulfur.** This is not deep-shaft
coal requiring pit props, ventilation, pumping, and a mining culture. **This is coal you can reach with a
shovel.** Low energy density and high moisture make it poor for long-haul transport and mediocre for
metallurgical use, but excellent for burning near the mine.

Other coal: Colorado's North Fork Valley, Utah's Book Cliffs/Emery fields, Wyoming's Hanna and Kemmerer
fields, and Montana's Colstrip-area deposits.

### 3.2 Electricity generation as it stands

- **Wyoming's 2025 generation mix: 58.5% coal, 27.1% wind, 9.9% natural gas, 2.3% hydro, 1.1% solar**, the
  rest miscellaneous. Wyoming is a massive net *exporter* of electricity.
  ([List of power stations in Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Wyoming))
- **Jim Bridger** (Sweetwater County, WY): **2,326 MW**; Units 1 and 2 converted from coal to gas in 2024.
  ([Jim Bridger Steam Plant, Global Energy Monitor](https://www.gem.wiki/Jim_Bridger_Steam_Plant);
  [WyoFile](https://wyofile.com/pacificorp-throws-lifeline-to-one-wyoming-power-plant-confirms-end-of-coal-at-another/))
- **Intermountain Power Plant** (Delta, UT): **1,900 MW**, owned by the Intermountain Power Agency and
  operated by the **Los Angeles** Department of Water and Power, scheduled for replacement from 2025 with an
  **840 MW** gas plant designed to also burn hydrogen.
  ([Intermountain Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermountain_Power_Plant))
- **Colstrip** (eastern Montana): roughly **700 MW** represented by one utility's partial ownership share;
  total plant capacity is larger. ([Puget Sound Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puget_Sound_Energy))

**The Intermountain Power Plant is the tell.** A 1,900 MW power station in the Utah desert, owned by a Utah
agency, operated by *Los Angeles*, sending its power to *California* over a dedicated DC line. That is the Far
West's defining relationship in one sentence: **the interior produces the energy and an outside coastal
metropolis consumes it and paid to build the machinery.** When the outside power disappears, so does the
reason the plant exists at that scale.

### 3.3 Hydroelectric

- **Hoover Dam:** nominal **2,074 MW**, falling to roughly **650 MW** at elevation 950 ft.
- **Glen Canyon Dam:** nominal **1,320 MW**, losing about **3 MW per foot** of reservoir decline, with minimum
  power pool at elevation 3,490 ft — which April 2027 projections come within **1.75 feet** of.
- **Colorado–Big Thompson:** seven plants, about **770 million kWh/yr**.
  ([Reclamation Post-2026 DEIS TA-15](https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/draft-eis/docs/vol-3/P26-DEIS-TA-15.pdf);
  [Glen Canyon Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_Canyon_Dam);
  [Land Desk on Glen Canyon hydropower](https://www.landdesk.org/p/glen-canyon-dam-hydropower-whats))

Note that Hoover and Glen Canyon are both on the *southern* margin of the territory — Hoover is on the
Nevada–Arizona line, Glen Canyon is in northern Arizona just below the Utah line. Whether they are inside the
Colorado Republic is a border question. There is also substantial hydro in Montana and Idaho (the Missouri and
Snake systems) that I did not have budget to quantify; **flagged as a gap worth filling.**

### 3.4 Geothermal — Nevada

In 2024 **Nevada accounted for about 25% of all US utility-scale geothermal generation**, second only to
California, with about **twenty geothermal plants supplying roughly 8% of the state's own electricity**.
([EIA Nevada State Analysis](https://www.eia.gov/states/NV/analysis);
[EIA Nevada Electricity Profile 2024](https://www.eia.gov/ELECTRICITY/STATE/nevada/);
[Nevada Governor's Office of Energy, 2023 Status of Energy](https://www.energy.nv.gov/siteassets/content/home/features/2023_Status_of_Energy_Report.pdf))

Geothermal deserves special attention in a post-collapse frame because **it is the one large-scale electricity
source in this territory that does not depend on fuel logistics, on a reservoir that can fall, or on weather.**
Basin and Range extensional faulting puts hot water within drilling reach across much of Nevada — the same
tectonics that made the mountains made the heat. It is baseload, it is local, and the resource is permanent.
Its weakness is that it requires *drilling* and *turbines*, both of which are hard.

### 3.5 Oil and gas

- **Niobrara** (Denver–Julesburg Basin, northeastern Colorado) — the region's main tight-oil play, with rapid
  growth through the 2010s–2020s.
- **Uinta Basin** (eastern Utah) — rapid recent oil growth, though small compared to the Permian or Bakken;
  natural gas output there has declined.
- **Greater Green River Basin** (southwestern Wyoming) — long-producing gas province.
- **Powder River Basin** (Wyoming) — oil and gas from the Turner, Frontier, Sussex-Shannon, and
  Teapot-Parkman formations around the Niobrara.
  ([WSGS Greater Green River Basin](https://main.wsgs.wyo.gov/energy/oil-gas/oil-gas-basins/greater-green-river-basin);
  [EIA oil and gas maps](https://www.eia.gov/maps/oil-naturalgas.php);
  [EIA, regional production forecast](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=62283);
  [Novi Labs — Uinta Basin](https://novilabs.com/uinta-basin/))

**Flagged:** EIA does not publish a current standalone Green River Basin production series, and the Niobrara
is folded into EIA's "rest of Lower 48" aggregate in its regional forecasts, so clean state-by-basin
production numbers for 2025 were not obtainable in the time available. Treat the *existence and relative
scale* of these plays as established and the exact volumes as unverified.

**The crucial realism point:** essentially all of this is **tight oil and gas requiring horizontal drilling and
hydraulic fracturing.** That is arguably the single most technology-dependent extraction method humans have
ever deployed at scale — it needs steerable drilling rigs, downhole telemetry, high-pressure pumps, engineered
proppant, and a supply chain of specialty chemicals. **No post-collapse society is fracking.** Conventional
vertical wells in this territory are largely depleted. The author should treat the region's oil and gas as
effectively **gone** after the war in a way that the coal, trona, and potash are not.

### 3.6 Oil shale — the great mirage

**Flagged as general knowledge, not verified in this research pass:** the **Green River Formation** of
Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming contains the largest known oil shale resource on Earth — on the order of a
trillion-plus barrels of oil equivalent in place. It has never been commercially produced, despite repeated
booms (most famously ending with Exxon's "Black Sunday" withdrawal from western Colorado in 1982). The
kerogen is not oil; it must be cooked at high temperature, which consumes enormous energy and water.

This is worth including precisely *because* it is a mirage. It is the perfect object for a post-collapse
polity's founding myth — an "infinite" energy resource sitting in the rock under Rifle and Vernal that nobody
has ever been able to get out, and that the Republic's engineers will keep failing to get out. The author
should verify the specific numbers before using them.

### 3.7 Wind and solar

Wyoming already generates **27.1% of its electricity from wind** (2025). The high plains of Wyoming and
Montana are among the best onshore wind resources in North America; the southwestern deserts of Nevada and
Utah are among the best solar resources.

Both are *fabrication*-dependent rather than *fuel*-dependent, which cuts both ways. A working wind turbine
needs no supply line. Building one needs rare-earth magnets (usually), fiberglass or carbon composite blades,
a gearbox, and a 300-foot tower. Photovoltaics need semiconductor-grade silicon and a fab. **Neither is a
reconstruction technology; both are salvage technologies for as long as the existing hardware lasts, and then
they stop.** Simple mechanical windmills for water pumping, by contrast, are entirely within reach of a
blacksmith — and are exactly what this territory used before rural electrification.

## Part 4 — Agriculture

### 4.1 The basic division

Agriculture in this territory splits into three categories that behave completely differently under collapse:

1. **Dryland grain** — grows on rainfall alone. Montana's wheat belt is the main example. Survives.
2. **Rangeland grazing** — cattle and sheep on unimproved native range, moved seasonally. Survives, at
   drastically lower stocking rates than current feedlot-supported numbers imply.
3. **Irrigated row crops** — Idaho potatoes, Colorado and Utah valley farming, alfalfa everywhere. **Depends
   entirely on moving water**, and therefore on whatever diversion works, canals, headgates, siphons, and (in
   many places) pumps still function.

### 4.2 Idaho and the Snake River Plain

Idaho is the number one potato-producing state in the nation.

- **2025 planted potato acreage: ~315,000 acres** (USDA NASS projection) / **313,045 acres** (Idaho Potato
  Commission count), of which about **32,850 acres** are seed potatoes. US total planted: **912,000 acres**.
- **2023 production: 143 million hundredweight**, about 30% of the US total.
- **2024 production: ~134 million hundredweight**, about 32% of national output.
  ([USDA NASS Idaho State Agriculture Overview](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Quick_Stats/Ag_Overview/stateOverview.php?state=IDAHO);
  [Idaho Farm Bureau, 2025 crop acres](https://www.idahofb.org/news-room/posts/a-look-at-idaho-crop-acres-in-2025/);
  [Agriculture in Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Idaho))

**Flagged:** the NASS projection and the Idaho Potato Commission's count differ slightly (315,000 vs. 313,045)
— they are measuring at different times with different methods. Not a meaningful discrepancy, but noted.

The whole thing sits on the Snake River Plain and depends on controlled irrigation drawn from the Snake and
from the Eastern Snake Plain Aquifer, which are the same water (see 1.7). **The potato is a genuinely
excellent post-collapse crop** — high calories per acre, high calories per gallon of water, stores through
winter in a root cellar, propagates vegetatively from saved tubers without needing a seed industry, and
tolerates cool short seasons. Idaho does not grow potatoes because of a marketing decision. It grows them
because the Snake River Plain's volcanic soils, cool nights, and irrigation are close to ideal for the crop.
**That advantage is geological and climatic and it survives.**

The seed potato acreage is worth noticing: **10% of Idaho's potato ground exists to produce planting stock for
the other 90%** (and for other states). That is an institution, not a field — certification, disease testing,
isolation distances. It is exactly the kind of thing that quietly disappears in a collapse and takes decades to
rebuild, and its loss shows up later as blight and degenerating yields.

### 4.3 Montana and dryland wheat

- **Montana all-wheat production 2025: 172.12 million bushels**, down 7% from 2024, at **34.2 bushels/acre**
  (down 3.0 bu from the prior year).
- Winter wheat: about **91.50 million bushels** in the year reported. Durum: about **19.78 million bushels**.
- Typical field-level yields reported in 2025 ranged around **50–70 bushels/acre** in better ground.
  ([USDA NASS Montana Crop Production, Jan 2025](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Montana/Publications/News_Releases/2025/MT-Crop-Production-01102025.pdf);
  [USDA NASS Small Grains 2025 Summary](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/5t34sj573/bc388k72n/g445fd47g/smgr0925.pdf);
  [Red River Farm Network](https://www.rrfn.com/2025/08/06/montana-wheat-yields-range-from-50-to-70-bushels-for-neumiller/))

**Flagged:** the winter and durum figures in the NASS release are compared against 2023, while the all-wheat
figure is compared against 2024, so the year-over-year framings in my summary are not consistent with each
other. The absolute production numbers are the reliable part.

A statewide average of **34.2 bu/acre is a dryland number** — it is low by Midwestern irrigated standards and
it is exactly what you would expect from farming on 13 inches of rain. **That is the honest yield expectation
for this territory without pumped water, and it is the single most important agricultural number in the
file.** It is also achievable with 19th-century technology, which the Idaho potato crop, at its current
intensity, is not.

The trade-off is **fallow**. Dryland wheat on the northern plains is typically grown on a crop–fallow rotation
where half the ground sits idle each year accumulating moisture. Effective productive area is therefore
roughly half of nominal cropland.

### 4.4 Livestock

- **Colorado, 1 Jan 2024: 2.61 million head** of all cattle and calves (down 2%, 40,000 head, from 2023).
- **Wyoming, 1 Jan 2024: 1.26 million head**, unchanged from 2023.
  ([USDA NASS Colorado Cattle Inventory](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Colorado/Publications/News_Releases/2024/CO-Cattle-Inventory-01312024.pdf);
  [USDA NASS Wyoming Cattle Facts](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Wyoming/Publications/Brochures/Wyoming-Cattle-Facts.pdf);
  [NASS Cattle Inventory series](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Charts_and_Maps/Cattle/inv.php))

**Flagged:** I could not obtain 2025 or 2026 inventory figures for Montana, Idaho, Utah, or Nevada within
budget. US cattle inventory was reported "down slightly" as of January 2026. Treat the Colorado and Wyoming
2024 numbers as illustrative rather than a complete regional census.

**Be blunt about what these numbers mean.** Colorado's 2.6 million cattle are not 2.6 million animals grazing
on grass. A large fraction are in feedlots — concentrated, eating irrigated corn and alfalfa, finished on
grain, and shipped. Take away the irrigated feed (see 1.10: cattle feed is 90% of Upper Basin agricultural
water) and take away the trucking, and the sustainable herd is whatever the **native range** can carry.

Native rangeland in this territory is *poor*. Nevada and Utah rangeland requires very large acreages per
animal unit — figures on the order of tens of acres per animal-unit-month are common in the Great Basin, which
is why historical ranches there were measured in tens of thousands of acres. **Flagged: I did not verify
specific AUM stocking rates and the author should not quote a number from me.** The qualitative conclusion is
solid and important: **this territory can support a substantial ranching culture and a small fraction of its
current cattle numbers, simultaneously.** Sheep, which handle sparse forage better than cattle, historically
had a large presence here (Basque herding in Nevada and Idaho) and would likely re-expand.

### 4.5 Front Range and valley agriculture

The Colorado–Big Thompson Project alone irrigates about **615,000 acres** in northeastern Colorado
([Northern Water](https://www.northernwater.org/what-we-do/deliver-water/colorado-big-thompson-project)).
Similar patterns hold for Utah's Wasatch Front valleys, the Uncompahgre and Grand Valley in western Colorado,
the San Luis Valley, the Bear River valley, and the Montana river bottoms.

The **San Luis Valley** in southern Colorado deserves a specific note as a cautionary case, **flagged as
general knowledge I could not verify in this pass**: it is a high, cold, extremely arid closed-ish basin that
supports major potato and barley production entirely through aquifer pumping and surface diversion, and it has
been in a sustained, legally contentious aquifer drawdown. It is a model of exactly the kind of agriculture
that does not survive the loss of pumps.

**Summary of agricultural collapse exposure:**

| Type | Water source | Survives collapse? |
|---|---|---|
| Montana/eastern-plains dryland wheat | rainfall | **Yes**, at ~34 bu/acre with fallow |
| River-bottom irrigation by gravity ditch | diverted surface water | **Mostly yes** — gravity canals are repairable by hand |
| High-lift / pumped irrigation | pumps | **No** |
| Deep-well aquifer irrigation (Ogallala, ESPA wells, San Luis) | pumps | **No** |
| Transmountain-diverted Front Range farming | tunnels + gravity | **Partly** — tunnels are gravity-fed and durable, but need maintenance |
| Feedlot beef | irrigated feed + trucking | **No** |
| Native-range grazing | rainfall | **Yes**, at a small fraction of current headcount |

### 5.2 What kind of cities these are

**Denver** is the only one of the four with a *reason* to be where it is independent of a single piece of
20th-century engineering: it sits at the junction of the plains and the mountains, at the mouth of the
mineral country, on the natural north–south corridor at the foot of the Front Range, with the South Platte
running through it and snowmelt-fed streams coming off the range behind it. It was a supply town for mining
before it was anything else. **That logic still works in 2233.** Denver at some size is geographically
inevitable. Denver at 3 million is not.

**Salt Lake City** likewise has a real site logic: a well-watered bench at the foot of the Wasatch, fed by
canyon streams (City Creek, Big and Little Cottonwood), at the point where the mountain wall meets the Great
Basin. It was founded in 1847 by people with hand tools and gravity ditches and it worked. **The Wasatch
Front's original settlement pattern — a string of towns each at the mouth of its own canyon, each irrigating
its own bench — is close to a textbook design for post-collapse habitation.** The modern Wasatch Front's 2.7
million people are a different matter.

**Las Vegas** has no site logic whatsoever. It is a set of artesian springs in the Mojave that became a
railroad water stop, then a place to build a dam, then a legal-gambling jurisdiction, then a metropolis. It
sits in a basin receiving around four inches of rain a year. It draws **90% of its water from Lake Mead
through pumps** and it is habitable in summer only because of air conditioning. **Las Vegas is the purest
artifact-city on the continent** and its post-collapse fate is not in serious doubt.

**Boise** sits on the Boise River at the western end of the Snake River Plain with real agricultural land
around it. Of the four, Boise is arguably the best-sited for a low-technology future: water, farmland,
moderate climate, defensible valley.

### 5.3 The industrial base is thin

This is important and the author should not overestimate it. The interior West is an **extraction** economy
with limited heavy manufacturing.

- **EVRAZ Rocky Mountain Steel, Pueblo, Colorado** — the first integrated steel mill built west of the
  Mississippi, first rail produced in 1882, grown by Colorado Fuel and Iron (CF&I) into the largest steel
  producer in the West. As of 2015 its rail mill was **the only domestic producer of deep-head hardened rail**,
  with about **520,000 tons/yr** capacity.
  ([Evraz Pueblo, Global Energy Monitor](https://www.gem.wiki/Evraz_Pueblo_steel_plant);
  [Colorado Fuel and Iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Fuel_and_Iron);
  [CLUI, Old Steel: The West](https://clui.org/projects/old-steel/old-steel-west))
- **Geneva Steel, Vineyard/Provo, Utah** — built with **federal** funds in 1944 on the shore of Utah Lake,
  operated by Columbia Steel and U.S. Steel; **closed in 2001 and nearly completely demolished.**
  ([Geneva Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Steel))
- **Kennecott's Utah smelter and refinery** (see 2.2) — the region's copper metallurgy.

Note the pattern in both steel mills: one built by eastern capital (Rockefeller and Gould money behind CF&I),
one built by the federal government for the Second World War, and one of the two already gone. **This
territory has historically never built its own heavy industry out of its own capital.** That is Woodard's
thesis showing up in the physical plant.

**Flagged:** I could not, within budget, verify the current operating status of the EVRAZ Pueblo mill (it has
had ownership questions given Evraz's Russian parent and post-2022 sanctions) or find good data on Nevada or
Montana smelting capacity beyond Kennecott. The Anaconda smelter in Montana is long closed and is a Superfund
site. Worth a follow-up pass if industrial capacity is plot-relevant.

collapse in the Midlands or Deep South produces nothing comparable, because that land was already private.

## Part 6 — Transport

### 6.1 The corridors

The interior West has, functionally, **three** east–west trunk routes and a couple of north–south ones, and
every one of them is a chokepoint.

**I-80 / the Overland Route (Union Pacific).** Cheyenne → Laramie → Rawlins → Rock Springs → Salt Lake City →
across the Great Basin along the **Humboldt River** → Reno → Donner Pass. This is the original first
transcontinental railroad corridor and it exists because it is the one place you can cross Wyoming and Nevada
with water at intervals and without a truly brutal grade. Its weakness is weather: I-80 across southern
Wyoming is one of the most frequently closed stretches of interstate in the country, subject to ground
blizzards and multi-day shutdowns — one 2020s closure lasted more than two days after a crash involving over a
hundred vehicles.
([Interstate 80 closure coverage](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/tag/interstate-80/))

**I-70 through the Colorado Rockies.** The hard one. The **Eisenhower–Johnson Memorial Tunnels** carry I-70
under the Continental Divide at a maximum elevation of **11,158 ft** over **1.7 miles** — the highest point on
the Interstate Highway System and its longest mountain tunnel. **Vail Pass** and **Glenwood Canyon** are both
listed by USDOT among the system's major engineering accomplishments, alongside the tunnel and the Dakota
Hogback cut.
([CDOT, Eisenhower–Johnson Memorial Tunnels](https://www.codot.gov/about/CDOTHistory/50th-anniversary/interstate-70/eisenhower-johnson-memorial-tunnels.html);
[Interstate 70 in Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_70_in_Colorado))

The tunnel detail worth keeping: **I-70 was closed at the Eisenhower Tunnel by a power outage.** Not a
blizzard, not a rockfall — the lights and ventilation went out. A two-mile bore at 11,000 feet cannot be used
without forced ventilation. **The single most important road crossing of the Colorado Rockies stops working
the moment the grid does.**

**Rail through the Colorado Rockies.** At **Dotsero** the line splits between the original **Tennessee
Pass/Royal Gorge** route and the newer, shorter **Moffat Tunnel** route under James Peak. Moffat remains in
use as a freight line carrying coal and other resources.
([Uncover Colorado, highway and railroad tunnels](https://www.uncovercolorado.com/highway-railroad-tunnels-in-colorado/))

**Other significant corridors** (not individually verified in this pass, flagged as general knowledge):
**I-15** running north–south from Montana through Idaho, along the Wasatch Front, and south through St. George
to Las Vegas and Los Angeles — the territory's main north–south spine; **I-25** along the Colorado Front Range
from Wyoming to New Mexico; **I-90** across Montana; **I-84** across southern Idaho along the Snake; **I-70
west of Denver** continuing through Utah to Cove Fort; and the **BNSF Northern Transcon** across Montana via
Marias Pass.

### 6.2 What happens when nobody maintains them

Be concrete about the failure modes, in rough order of how fast they bite:

1. **Tunnels fail first**, and fail on electricity, not on structure — ventilation, lighting, drainage
   pumping. Eisenhower–Johnson and Moffat both become impassable long before they become unsafe.
2. **High passes close seasonally and then permanently.** Keeping a 10,000–11,000 ft pass open in winter takes
   a fleet of rotary plows and a fuel supply. Without them, the mountain core of Colorado reverts to being
   **cut off from November to May**, which is exactly how it worked before the 1930s.
3. **Bridges.** Thousands of them, steel and concrete, on 50–100 year design lives with assumed maintenance.
   Glenwood Canyon's I-70 viaducts are cantilevered structures in a river gorge; they are not repairable by a
   local community.
4. **Rock and mud.** Glenwood Canyon, the Hogback, and every cut-slope in the mountains require active
   scaling, mesh, and clearing. Without it, roads close a slide at a time.
5. **Rail lasts longest.** Grade is everything and railroad grades were surveyed to be gentle; steel rail on a
   maintained ballast bed is more durable than asphalt. A rail corridor can be walked, ridden, or worked with
   handcars and animal haulage long after locomotives stop. **The rail alignments are, in practical terms,
   permanent gifts** — someone graded, cut, filled, and bridged them once, and the earthwork does not go away.

### 6.3 The isolation conclusion

Strip out maintained infrastructure and this territory **reverts to a set of separate basins connected by a
few hard corridors.** That is its natural state and it is what the geography always wanted. The Great Basin is
a set of disconnected valleys. The Colorado Plateau is canyon country that is genuinely difficult to cross.
The Rockies split Colorado into a populous eastern slope and a thinly populated western slope with three or
four ways through. Montana is separated from Idaho by the Bitterroots.

**Implication for a "Colorado Republic":** a unified state spanning six states' worth of this terrain is a
*hard* thing to hold, and holding it is an achievement that requires explanation — a maintained corridor
system, a rail network, a telegraph, something. The natural political geography here is a **confederation of
basins**, not a republic. If the author wants a genuine republic, the story of how it keeps the passes open is
one of its central material problems, and probably one of its central political ones too.

## Part 7 — Post-collapse assessment

**Everything in Parts 1–6 is sourced fact. Everything in Part 7 is reasoning from those facts and is labeled
as such.** It is the author's material, not a citation. Where I am extrapolating beyond what the data
supports, I say so.

### 7.0 A prior consideration: this territory is a nuclear target set

**Flagged as general knowledge, not verified in this research pass — but it matters too much to leave out of a
file written for a novel premised on a 2083 nuclear war.** To my knowledge, as of the mid-2020s:

- **F.E. Warren Air Force Base** (Cheyenne, Wyoming) hosts an ICBM wing, with silo fields spread across
  southeastern Wyoming, western Nebraska, and northeastern Colorado.
- **Malmstrom Air Force Base** (Great Falls, Montana) hosts a second ICBM wing with silo fields across central
  Montana.
- **Colorado Springs** is a concentration of military space and missile-warning command functions (Cheyenne
  Mountain, Peterson, Schriever, and the Air Force Academy).
- **Hill Air Force Base** (Utah) performs ICBM sustainment, adjacent to the vast Utah Test and Training Range.
- Legacy nuclear-weapons production and testing sites sit here too: **Rocky Flats** near Denver (plutonium
  pits, closed and remediated), the **Nevada National Security Site** (formerly the Nevada Test Site)
  northwest of Las Vegas, and **Idaho National Laboratory** near Idaho Falls.

**The author should verify all of this independently**, but if it holds, the implication is severe and
specific: **the Colorado Republic's territory contains one of the two largest concentrations of hardened
counterforce targets on Earth.** Wyoming and Montana are not incidental bystanders in a nuclear exchange.
Silo fields are struck with multiple ground-burst weapons each, and ground bursts are the fallout-producing
kind. The prevailing westerlies then carry that fallout east across the Great Plains.

Three consequences worth building on:

1. **Wyoming and central Montana** — some of the emptiest country in the territory — would be among the most
   heavily struck. Very few people die, and a great deal of land is contaminated. That inversion is
   dramatically useful.
2. **Denver and Colorado Springs** are near-certain targets, but for different reasons — Colorado Springs as
   command infrastructure, Denver as a population and logistics center.
3. **Salt Lake City, Boise, and Las Vegas** are less obviously counterforce targets. Which is a real reason
   the Wasatch Front might come through comparatively intact — and a real reason the post-war center of
   gravity might shift **from Denver to Salt Lake City**, which would be a genuinely interesting structural
   choice for the Republic.

### 7.1 Category (a): survives regardless — geology and climate

These do not care about the war. They are here in 2233 exactly as they are now.

- **The snowpack.** The mountains catch Pacific moisture and release it in spring, forever. This is the
  territory's real reservoir and its real wealth (§1.5).
- **The rivers as natural channels.** The Colorado, Green, Snake, Yellowstone, Missouri headwaters, Bear,
  Humboldt, Rio Grande headwaters, South Platte, Arkansas — all still flow. Diminished by whatever the war
  does to climate, but flowing.
- **Placer and near-surface metal.** Gold, silver, and copper in the historic districts. Not the industrial
  tonnages, but the shallow and residual ore, and the enormous quantity of already-broken rock on dumps and in
  tailings.
- **Trona at Green River.** Bedded rock, 1,300 square miles, mineable by hand at small scale (§2.5). **This is
  the most valuable single thing in the territory for a rebuilding society.** Glass and soap.
- **Potash and salt.** Brine evaporated by sunlight in ponds (§2.10). Needs shovels and patience, nothing more.
- **Phosphate rock** in southeastern Idaho. Diggable.
- **Powder River coal.** Thick seams at or near surface, strippable (§3.1).
- **Geothermal heat.** Basin and Range hot water. Permanent, though it needs drilling to reach at depth —
  surface hot springs are immediately usable for bathing, heating, and process heat with no technology at all.
- **The rangeland.** Native grass, sagebrush steppe, and mountain meadow. Poor, but it does not stop.
- **Dryland wheat country** in Montana and the eastern plains, at roughly 34 bu/acre with fallow (§4.3).
- **Volcanic soil of the Snake River Plain.** Some of the best potato ground on the planet (§4.2).
- **The rail grades and the passes.** The *earthwork* is permanent even when the surface fails (§6.2).
- **The internal drainage of the Great Basin.** Which is a permanent constraint, not an asset — closed basins
  concentrate salt and have no downstream to appeal to (§1.6).

### 7.2 Category (b): depends on surviving infrastructure, plausibly repairable

These are the interesting ones — the contested middle where the story lives.

- **Gravity canals and ditches.** A diversion dam, a headgate, and an earth ditch running downhill is
  **pre-industrial technology.** The Mormon settlement of the Wasatch Front in 1847 did exactly this with hand
  tools. Existing canal networks, where they are gravity-fed, are repairable by organized communities with
  shovels. **This is the single most recoverable piece of infrastructure in the territory and it is the basis
  of any agricultural revival.**
- **The transmountain tunnels.** The Alva B. Adams Tunnel and its kin are gravity-fed bores through rock.
  Rock bores are durable. They will need clearing, and their intake and outlet works will need rebuilding, but
  a society that can maintain a mine can maintain a water tunnel. **Whoever holds the transmountain tunnels
  holds Front Range agriculture.** That is a concrete, defensible, story-shaped object.
- **The dams as dams (not as power plants).** Concrete arch and gravity dams are extraordinarily durable
  structures. Their *gates, penstocks, turbines, generators, transformers, and control systems* are not. A
  realistic 2233 Hoover or Glen Canyon is a standing wall of concrete with a dead powerhouse — still impounding
  water, still controlling floods, no longer making electricity, and with its outlet works probably jammed.
  Restoring generation is a decades-long project requiring machining, winding, and metallurgy the territory
  does not initially have.
- **Small hydro.** Far more plausible than large hydro. A low-head water wheel or a small turbine on a mountain
  stream is within reach of a good machine shop, and this territory has thousands of suitable sites. **Small
  hydro, not Hoover Dam, is how the lights come back on.**
- **Coal mining at small scale.** Surface coal at the Powder River outcrops, and the many small drift mines of
  Colorado and Utah, are workable with picks, carts, and rails. Coal gives heat, lime, brick, and — critically
  — **coke for smelting**, though PRB sub-bituminous coal is poor coking coal. **Flagged as a real gap: I did
  not verify whether any coking-quality coal exists in this territory.** Colorado's Trinidad/Raton field
  historically produced metallurgical coal; that should be checked, because it determines whether the Republic
  can smelt iron.
- **Iron.** **Flagged as an important unverified gap.** Utah's Iron County (Cedar City) supplied ore to Geneva
  Steel, and Wyoming has Precambrian iron formations. Whether there is workable, findable iron ore in this
  territory at a scale a small society can use is one of the most consequential unanswered questions in this
  file, and the author should chase it down. Without local iron, the Republic is smelting scrap — of which
  there is a great deal, but it is finite.
- **The railroads.** Restoring rail is the highest-leverage infrastructure project available. The grades exist.
  The rail exists (and Pueblo historically made rail, §5.3). Animal or steam haulage on existing alignments
  reconnects the basins.
- **Smelting and refining.** Kennecott's Utah smelter complex is exactly the kind of asset that is
  irreplaceable if lost and transformative if recovered. Same for Pueblo's mill. These are the "one working
  factory" objects a reconstruction story can be built around.
- **Deep mines.** Butte, the Coeur d'Alene mines, Henderson. These flood without pumping and stay flooded.
  Re-entering them is possible but expensive, and the top few hundred feet of an old mine is a reasonable
  small-scale target while the depths are not.

### 7.3 Category (c): vanishes, and does not come back

Be unsentimental here.

- **Air conditioning as a basis for habitation.** Las Vegas, St. George, the lower Virgin and Colorado
  valleys. These places are survivable in summer because of mechanical refrigeration. They are not marginal
  without it; they are lethal.
- **Pumped water.** Every deep well on the Ogallala, every ESPA irrigation well, every high-lift canal pump,
  the San Luis Valley's aquifer farming, and **the Las Vegas Intake No. 3 / Low Lake Level Pumping Station**,
  which is nothing but pumps (§1.9). Immediate, total, no partial recovery.
- **Tight oil and gas.** Fracking (§3.5). Not "difficult" — structurally impossible without a global
  industrial supply chain. Treat the region's petroleum as gone.
- **In-situ uranium recovery** (§2.6). Same reasoning. Wyoming's uranium becomes a rock-mining problem again,
  and only if anyone has a reason to want it.
- **Oil shale** (§3.6). Never worked with a functioning global economy behind it. It will not work without one.
- **Industrial feedlot beef** (§1.10, §4.4). Depends on irrigated feed, grain trucking, refrigeration, and
  packing plants. The cattle survive; the *industry* does not. Herd sizes fall to what native range carries.
- **The seed-potato certification system** (§4.2), and all similar institutional agriculture — hybrid seed,
  disease-free stock, veterinary pharmaceuticals.
- **Utility-scale wind and solar** (§3.7), once the installed hardware wears out. Salvage, not production.
- **Grid-scale long-distance electricity trade.** Intermountain Power exists to sell to Los Angeles (§3.2).
  When there is no Los Angeles buying, there is no reason for a 1,900 MW plant in the Utah desert.
- **The federal land management system** (§5.4). Not infrastructure, but it is the legal object that makes
  four-fifths of Nevada mean anything, and it evaporates instantly.
- **The interstate highway system as a *system*.** Individual segments persist as roads. The continuous,
  all-weather, high-speed network does not (§6.2).
- **Rare-earth metal production** (§2.9) and **lithium-ion battery production** (§2.8). The ore is there; the
  separation and manufacturing chains are a global-civilization capability.

### 7.4 Carrying capacity — the blunt version

**This is my reasoning, not a sourced figure. Treat every number here as an argued estimate the author is free
to overrule.**

Present population of the six core states: **~15.84 million** (§5.1).

The relevant question is: how many people can this territory feed and warm using only category (a) and
category (b)? Three ways of triangulating it:

**Approach 1 — the pre-engineering benchmark.** The Far West's population before the great federal water
projects gives a floor. Reclamation's big works (Hoover 1936, C–BT 1938–56, Glen Canyon 1963) are 20th-century.
Before them, this territory supported mining camps, ranches, railroad towns, and the irrigated Mormon valleys
— **on the order of one to two million people across the six states**, and even that depended on eastern
capital, imported goods, and rail. **Flagged: I did not verify historical census figures for this and the
author should check them — they are easy to find and would materially sharpen this estimate.**

**Approach 2 — the food math.** Dryland wheat at ~34 bu/acre (§4.3) on a crop-fallow rotation yields roughly
17 bu/acre/year averaged over the rotation, about 1,000 lb of grain, very roughly **1.5 million calories per
acre per year** — perhaps 1.5 to 2 person-years of calories per acre of *cropland-plus-fallow*, before losses,
seed, and feed. Montana has on the order of five million acres of wheat, which is a real but not enormous
number of people. Irrigated river-bottom land is far more productive per acre but far more limited in extent.
Rangeland feeds people at a very low density.

**Approach 3 — the water math.** Take the ~12.4 MAF the Colorado actually delivers this century (§1.3),
subtract the ~79% currently going to agriculture (§1.10), and note that a low-technology society's municipal
water needs are a tiny fraction of modern per-capita use. **Water is not the binding constraint at low
population.** It is the binding constraint at *current* population and current consumption patterns. This is
the key reframe: the territory is water-poor for 15.84 million people living a 2026 lifestyle, and reasonably
water-adequate for two million people farming river bottoms.

**My estimate:** a self-sufficient Colorado Republic in the reconstruction era supports somewhere in the range
of **1.5 to 4 million people**, with the wide range depending almost entirely on three variables:

1. **How much gravity irrigation is restored** — the single biggest lever.
2. **Whether the eastern grain plains are inside the borders** (the Prairie Federation overlap question).
3. **Whether trade exists.** An isolated Republic sits at the bottom of that range. One that trades trona,
   soda ash, potash, metal, and coal for Midwestern grain and Cascadian timber and fish sits well above the top
   of it, possibly far above.

That last point is worth stating explicitly, because it is the *interesting* answer: **this territory's
natural post-collapse role is not to be self-sufficient. It is to be the supplier.** It has the glass, the
soap, the fertilizer, the metal, the coal, and the moly-steel that everybody else needs and nobody else has in
one place. It does not have the food. A Colorado Republic that thrives is a **trading** Republic with a
permanent structural food deficit and a permanent structural materials surplus — which means its foreign
policy, its border decisions, and its transport problem are all the *same* problem, and that problem is the
engine of its politics.

### 7.6 What the Republic can actually make

**Immediately (hand tools, sunlight, gravity):**
salt, potash, soda ash (trona), phosphate rock, lime, brick, adobe, timber from the mountain forests, wool,
leather, dried and root-cellared food, coal from outcrops, placer gold, **glass** and **soap** as soon as
there is a kiln.

**Within a generation (organized labor, a forge, small water power):**
restored gravity irrigation, restored rail alignments with animal haulage, charcoal and coke, small-scale iron
and copper smelting from scrap and shallow ore, lead and zinc from the Coeur d'Alene, galvanizing, lead-acid
batteries, black powder (saltpeter is a problem; **flagged as unverified whether nitrate deposits exist in
this territory**), cement, small hydro, geothermal direct-use heating and greenhouses.

**Long-horizon (a real industrial base, and probably only with outside trade):**
molybdenum alloy steel from Climax/Henderson — **the Republic's signature export and the thing it can offer
that nobody else can** — copper refining at Kennecott scale, rail manufacture at Pueblo, platinum-group
catalysts from Stillwater, machine tools, restored large hydro.

**Probably never, without a full global industrial civilization:**
rare-earth metals, lithium-ion cells, semiconductors, fracking, in-situ uranium, oil shale, utility-scale wind
and solar manufacture.

**Permanent structural shortfalls, regardless of effort:**
**food** (above roughly two million people), **timber at scale** (the mountain forests are real but slow-growing
and the territory is mostly treeless), **fiber** (no cotton; wool and hemp/flax are the answers), and **almost
everything that requires a coastline** — fish, salt-free trade, cheap bulk shipping.

### 7.7 The five things the author should take away

1. **The snowpack is the wealth and it is indestructible.** The dams are convenience. Frame water security
   around who controls the *high country*, not who controls the reservoirs.
2. **Eighty percent of the water currently grows cattle feed.** Losing industrial beef *solves* the water
   crisis. The post-collapse Republic is, counterintuitively, **less** water-stressed than 2026 Colorado.
3. **Trona, potash, and phosphate are the real treasure, not gold.** Glass, soap, and fertilizer from rocks
   you can dig with a shovel, in quantities that are effectively infinite. This is the Republic's actual
   economic basis and almost no one writing post-apocalyptic fiction uses it.
4. **The Republic is an upstream power and a food importer.** It sits on the headwaters of the Colorado, the
   Snake, the Missouri, the Rio Grande, and the Platte — and cannot feed itself. That combination (control of
   everyone's water, dependence on everyone's grain) is an extraordinarily productive engine for
   international plot.
5. **Four-fifths of Nevada has no owner the morning after.** The disposition of former federal land is the
   Republic's founding political question, and it is unique to this region.

## Part 8 — Flagged uncertainties and gaps (consolidated)

Collected here so the author knows exactly what not to trust and what to chase.

**Sources genuinely disagree:**
- Colorado River "natural flow" — 13.5 / 14.6 / 15.0 / 16.4 MAF all appear, for different periods and
  different reconstruction methods (§1.3).
- Colorado–Big Thompson annual volume — 215,000 vs. 260,000 vs. 310,000 acre-feet (§1.9).
- Great Salt Lake dust health risk — exposure undisputed, measured community-level harm contested (§1.6).
- Las Vegas and Salt Lake City metro populations — urban agglomeration vs. Census MSA vs. CSA differ by up to
  a million people (§5.1).
- Bingham Canyon copper output — ~300,000 t/yr nominal vs. 159,400 t actual in 2021 (§2.2).

**Data that is stale or of uncertain currency:**
- Stillwater PGM production figures are 2013-era; current output is likely lower (§2.7).
- Climax molybdenum capacity is nameplate; the mine has been idled and restarted repeatedly (§2.3).
- Cattle inventory figures are January 2024 and cover only Colorado and Wyoming (§4.4).
- Montana wheat year-over-year comparisons in the NASS release mix 2023 and 2024 baselines (§4.3).
- State-average precipitation figures come from a secondary aggregator, not NOAA normals directly (§1.1).
- Lake level percentages come from a tracking aggregator, not Reclamation's own daily data (§1.4).

**Could not verify within the research budget — the author should chase these:**
- The 2024 Idaho ESPA curtailment order (acreage, parties, outcome) (§1.7).
- Whether **White Mesa Mill** is genuinely the only operating conventional uranium mill in the US (§2.6).
- Current operating status of the **EVRAZ Pueblo** steel mill (§5.3).
- Montana and Idaho **hydroelectric capacity** on the Missouri and Snake systems (§3.3).
- **Oil shale** in-place resource figures for the Green River Formation (§3.6).
- Rangeland **stocking rates (AUMs per acre)** for Great Basin and Rocky Mountain range (§4.4).
- **San Luis Valley** aquifer situation specifics (§4.5).
- **Historical census figures** for the six states in 1900 and 1930 — this would materially sharpen the
  carrying-capacity estimate (§7.4).
- **Iron ore** resources in Utah (Iron County) and Wyoming, and whether any **metallurgical/coking coal**
  exists in the territory (Trinidad/Raton field?). These two together determine whether the Republic can make
  iron at all, and are the highest-value follow-up in this list (§7.2).
- **Nitrate/saltpeter** deposits for black powder (§7.6).
- The **ICBM basing and military target geography** in §7.0 — stated from general knowledge, entirely
  unverified, and load-bearing for a nuclear-war premise.

**Deliberately excluded:** I have not attempted to model what the 2083 war itself does to climate,
precipitation, or snowpack. Everything above assumes roughly present-day hydrology. Any nuclear-winter or
post-nuclear-winter climate shift the author adopts would need to be applied on top of this baseline, and it
would hit the snowpack — the territory's whole water supply — first and hardest.
