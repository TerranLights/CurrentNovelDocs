# Middle East, Turkey and Afghanistan — Material and Economic Base

2026-09-28. **Survey only.** What **Turkey, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel/Palestine, Saudi Arabia,
Yemen, Oman, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Afghanistan** physically hold, and what survives a collapse.

**Companion:** `../../extractions/Middle East - Sykes-Picot, Sect and the Kurdish Question (survey).md` (Stage
1). This file does not relitigate that survey's ethnic, political, or border analysis — Sykes-Picot, the
Kurdish question, the Sunni-Shia split, Israel/Palestine, Jordan, the Arabian Peninsula, the 2026 Iran war, or
the Turkey/Afghanistan bridges to Central Asia. It builds on top of that survey materially: what the ground
holds, who depends on what, and what a collapse would and would not take away.

⚠️ **This survey inherits the same live-news caution its companion opened with, and it applies here with equal
force.** As of this research pass (2026-09-28), the US-Israel war with Iran (since 28 February 2026) has
directly struck energy infrastructure on both sides of the Persian Gulf; the Strait of Hormuz has seen its
traffic cut by roughly half; and a Houthi seizure of the Bab-el-Mandeb strait is recent, active news. **Every
fact below describing a live 2026 conflict or its economic fallout is a snapshot, not a settled baseline** — the
region's *underlying geology, hydrology, and infrastructure* (Ghawar, the Tigris-Euphrates basin, the Strait of
Hormuz's chokepoint geometry) are stable, durable facts of the kind this project's other material surveys are
built on; the *war-damage and disruption figures layered on top of them* are not, and are flagged accordingly
throughout.

## 0. The organizing facts — this region has two, and they run in opposite directions

Every prior region in this project organized its material survey around **one** dominant physical fact: East
Asia's Great Wall line, Mainland Southeast Asia's upstream Mekong, Maritime Southeast Asia's Strait of Malacca.
**This region has two, and the load-bearing point is that they point opposite ways across the map.**

**Hydrocarbons run from the Gulf outward.** Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Iran, Qatar, the UAE, and Kuwait sit on some of
the largest conventional oil and gas accumulations on Earth, and the wealth radiates outward from that fact —
this is the one thing this region has that almost nowhere else in this atlas can match. **Water runs the other
way, from Turkey inward.** The Tigris and Euphrates rise almost entirely in Turkish territory and flow
*downstream into* the region's poorest and most fractured states (Syria, Iraq); the same shape recurs in
miniature with the Jordan River basin (Israel/Palestine/Jordan/Syria/Lebanon). **The countries richest in the
first resource are, with only partial overlap, structurally dependent on outside actors for the second** — the
Gulf monarchies solve their own water problem with energy-intensive desalination (§2.4), which only works
because they have the hydrocarbons to burn; Iraq and Syria have no equivalent workaround, because the thing
they lack (water) cannot be manufactured from the thing they have plenty of (in Iraq's case, oil).

**This is a sharper, doubled version of the Mainland Southeast Asia survey's Mekong finding**, where one
upstream power (China) held leverage over four downstream states. Here, **the hydrocarbon wealth and the water
leverage sit in different hands** — Turkey has serious oil and gas import needs of its own and comparatively
modest domestic reserves, while the water-rich states are not the same states that produce the oil. No single
actor holds both master resources at once, which is itself worth noting as a structural check against any one
country dominating this region the way the Sinian Federation's Mekong headwaters position lets it dominate
Mainland Southeast Asia.

## 1. Hydrocarbons — the master fact of the Gulf

### 1.1 Ghawar (Saudi Arabia) — the largest conventional oil field on Earth

**Ghawar**, in Saudi Arabia's Al-Hasa Province, is **by far the world's largest conventional oil field**: a
reservoir roughly 280 km long and 30–40 km wide, underlying about 1.3 million acres. Original oil in place is
estimated above 170 billion barrels; **remaining reserves are still estimated at 48–70 billion barrels**
depending on source and vintage. As of 2025–2026 it still produces **roughly 3.8–4 million barrels a day —
50–60% of Saudi Arabia's entire crude output** — from a single field discovered in 1948. **Saudi Arabia's total
proven oil reserves run about 258–268 billion barrels**, the world's second-largest after Venezuela.
**Confidence: high** — multiple industry and encyclopedia sources converge on both the field's dimensions and
its continuing share of national output.

### 1.2 The North Field / South Pars — the largest gas field on Earth, shared and now a war target

**The single largest natural gas field in the world**, shared between Qatar (North Field) and Iran (South
Pars), sits 62 miles offshore in the Persian Gulf: roughly 1,800 trillion cubic feet (51 trillion m³) of
in-place gas plus about 50 billion barrels of condensate, spread across 9,700 km² — 6,000 km² on Qatar's side,
3,700 km² on Iran's. **It holds almost as much recoverable gas as every other gas field on Earth combined.**
Before the 2026 war, Qatar was producing roughly 18.5 billion cubic feet/day from its side — **around 80% of
Qatari government revenue runs through this one field** — against Iran's much smaller ~2 billion cubic feet/day
on its own side, a striking asymmetry in how effectively the two owners have developed the same geological
structure.

⚠️ **War damage, 2026 — a live, still-unfolding fact, not background.** On 18 March 2026, Israel struck the
Iranian side of South Pars directly, along with the Asaluyeh oil/gas processing complex; **at least four
sections of South Pars sustained damage, amounting to roughly 12% of Iran's total gas production capacity.**
Iran retaliated by striking Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG facility — the terminal that processes gas from the Qatari
side of this same shared field and supplies roughly one-fifth of the entire world's LNG — with damage described
as extensive enough to delay Qatar's return to full export capacity even after the war ends. **This is the
single clearest example in this survey of the same field, and the same war, damaging both a Gulf monarchy's
crown-jewel export asset and its wartime adversary's own share of the identical resource simultaneously.**
**Confidence: medium-high** on the damage figures (Human Rights Watch, the Soufan Center, and contemporaneous
2026 wire reporting converge on the broad shape; exact percentage damage and recovery timelines are still
moving targets as of this survey's date).

### 1.3 Iraq — Rumaila and the OPEC+ ceiling

**Rumaila**, in southern Iraq, is a supergiant field (~17 billion barrels proven reserves, ~17,000 million
barrels oil-in-place) that alone supplied roughly **30–40% of Iraq's 2025 output of 4.6 million bpd** — about
1.3–1.47 million bpd, operated under a technical service contract with a BP-led consortium. **Iraq's own
production is currently capped below its physical capacity by OPEC+ agreement**, held near 4 million bpd
against a real capacity closer to 4.6 million; Iraq is also OPEC+'s "chronic laggard" on quota compliance
(~72%), reflecting real infrastructure limits and a less centralized upstream sector than Saudi Arabia's.
**Confidence: high** on Rumaila's reserve size and Iraq's OPEC+ constraint; the exact 2026 production figures
are a moving monthly number, not a fixed fact.

### 1.4 Iran — the world's largest total hydrocarbon endowment, mid-war

**Iran ranks third in the world in proven oil reserves (~208–209 billion barrels) and second in proven natural
gas reserves (~34 trillion m³, ~17.8% of world reserves) — and, combining liquids and gas, some sources rank it
first in the world in total hydrocarbon reserves.** This is the essential baseline fact against which the 2026
war's damage (§1.2, and see the frameworks survey's §6 for the war's political shape) has to be read: Iran is
not a resource-poor combatant, it is arguably the single richest hydrocarbon endowment in this survey's entire
scope, currently unable to fully monetize or even safely operate a meaningful share of it. ⚠️ **No comprehensive
post-war damage assessment for Iran's *oil* sector (as distinct from the gas-field strikes in §1.2) was found
this pass** — the 2026 Kharg Island attack (Iran's primary oil export terminal) is independently documented in
the sources found, but this survey did not verify its current operational status; flagged as an open item.

### 1.5 OPEC+ spare capacity, 2026 — who could produce more, and who could not

| | Spare capacity (April 2026 estimate) |
|---|---|
| **Saudi Arabia** | ~3.0 million bpd |
| **UAE** | ~1.0 million bpd (pipeline-constrained; targeting 5 Mbpd total capacity by 2027) |
| **Kuwait** | ~0.4 million bpd |
| **Iraq** | ~0.3 million bpd (compliance-constrained, not geology-constrained) |

**Total OPEC+ spare capacity above 5 million bpd** as of April 2026 — the highest reading since 2009, reflecting
years of deliberate under-production more than any shortage of reserves. **The practical meaning for this
survey**: Saudi Arabia alone holds more genuinely available spare capacity than the rest of the bloc combined,
a real concentration of flexibility that matters for how any future collapse or realignment plays out — Saudi
Arabia is the one Gulf producer that could meaningfully expand output on short notice; nearly everyone else
already is.

### 1.6 The Strait of Hormuz's 2026 wartime disruption, as a live case study

Before the war, roughly **38% of global crude flows, 29% of LPG, and 19% of LNG** moved through Hormuz in
normal 2025 conditions (a higher crude figure than the frameworks survey's own headline 20–25%, reflecting a
different, narrower "Gulf-origin crude specifically" denominator rather than "all world oil"). **Since the war
began, Gulf crude exports have fallen by roughly 47%** — from ~17 million bpd in 2025 to roughly 9 million bpd
as of August 2026 — and Al Jazeera reporting cites a **95% drop in Hormuz vessel-transit counts** at the
conflict's most acute point. **Confidence: medium** — these are fast-moving wartime figures from a single
research pass, not a stable multi-year average; treat as illustrative of the chokepoint's real fragility rather
than as a number to cite precisely in any later session.

## 2. Water — the other master fact, and Turkey holds the tap

### 2.1 The Tigris-Euphrates system — Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP)

Both the Tigris and Euphrates rise almost entirely inside Turkish territory. Turkey's **Southeastern Anatolia
Project (GAP)** — one of the largest river-basin development schemes on Earth — comprises **22 dams and 19
hydroelectric plants** across the two rivers' headwaters, anchored by the **Atatürk Dam**, one of the world's
largest. **The consequence downstream is severe and already realized, not hypothetical**: Turkish dam and
hydropower construction has cut **Iraq's water supply along both rivers by an estimated 80% since 1975**, and
the Atatürk Dam's own reservoir-filling alone cut the Euphrates's flow into Syria and Iraq by an estimated 75%
during a single 1990 diversion episode; ongoing GAP operation has reduced the Euphrates's baseline flow by
roughly a third on a standing basis. **Turkey does not recognize the rivers as international watercourses** —
it treats them as domestic Turkish rivers subject to its own sovereign development, a legal position Syria and
Iraq both reject. **This is functionally identical in shape to the Mainland Southeast Asia survey's Mekong
finding**, with Turkey standing in the Sinian Federation's role and Iraq/Syria standing in the four downstream
Mekong states' role — except here the upstream/downstream relationship sits entirely *inside* this survey's own
region, not across a region boundary. **Confidence: high** (multiple corroborating sources: Encyclopedia.com,
ISPI, the Arab Center DC, the Maritime Executive, the International Water Law Project Blog).

### 2.2 The Mesopotamian Marshes — this region's Tonlé Sap, and it is losing its second recovery

**A genuine parallel case to the Mainland Southeast Asia survey's Tonlé Sap**: a hydrologically singular wetland
whose fate is set almost entirely by decisions made outside it. Saddam Hussein drained the marshes in the early
1990s to punish the Marsh Arabs (the Ma'dan) for their role in the 1991 uprising, reducing them to roughly
one-tenth of their original extent. **After 2003, the drainage structures were torn down and the marshes began
an unusually fast ecological recovery** — 42% of original extent restored by November 2005, rising to a peak of
roughly 75% by 2008, with researchers documenting a genuinely rapid reestablishment of native fish,
invertebrates, and birds, and roughly 250,000 Marsh Arabs eventually returning to resume reed-harvesting,
water-buffalo herding, and fishing.

⚠️ **The recovery is now failing a second time, and the cause is the same one named in §2.1.** As of the most
recent reporting found this pass, the marshes have fallen back to **roughly 58% of their pre-drainage extent
and are projected to shrink below 50%** — driven by continued Turkish and Iranian upstream damming, which has
cut the combined volume of both rivers by an estimated 60%. Buffalo herders — described as the historic
backbone of the marsh economy — are selling their herds or switching to cattle that need less water. **The
structural point worth carrying forward**: this is not a wetland lost to neglect, it is a wetland that
*already proved it can recover fully and fast, twice*, once the water is restored — its fate is entirely a
downstream consequence of upstream dam operation, exactly the Tonlé Sap's own "restore the pulse and it feeds
the country indefinitely" logic. **Confidence: high** on the restoration/re-collapse cycle and the current ~58%
figure (National Geographic, NASA Earth Observatory, the UN in Iraq, Jacobin, and Circle of Blue all converge).

### 2.3 The Jordan River basin — a second, smaller version of the same shape

Not independently re-researched to full depth this pass beyond what the frameworks survey's own §5.3 already
established for Jordan specifically (roughly 60–61 m³ of renewable water per person per year, among the lowest
figures on Earth, and a structural deficit against ~1.2 billion m³ of annual consumption versus under 900
million m³ of supply). ⚠️ **Flagged rather than filled**: the Jordan and Yarmouk river basins are shared among
Israel/Palestine, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon, and this survey did not independently verify each of those four
countries' own share of basin flow, historical diversion disputes (the 1964 Israeli National Water Carrier and
the Arab League's rival diversion plan are the well-known real-world flashpoints, not independently checked
here), or current desalination-versus-basin-water balance for Israel specifically. A genuine gap for a future
pass, structurally the same shape of question as §2.1 but at a smaller and more fragmented scale.

### 2.4 Gulf desalination — solving the water problem by spending the oil problem

The GCC states have almost no permanent rivers or lakes and answer their water scarcity with desalination at a
scale found nowhere else on Earth: **Kuwait and Qatar rely on desalination for effectively 100% of potable
water; Bahrain ~95%; Oman ~86%; Saudi Arabia ~70%; the UAE 40%+.** Collectively, **the GCC operates 400+
desalination plants, produces roughly 40% of the world's desalinated water, and accounts for some 60% of global
desalination capacity.** The catch is the one this survey's other regions don't have to reckon with: **this
solution is itself entirely energy-dependent** — Saudi Arabia alone burns an estimated 300,000 barrels of oil a
day just to power its own desalination plants. **This is the load-bearing collapse-fragility point for the
entire Gulf**: unlike Mainland Southeast Asia's rice (needs only rain and gravity) or the Tonlé Sap (needs only
a flood pulse), Gulf water security needs *continuous, functioning, fueled infrastructure* with no natural
fallback whatsoever. A Gulf monarchy that loses its energy infrastructure or its ability to run it (through war
damage, sanctions, or any other collapse mechanism this project's future-history might invoke) loses its
drinking water at the same time, on the same timeline — the two vulnerabilities are not independent. **This is
also the exact mechanism the frameworks survey's §8 and the Soufan Center source above already flag as
militarily live**: CSIS has separately assessed whether Iran could deliberately target Gulf desalination
capacity as a wartime lever, precisely because of this dependency chain.

## 3. Chokepoints — three of them, two currently disrupted by active conflict

### 3.1 The Strait of Hormuz

Covered in wartime-snapshot detail at §1.6 above; the underlying, stable geographic fact is that essentially
**all** Gulf seaborne oil and LNG exports (Saudi Arabia's, Iraq's, Iran's, Qatar's, the UAE's, Kuwait's) must
transit this one strait, with **no credible bypass infrastructure** — the frameworks survey's own headline
finding 9 already established this; this survey's contribution is the 2026 war's live demonstration of exactly
how much traffic can actually be lost when the chokepoint becomes contested (§1.6's ~47–95% figures,
depending on measure).

### 3.2 The Suez Canal and the Bab-el-Mandeb / Red Sea route

**Out of scope territorially** (Egypt is excluded from this project's West Asia region per the frameworks
survey's own settled boundary), but the Canal's chokepoint status and its 2023–2026 disruption are Middle
East-side facts because the disruption's *cause* — the Yemeni Houthi movement, based in this survey's own
scope — sits squarely inside it. **Since Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping began in late 2023** (in declared
solidarity with Hamas after 7 October 2023), **Suez Canal traffic has fallen by roughly half, canal revenue by
about 60%, and container-ship transits specifically by as much as 90% at the worst of it**; the vast majority
of diverted traffic now rounds Africa via the Cape of Good Hope instead, adding up to two weeks of transit time.
Egypt's own canal-revenue losses are estimated at roughly $7 billion across 2023–2024 alone. **The Houthis
suspended their maritime campaign in November 2025 after their last confirmed attack (29 September 2025), but
traffic was still running roughly 60% below pre-crisis levels 100 days later** — the disruption has
substantially outlasted the active attacks themselves, a real lesson about how slowly shipping confidence
rebuilds even after a chokepoint threat formally ends. ⚠️ **Most current and most volatile**: a 13 September
2026 Al Jazeera report describes the Houthi movement **seizing control of the Bab-el-Mandeb strait itself** —
the actual physical chokepoint at the Red Sea's southern mouth, distinct from the Canal further north — a
development this survey could not follow to its current resolution within this research pass, and which may
have already changed again by the time this file is read.

### 3.3 Chokepoint dependency compounds directly with food dependency

**70%+ of all GCC food imports transit the Strait of Hormuz specifically** — 80–100% of the wheat bound for
Qatar, Kuwait, and Bahrain moves through it. This is the same chokepoint carrying the oil exports that fund the
desalination that provides the water (§2.4) — **a single strait's disruption threatens Gulf food, water, and
export revenue simultaneously**, not three independent risks. No other region in this project has quite this
degree of single-point structural concentration across three different survival-critical systems at once.

## 4. Food security — a sharp, almost total inversion between the Gulf and Turkey

**Turkey is genuinely food self-sufficient and a real agricultural exporting power** — one of very few
countries on Earth that can make that claim across a broad basket, not just one crop. It produces 206
distinct agricultural product varieties with self-sufficiency in most; wheat self-sufficiency runs "well over
100%" (durum wheat production can run more than double domestic demand); Turkey is the world's **#1 exporter of
wheat flour** and has held that position since 2005; it ranks as the world's **9th-largest agricultural economy
overall** and among the top 10 global producers of fruit, wheat, and cotton. **This stands in near-total
contrast to the GCC states**, which import **up to 85–90% of all food** (90%+ of cereals, close to 100% of
rice) — a dependency so total that, per §3.3, it converges on the same single chokepoint as their oil exports
and water security. ⚠️ **Iraq, Syria, Iran, Yemen, and Afghanistan's own food-security positions were not
independently researched to comparable depth this pass** — a real gap, though the water-scarcity material in
§2 (especially the Tigris-Euphrates findings for Iraq/Syria and Jordan's own figures) already implies serious
downstream food-production vulnerability for at least Iraq and Syria specifically, not yet quantified in
calories or tonnage the way this section quantifies Turkey and the GCC.

## 5. Other named resources and structural facts

### 5.1 Afghanistan — a widely repeated trillion-dollar claim, and a very different real economy

**The "$1 trillion in unexploited minerals" figure needs the same skeptical treatment this project gave the
Araucanía-Patagonia naming question or the Mainland Southeast Asia ECI discrepancy** — it is real, widely
cited, and traces to a genuine 2010 USGS assessment (roughly 60 million tonnes of copper, 2.2 billion tonnes of
iron ore, 1.4 million tonnes of rare-earth elements, plus lithium deposits some US assessments compared in
scale to Bolivia's — the world's largest known reserve), amplified by a Pentagon memo calling Afghanistan "the
Saudi Arabia of lithium." **But the figure is also, by informed critics' own account, a "flawed and farfetched
estimate"** built on incomplete surveying rather than proven, bankable reserves, and the country lacks the most
basic prerequisites to ever realize it: no adequate roads, railways, water, or power to reach most deposits;
decades of war have driven away the capital and expertise needed to develop them; and what mining does happen
today is described as substantially informal, warlord-controlled, and a direct revenue source for the Taliban
government rather than a national development asset. **Treat as a real geological fact with an essentially
unrealized, likely-to-remain-unrealized economic one** — the gap between "what is in the ground" and "what the
country can actually extract, refine, transport, and sell" is enormous and, per the sources reviewed, not
closing.

**The opium economy, by contrast, is a real and current fact, sharply reduced but not gone.** Afghanistan
supplied roughly 80% of the world's illicit opiates as recently as 2022, before the Taliban's April 2022
cultivation ban. **The ban has been genuinely, dramatically effective by area**: cultivated area fell from
~232,000 hectares (2022) to just 10,200 hectares (2025) — a drop of over 95%. Production has fallen less
steeply in proportion (down roughly a third year-on-year to 296 tonnes in 2025) and farmer income from opium
has nearly halved. ⚠️ **Two live uncertainties, both flagged in the sourcing rather than resolved**: cultivation
is shifting geographically toward northeastern Badakhshan province, where enforcement is weaker; and large
pre-ban stockpiles plus the possible rise of alternative suppliers or synthetic-drug substitution mean the
*global* opiate market's actual contraction may not match the *Afghan cultivation* collapse one-for-one — this
survey did not find a source resolving that specific question. **Whether Afghanistan still deserves the
"world's largest illicit opiate source" label as of 2026 is genuinely unclear from the sources reviewed** —
production is far lower than in 2022, but no clear successor supplier was identified either.

### 5.2 Israel's gas fields — a genuine regional export success story

**Leviathan and Tamar**, Israel's two major offshore Mediterranean gas fields, have turned Israel from a gas
*importer* into a regional *exporter* within roughly a decade. Tamar has exported to Jordan since January 2017;
Leviathan began exporting to Egypt in January 2020, using a *reversed* East Mediterranean Gas pipeline that
originally ran the opposite direction. **2022 export volumes**: 2.9 bcm to Jordan, 6.5 bcm to Egypt. **A 2025
deal roughly doubles the Egypt-bound volume** (from ~0.6 to ~1.25 bcf/day), while Jordan's flow holds steady or
grows slightly (~0.2 bcf/day). **The strategic effect on both neighbors is real and already realized, not
speculative**: Egypt now depends on Israeli gas to compensate for its own declining domestic production and to
keep its LNG re-export business (via Damietta) running at all; Jordan has been able to substantially cut its
own LNG import bill because of stable Israeli pipeline supply. **A genuine cross-border energy-interdependency
fact, the same shape as Venezuela/Amazonia's power trade in the Latin America survey** — worth remembering
given how politically fraught the Israel/Jordan and Israel/Egypt relationships otherwise are per the companion
frameworks survey.

### 5.3 Turkey — pipeline corridor and defense-export power, not just an agricultural one

Beyond the food-security picture in §4, Turkey plays a distinct energy-*transit* role, separate from being a
producer itself: **TANAP** (the Trans-Anatolian Natural Gas Pipeline), at 1,811 km, is the longest gas pipeline
in Turkey, the Middle East, or Europe, carrying Azerbaijani gas westward toward European buyers; **TurkStream**
carries discounted Russian gas through Turkey, a relationship Turkey has maintained even as sanctions have
otherwise isolated Russia. **Turkey is also, separately, NATO's second-largest army** and has built a genuine
defense-export industry over the past two decades — drone and military-equipment exports have roughly tripled
to around $10 billion, reaching nearly 40 countries concentrated in the Gulf, Africa, and parts of Asia and
Europe. **The combined picture — food-secure, a real energy transit corridor between the Caspian/Russia and
Europe, and a rising defense-export power with NATO's second-largest standing military — makes Turkey this
survey's single most self-sufficient and outward-facing power**, structurally quite different from every other
country covered here, all of which are either resource-rich-but-import-food (the Gulf) or resource-poor and
import-dependent across the board (Jordan, Lebanon, Afghanistan).

## 6. Post-collapse assessment

Using the project's three-tier framework: **survives regardless / repairable with effort / gone for good.**

### Survives regardless

- **The oil and gas in the ground.** Ghawar, the North Field/South Pars, Rumaila, and Iran's own reserves are
  geological facts, not infrastructure — the resource itself cannot be destroyed by war or collapse, only the
  ability to extract, refine, and ship it (below).
- **Turkey's rain-fed and river-irrigated agriculture.** Unlike Gulf food security, Turkish wheat and general
  food self-sufficiency depend on the same kind of gravity-fed and rain-fed agriculture the Mainland Southeast
  Asia survey found durable for mainland rice — this is a real, structural, collapse-resistant advantage unique
  to Turkey in this survey's scope.
- **The upstream position itself.** Whatever government or successor state controls Turkey's own Anatolian
  highlands controls the Tigris-Euphrates headwaters regardless of what happens downstream — a geographic fact,
  not an infrastructure one, and the single most durable form of leverage in this entire region.

### Repairable with effort

- **The Mesopotamian Marshes**, per §2.2's own proof-of-concept: they have already fully demonstrated a fast,
  near-total recovery once upstream flow was restored, twice. Restoring them a third time requires only that
  the upstream dams release water, not that any infrastructure be rebuilt within the marshes themselves.
- **Extraction and export infrastructure at existing fields** (Ghawar, Rumaila, the North Field/South Pars) —
  wells, pipelines, processing plants, and LNG terminals are repairable given capital, security, and technical
  expertise, the same category as the Mainland Southeast Asia survey's Lao dams ("the concrete does not
  vanish").
- **Gulf desalination plants themselves** as physical structures — but only if a stable energy supply to run
  them also survives, which is not guaranteed (below).

### Gone for good

- **Continuous Gulf desalinated water, absent continuous energy input.** Unlike the Marshes, desalination has
  no dormant/restorable state — it requires uninterrupted operation, and a Gulf state that loses reliable
  energy supply loses its drinking water on the same timeline, not on a lag.
- **The current Suez Canal/Red Sea shipping equilibrium**, at least in its pre-2023 form — even after the
  Houthi campaign's own suspension, traffic remained roughly 60% below pre-crisis levels 100 days later;
  shipping confidence, once broken at this chokepoint, appears to rebuild far more slowly than the disruption
  itself took to cause.
- **Afghanistan's already-marginal opium export economy**, if the Taliban ban's current trajectory holds — the
  95%+ area reduction since 2022 is, on its own numbers, most of the way to actually gone rather than merely
  disrupted, though see §5.1's own flagged uncertainty about stockpiles and substitute suppliers.

## 7. What this means for the nations layer

Offered, not asserted — per this project's standing rule and this survey's own companion frameworks document.

1. **Whoever controls Anatolia controls the Tigris-Euphrates**, and by extension holds real, quantified,
   already-exercised leverage over Iraq and Syria specifically — a direct material echo of the frameworks
   survey's own §7.1 finding that Turkey is one of this region's two clean "founding break" cases, and now also
   its principal upstream water power.
2. **No single actor in this region controls both master resources (hydrocarbons and water) at once** — a
   genuine structural check against any one successor state dominating the whole region the way the Sinian
   Federation's Mekong position lets it dominate Mainland Southeast Asia. Whatever this region's own political
   nations-layer eventually looks like, that asymmetry is worth preserving rather than accidentally erasing by,
   for instance, merging an upstream Anatolian polity with a downstream Mesopotamian one.
3. **The Gulf monarchies' food, water, and hydrocarbon-export systems are not three independent vulnerabilities
   but one single-chokepoint vulnerability (§3.3)** — any future-history event that closes or contests the
   Strait of Hormuz threatens all three simultaneously, a much sharper version of the Mainland Southeast Asia
   survey's Strait of Malacca finding.
4. **Afghanistan's mineral wealth is a real geological fact but, on the evidence reviewed, not a realistic

Collected so nothing is lost when a section is read alone.

1. ⚠️ **Iran's post-2026-war oil-sector damage** (§1.4) — the gas-field strikes (§1.2) are well-documented; a
   comprehensive assessment of damage to Iran's *oil* extraction, refining, and export infrastructure
   (including the independently-named 2026 Kharg Island attack) was not found and verified this pass.
2. ⚠️ **The Jordan/Yarmouk river basin's own detailed water-sharing picture** across Israel/Palestine, Jordan,
   Syria, and Lebanon (§2.3) — only Jordan's own figures (already in the frameworks survey) are covered in
   depth; the other three basin states' shares and historical diversion disputes are a real, named gap.
3. ⚠️ **Iraq, Syria, Iran, Yemen, and Afghanistan's own food-security figures** (§4) — Turkey and the GCC are
   covered in comparable depth; the remaining seven countries in this survey's scope are not, beyond what the
   water-scarcity findings in §2 already imply.
4. ⚠️ **The current, September-2026 status of the Houthi seizure of the Bab-el-Mandeb strait** (§3.2) — named
   in a source found this pass but not followed to its actual present resolution; likely to have changed again
   by the time any future session reads this file.
5. ⚠️ **A genuine economic-complexity backbone for this region, of the kind East Asia's and Latin America's
   surveys achieved** (§5.4) — not built this pass; the partial GDP-per-capita table given is a convenience
   reference, not a completed analytical layer, and several countries (Turkey, Israel, Lebanon, Syria, Yemen,
   Afghanistan) have no comparable-vintage figure in it at all.
6. ⚠️ **Whether Afghanistan still holds the "world's largest illicit opiate source" title as of 2026** (§5.1) —
   its own cultivation has collapsed by area, but no source reviewed named a clear successor or resolved
   whether global opiate supply contracted proportionally.
7. ⚠️ **Turkey's own mineral resources beyond agriculture and its pipeline-transit role** — not researched this
   pass; a real gap given how thoroughly this survey covered Turkey's food and energy-transit position but not
   its subsurface resources specifically.

**Not a research gap:** how the region's material facts should translate into any nations-layer decision (§7)
