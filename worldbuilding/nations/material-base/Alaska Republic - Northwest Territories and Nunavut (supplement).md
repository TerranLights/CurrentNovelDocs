## The nine findings that actually matter

1. **The NWT diamond industry is not a resource. It is a business, and it is closing in real time.** Diavik
   ceased production 26 March 2026; Ekati went into receivership 14 July 2026; De Beers expects Gahcho Kué to
   close in 2028; Snap Lake failed in 2015 after seven years. **A collapse would arrive to find it already
   gone.**
2. **The Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road — the longest heavy-haul ice road in the world — is not being built
   for the 2027 season.** Announced 14 September 2026. The umbilical is being cut before anyone imagined a war.
3. **Norman Wells, producing oil since 1920, shut down between mid-August and mid-September 2026.** The
   Northwest Territories now has no oil production, and neither territory has ever had a refinery.
4. **Mary River on Baffin Island is 65–70% Fe direct-shipping ore — probably the Alaska Republic's only iron —
   and there is not a tree within a thousand kilometers of it.** See Part 10(e). This is the most important
   single entry in the file.
5. **Giant Mine holds 237,000 tonnes of soluble arsenic trioxide next to Yellowknife Bay, contained by an
   artificial ground-freezing plant that must run forever.** It is a machine, not a seal.
6. **The Bathurst caribou herd fell from ~470,000 animals in 1986 to 3,609 in 2025**, and the cause is not
   understood.
7. **Nunavut has 25 communities, roughly 850 km of road in total, and no road connection to anywhere.** Its
   entire electricity supply is 25 unconnected diesel plants burning fuel shipped in once a year.
8. **Every land and rail link from the NWT to the continent crosses into the ⚑ Prairie Federation** — the
   Mackenzie Highway from Alberta, the CN line to Hay River, the Norman Wells pipeline to Zama, and the Port of
   Churchill. A boundary problem as severe as losing the Panhandle.
9. **Carrying capacity without the sealift is plausibly 15,000–35,000 people against a present 83,000** — and
   the bigger problem is that the settlements are in the wrong places for it.

# PART 1 — DIAMONDS

## The headline, and it is not the one the region's reputation would suggest

Canada became a top-tier diamond producer in a single generation on the strength of four Northwest Territories
mines, and **that era is ending right now, in real time.** As of late 2026 the NWT diamond industry is closing
down. This is the single most important fact in the diamond section and it reframes everything else in it.

**NRCan, Diamond facts:** In 2024 Canada was the **world's fourth-largest rough diamond producer both by value
(11%) and by volume (12.3%)**, behind Russia (34.6% by volume), Botswana (16.8%) and Angola (13.0%). Canadian
mines produced **13.3 million carats worth C$1.47 billion in 2024** — volume down 17% and value down 30% year
over year.
https://natural-resources.canada.ca/minerals-mining/mining-data-statistics-analysis/minerals-metals-facts/diamond-facts

**The decline curve, from the same source:**

| Year | Canadian production (M carats) | Value (C$B) |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 11.7 | 2.14 |
| **2017–2018** | **23.2 (all-time high)** | ~2.7 |
| 2019 | 18.6 | 2.25 |
| 2020 | 13.1 | 1.25 |
| 2021 | 17.6 | 1.90 |
| 2022 | 16.3 | 2.44 |
| 2023 | 16.0 | 2.09 |
| **2024** | **13.3** | **1.47** |

NRCan's own forward assessment is unusually blunt for a government page: production is falling because of mine
closures, exhaustion of the high-grade near-surface material, and declining ore grades, compounded by weaker
demand for natural stones, **competition from lab-grown diamonds**, and the fact that Canada's mines are simply
**near the end of their operational lives**. It expects the trend to continue.

Note also the world price collapse behind this: average value per carat of world production fell from
**US$132.30 in 2022 to US$115.04 in 2023 to US$88.35 in 2024** (Kimberley Process figures via NRCan). World
production value fell from US$16.0 billion in 2022 to **US$9.5 billion in 2024**.

## Ekati — Canada's first diamond mine, and now in receivership

At **Lac de Gras, ~310 km northeast of Yellowknife**, about 200 km south of the Arctic Circle. Discovered 1991
by Chuck Fipke and Stewart Blusson, whose Point Lake kimberlite was itself uneconomic but touched off one of
the largest staking rushes in mining history, covering most of the ground between Yellowknife and the Arctic
coast. **156 known kimberlite pipes** sit within the Ekati claim block.

- **Opened 14 October 1998**, operated by BHP. Canada's first surface *and* underground diamond mine.
- **~67.8 million carats produced 1998–2017**; ~40 million carats from six open pits 1998–2009 alone.
- Recent annual production estimated at **~7.5 million carats**.
- Underground kimberlites: Koala, Koala North, Panda. Open pits including Sable, Misery, Point Lake, Fox, Lynx.
- Workers **fly in and out through Ekati Airport** — resupply is the ice road, people are aircraft.
- Ownership chain: BHP → Dominion Diamond Mines (formerly Harry Winston) → **Dominion went bankrupt during
  COVID** → Arctic Canadian Diamond Company (2021) → **Burgundy Diamond Mines (July 2023)**.

**The ending:** in **May 2026** Arctic Canadian Diamond Company, the Burgundy subsidiary holding Ekati, obtained
**bankruptcy protection under the Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act**, citing US tariffs affecting India,
lab-grown diamond competition, falling Chinese consumer spending, global oversupply, and a fuel-cost spike. On
**14 July 2026, after a failed sale process, the Government of the Northwest Territories obtained a
receivership order** and the court appointed PwC to oversee **shutdown and reclamation**. Reclamation is funded
by **C$325 million in surety bonds, cash deposits and trust funds** held by the territorial government as
security. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekati_Diamond_Mine

⚠️ The Ekati receivership is recent and fast-moving. The Wikipedia account is internally consistent and dated,
but **it should be re-verified against CBC North or NWT government filings before being treated as settled.**

## Diavik — closed March 2026

On a **20 km² island in Lac de Gras ("East Island"), ~300 km northeast of Yellowknife**, roughly 220 km south
of the Arctic Circle. Four kimberlite pipes of the Lac de Gras field, all of them **under the shallow water of
the lake**, mined behind engineered rockfill dikes (A154, A418, A21).

- Surveyed 1992, construction from 2001, **commercial production January 2003**.
- **~7 million carats a year**, ~1,000 employees at peak. Rio Tinto (60%) / Dominion (40%), later Rio Tinto sole
  owner, operated by Yellowknife-based Diavik Diamond Mines Inc.
- Open pit to 2010, **fully underground by September 2012**.
- **Diavik Airport: a 1,595 m (5,234 ft) gravel runway taking Boeing 737s and C-130s.**
- **Largest diamond ever found in North America: a 552-carat yellow stone, October 2018.** Also the 187.7-carat
  Diavik Foxfire (2015), given the Tłı̨chǫ name *Noi?eh Kwe*, "caribou crossing stone."
- **27 February 2026:** closure agreement signed with the **Tłı̨chǫ Government**. **26 March 2026: final day of
  production.** Wind-down continues **to 2029**.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diavik_Diamond_Mine

**The Diavik wind farm is worth its own line.** Completed September 2012 — the NWT's first large-scale wind
installation, **four turbines, 9.2 MW**, supplying about **11% of the mine's power** and displacing roughly
**5 million liters of diesel a year**. Described as the **world's largest wind-diesel hybrid at a remote
off-grid mine**, rated to operate down to **−40 °C**. See Part 9; this is the single best real-world datapoint
on Arctic wind viability in the territory.

**Diavik is also the origin of the ice-road cautionary tale.** In **2006 the Yellowknife ice road froze late
and thawed early**, and Diavik could not haul in a full year's supplies before it closed; the balance had to be
**flown in**. One warm winter, one mine, one airlift. See Part 5.

## Gahcho Kué — the last one standing

At **Kennady Lake, ~280 km east-northeast of Yellowknife**, in the Akaitcho Treaty 8 claim block, 85 km
southeast of Snap Lake. A joint venture of **De Beers Canada (51%) and Mountain Province Diamonds (49%)**.
**Officially opened 20 September 2016** — the newest of the four and, with Diavik shut and Ekati in
receivership, **the only NWT diamond mine still producing.** Served by **Gahcho Kué Aerodrome** (a winter ice
runway plus a year-round gravel strip) and by a **spur of the Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road** off the old
Lupin Mine route. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gahcho_Ku%C3%A9_Diamond_Mine

⚠️ **Annual carat production, reserves and stated mine life for Gahcho Kué could not be verified from a primary
source in this pass.** Do not use a number for it. Mountain Province Diamonds files quarterly production; that
is the place to get it.

## Snap Lake — the one that already failed

**~220 km northeast of Yellowknife. De Beers' first mine outside Africa and Canada's first entirely
underground diamond mine.** Winter access road opened 2005; **commercial production 16 January 2008**. De Beers
had spent **US$1.8 billion** on it by end-2013, of which US$1.3 billion went to NWT-based contractors and
US$723 million to Indigenous businesses or joint ventures. Life-of-mine resource estimated at **16.1 million
carats**; expected life ~15 years; ~400 people on site on a given day.

**It lasted seven years.** On **4 December 2015** De Beers announced it was stopping production because of the
diamond price and a costly license exemption, putting the mine into care and maintenance — **434 laid off, ~70
retained.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snap_Lake_Diamond_Mine

⚠️ **Snap Lake's fate after 2015 is not verified here.** Reporting at the time indicated De Beers later moved
from care-and-maintenance toward permanent closure and allowing the underground workings to flood. **Treat the
mine as gone but verify the flooding claim before using it.**

## What diamonds mean after a collapse — read this part carefully

Four facts stack up, and together they are decisive:

1. **Diamond mining here is capital-extreme.** Snap Lake alone consumed US$1.8 billion and returned seven
   years. Diavik required building rockfill dikes into a lake, then dewatering the lakebed, then sinking an
   underground mine, then a 737-capable gravel airstrip, then a wind farm, in a place with no road for ten
   months of the year.
2. **Every one of these mines is fly-in/fly-out with a seasonal ice-road umbilical.** There is no permanent
   road to any of them. Fuel, explosives, steel, cement, vehicles and a year of food all arrive in a two-month
   window over frozen lakes.
3. **The product is a luxury good with no use value.** Roughly 80% of world diamond production by weight is
   industrial bort (abrasives, drilling, grinding, heat sinks) — but **that is world production, dominated by
   low-quality output.** The NWT mines were built for and sold into the **gem** market: Canada's 2024 exports
   were ~95% by value and >99% by quantity **unsorted and gem-quality rough**, shipped to **Belgium, Botswana
   and India** for cutting. Canada does essentially no cutting and polishing at scale.
4. **The industry was already dying before any imagined catastrophe** — killed by lab-grown stones, Chinese
   demand, oversupply and grade exhaustion.

**Conclusion, stated plainly: the NWT diamond industry does not survive a collapse, and it would not need a
collapse to die.** It vanishes in category (c). A post-war society has no use for gem diamonds, no ability to
finance a billion-dollar underground mine under a lake, and no market to sell into — the cutting centers are
in Antwerp, Surat and Gaborone.

**What is left behind is more interesting than what is lost.** Four industrial sites with airstrips, power
plants, fuel farms, accommodation blocks, maintenance shops, and — critically — **processing plants full of
heavy steel: crushers, mills, conveyors, dense-media separation circuits.** Plus the Diavik wind farm. In a
territory with essentially no domestic iron (see Part 2), **the diamond mines are scrap-steel deposits sitting
on the tundra at the end of a winter road**, and the Diavik and Ekati reclamation trusts (C$325 million at
Ekati alone) are a measure of how much material is there to be walked away from. The industrial-diamond
fraction of any remaining stockpile is genuinely useful for drilling and cutting tools; the gem fraction is
gravel.


# PART 2 — OTHER MINERALS

## Mary River — the most important single deposit in the Alaska Republic

**Read this against the companion file's finding that Alaska and the Yukon have no commercial iron ore
resource at all.** If that finding holds, **Mary River is the Republic's only iron.** It is also, by grade, one
of the best iron deposits on the planet.

**Location and nature.** Mary River (Inuktitut *Nuluujaat*, long used by Inuit as a travel landmark) is in the
**Qikiqtani Region of northern Baffin Island, Nunavut**, at roughly 71°19′ N — well above the Arctic Circle.
**Four deposits, numbered 1 through 4, grading 65–70% iron.** That is **direct-shipping ore (DSO)**: it can go
into a blast furnace with nothing but crushing and screening, no concentrator, no pelletizing plant, no
beneficiation whatsoever. The ore was spotted from a Cessna in **1962** by Murray Edmund Watts, who saw the
deposits as large black circles on the tundra and spent the rest of his life failing to finance them; he died
in 1982. Testing in the early 2000s confirmed the grade and put the resource at **337 million tons**.
ArcelorMittal's and ThyssenKrupp's European blast-furnace trials on a 113,217-tonne bulk sample found the
Mary River fine ore **superior to what they were then using**.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_River_Mine

**How it is actually mined and moved — this is the whole story:**

- **Open pit**, with **crushing and screening only** on site.
- A **~100 km unpaved "tote road"** from the mine to **Milne Inlet**, run with **22 ultra-large dump trucks**
  (the Phase 2 plan wanted 75).
- **Milne Inlet is a shallow, high-tide, windy inlet with a roughly 90-day open-water season, August to
  mid-October.** Ore is moved from shore to ships partly by barge.
- Ships must then transit **Eclipse Sound and the protected waters around Bylot Island** — inside the
  **Tallurutiup Imanga National Marine Conservation Area**, and the summering ground of Nunavut's largest
  narwhal population — to reach **Baffin Bay** and the Atlantic.
- **Mary River Aerodrome** handles people and freight.

**Production history.** Operational September 2014. **First shipment 8 August 2015: 53,624 tonnes on the bulk
carrier *Federal Tiber* to Nordenham, Germany.** Output 1,331,000 wet metric tonnes (2015) → 5,443,000 wmt
(2018) → 5,700,000 wmt (2019). **2018 was the benchmark season: over 5 million tonnes in an 86-day window, 71
voyages averaging 71,750 tonnes each** — the largest shipping program by volume in the Canadian or Scandinavian
high Arctic. Two of those cargoes went to Asia **via Russia's Northern Sea Route**. Baffinland leases panamax
and handysize ice-class bulkers (Nordic Bulk Carriers) and has chartered the Estonian icebreaker **MSV
*Botnica*** for July–October ice management. Permitted ceiling: **6 million tonnes per year.** 2019 revenue:
**$507 million.**

**The expansions that never happened, which is the strategically interesting part.** The original 2008 plan was
a **C$4.1 billion** project: **18 Mtpa**, a **C$1.2 billion, ~149 km cold-weather "South Railway"** to a
**C$0.7 billion all-season deep-water port at Steensby Inlet**, and **nine 190,000-tonne icebreaking freighters
sailing every second day, year round**, via Foxe Basin and Hudson Strait. Baffinland holds a 2012 NIRB
certificate for exactly that and has never built it — iron prices fell from ~US$150/t in 2010–2013 to ~US$60 in
2014 and the company retreated to the cheap option, the truck road to Milne Inlet. The later **Phase 2**
proposal (12 Mtpa, a **110 km North Railway** replacing the tote road, 176 ship transits a year, a 10-month
shipping season) was **rejected by the Nunavut Impact Review Board in May 2022**, which found it would cause
significant lasting harm to marine mammals, fish, caribou, vegetation and freshwater, and would impact **Inuit
harvesting, culture, land use and food security**. In **February 2021 a group of Inuit hunters blockaded the
airstrip and tote road for a week.**

**Current status — and it is precarious.** Baffinland is **Nunavut's largest private employer (2,600+ workers)
and accounted for roughly 23% of the territory's economic activity in 2019.** In **May 2026 Baffinland entered
creditor protection under the CCAA**; a judge approved a **$110 million interim loan in June 2026** to keep the
mine running through the summer, and in **July 2026 the company secured a $660 million federal loan.** Headlines
from the period — *"As Baffinland goes, so goes Nunavut"* — capture the exposure accurately. (Sources: CBC,
Nunatsiaq News, APTN, North of 60 Mining News, via Google News RSS, May–July 2026.)

⚠️ **Not verified in this pass:** current NI 43-101 reserves and mine life; 2024–2026 shipped tonnage; the
precise terms of the federal loan; whether the Steensby certificate remains live. Get these from Baffinland
filings and NIRB records before relying on them.

## Pine Point — a lead-zinc district with a railroad already built to it

South shore of **Great Slave Lake, between Hay River and Fort Resolution**, NWT. A **Mississippi Valley Type**
carbonate-hosted lead-zinc system: **more than 80 separate orebodies scattered along a trend roughly 65 km long
by 24 km wide**, hosted in karst collapse structures in dolomite and limestone. Known to Dene people and to fur
traders at Fort Resolution in the nineteenth century; a minor staking rush in 1898 when Klondike-bound
prospectors heard rumors of silver (there was none).

**Cominco produced here from 1964/65 to 1987**, mostly open-pit. **Lifetime: 69,416,000 tons of ore milled,
10,785,000 tons of concentrate and high-grade direct ore shipped, containing roughly 2 million tons of lead and
7 million tons of zinc.** To make it work the **Government of Canada built the Great Slave Railway from
Grimshaw, Alberta to the mine between 1962 and 1964** — later the Mackenzie Northern Railway, now CN. When the
mine closed in 1987 the purpose-built town of Pine Point (peak population **1,915 in 1976**) was **sold off,
trucked away and demolished**; buildings including the ice arena were moved to Fort Resolution and Hay River.
By 1991 the census counted **9 people**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_Point_Mine

**Redevelopment.** Tamerlane Ventures tried and went bankrupt. Darnley Bay Resources bought the project for $8
million in December 2016. Osisko Metals acquired it in December 2017; since 2023 it has been held by **Pine
Point Mining, an Osisko Metals / Appian Natural Resources joint venture.** The redevelopment case rests
explicitly on **existing highway, existing railway, and Taltson hydro power** — which is precisely why it
matters here.

**For a reconstruction scenario, Pine Point is the single best-positioned base-metal asset in the two
territories:** shallow, open-pittable, on an all-season road, on a rail line to Alberta, beside a lake with
barge access, next to an existing hydro dam, and with 80-odd separate orebodies so that a small operation can
work one at a time. Lead and zinc are the metals of ammunition, batteries, solder, sheet, and galvanizing.
⚠️ Current resource/reserve figures for the redevelopment **not verified here.**

## Tungsten — Cantung and Mactung

**Cantung**, in the Flat River valley of the Selwyn Range near the Yukon border (Nahanni country), produced
tungsten **1962–1986, 2002–2003, and 2005–2015**. A company town, **Tungsten, NWT**, was built for it with its
own airport. North American Tungsten **went bankrupt in 2015** and the mine closed that October; **the federal
government now owns the site and carries the cleanup liability.** As of February 2019 Ottawa and the GNWT were
still trying to sell it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantung_Mine

**Mactung** sits on the **Yukon–NWT boundary** in the Selwyn Mountains — one of the largest tungsten resources
in Canada at **33 million tonnes grading 0.88% WO₃**. It cleared Yukon environmental assessment in July 2014
and was approved for construction that September; capital cost was estimated at **~C$400 million**. The
**Government of the Northwest Territories bought the deposit outright in November 2015**; **Fireweed Metals
acquired it in 2023.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mactung_mine

North American Tungsten claimed in 2007 that Cantung and Mactung together held **15% of the world's known
tungsten**, against China's ~85% of reserves. ⚠️ **That is a company statement in a promotional context, not an
independent assessment — flag it as such.** The underlying point that stands: these are among the very few
substantial tungsten deposits outside China, and tungsten is the metal of cutting tools, armor-piercing cores,
and high-temperature filaments.

## Nunavut base metals — Izok Lake, High Lake, Hackett River, and the Grays Bay road

The **Izok Corridor** (MMG Resources, a subsidiary of Chinese state-owned China Minmetals) bundles **Izok Lake**
and **High Lake** copper-zinc deposits in the Kitikmeot region inland of Coronation Gulf. The 2012 proposal:
**180,000 tonnes of zinc and 50,000 tonnes of copper a year**, a **350 km all-weather road with 70 bridges**
from Izok Lake to a new port at **Grays Bay**, and a port handling ships up to **50,000 tonnes making 16 round
trips a year — shipping out through *both ends* of the Northwest Passage**, with a roughly three-month open
season. The engineering required is extreme: **Izok Lake would be drained and its water dammed and diverted,
three lakes at High Lake would also be drained, and Grays Bay would be substantially filled in.** Wolfden
Resources had earlier gotten a favorable NIRB review for a 53 km road and dock at Grays Bay serving High Lake.
The project was revived in 2024 with permitting expected to run to 2027 and construction from 2030.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grays_Bay

**The road may now actually get built, for military reasons rather than mining ones.** On **12 March 2026 Prime
Minister Mark Carney announced funding for a 230 km all-season road from Grays Bay south to the NWT border**,
framed as Arctic sovereignty infrastructure, alongside northern military support hubs at **Whitehorse,
Resolute, Rankin Inlet and Cambridge Bay** and upgrades at **Yellowknife, Inuvik, Iqaluit and Happy
Valley-Goose Bay**. If completed, this would be **the first road link between the Arctic coast and the
continental highway network** — a permanent replacement for the ice road, and a structural change to the
region's geography. ⚠️ **Announced funding is not a built road. Status beyond the announcement is unverified.**

Also in the Kitikmeot: **Hackett River** (Glencore — silver, zinc, copper, lead, near Bathurst Inlet).

## Nunavut gold

- **Meliadine** (Agnico Eagle), near **Rankin Inlet** on Hudson Bay. Commercial production **14 May 2019**,
  projected to run **to 2032**. A **700-person camp** and a **31.3 MW diesel-fired power plant** — the mine
  generates its own electricity, from fuel shipped in. A 2021 Agnico-commissioned study found the mine road had
  "virtually no impact" on migrating caribou; the **Kivalliq Inuit Association commissioned its own report
  calling that study narrow and unrealistic.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meliadine_Gold_Mine
- **Meadowbank** (Agnico Eagle), **~300 km west of Hudson Bay** near **Baker Lake**; Agnico's first Low Arctic
  mine. Opened June 2010, hit **three million ounces in 2018**, and the **original pit stopped in 2019** — but
  **the mill and camp remain in use**, processing ore trucked from the **Amaruq** satellite deposit (Whale
  Tail, V Zone, Mammoth). Area probable reserves have been stated at **3.5 Moz (29.3 Mt at 3.7 g/t)**, open
  along strike and at depth. ⚠️ That reserve figure is undated on the source page; treat as indicative.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadowbank_Gold_Mine
- **Hope Bay**, on the **Hope Bay greenstone belt** in the Kitikmeot. Explored since the early 1990s; Newmont
  parked it in 2012; TMAC Resources bought it in 2013 and produced gold from **2017**. In 2020 **Shandong Gold,
  a Chinese state company, agreed to buy it for C$230 million and the Canadian government blocked the deal in
  December 2020 on national-security grounds** under the Investment Canada Act. **Agnico Eagle bought it in
  February 2021 and then paused production in 2022 in favor of exploration.**
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_Bay_Mine
- Advanced projects listed by the territory: **Back River** (gold, Bathurst Inlet), **Committee Bay / Three
  Bluffs** (Naujaat), **Ulu and Lupin** (Contwoyto Lake), **Storm Copper** (Taloyoak), **Chidliak** diamonds
  (De Beers, between Iqaluit and Pangnirtung), **Kiggavik** uranium (Areva, Baker Lake), and **Roche Bay** iron
  ore (Hall Beach). ⚠️ **None of these was researched to resource level in this pass. Roche Bay deserves a
  second look specifically because it is a second Nunavut iron occurrence.**

**Nunavut's closed mines, which show the pattern:** **Lupin** gold (1982–2005, Contwoyto Lake), **Polaris**
lead-zinc (1982–2002, Little Cornwallis Island — one of the northernmost base-metal mines ever operated),
**Nanisivik** lead-zinc (1976–2002, near Arctic Bay), **Rankin Inlet** nickel-copper-PGM (1957–1962), and
**Jericho** diamonds (2006–2008, with a stockpile run in 2012). Every one is a fly-in or sealift operation that
opened, exhausted its economic core, and shut. **The Nunavut norm is a mine that lasts twenty years or less.**

## The Yellowknife gold camp — and the arsenic

Yellowknife exists because of gold. **Con Mine produced over 5 million ounces between 1938 and 2003**; **Giant
Mine produced over 7 million ounces between 1948 and 2004.** Both are gone; Con was demolished to rubble after
closure. Other NWT producers on the historic list: Negus, Ptarmigan and Tom, Thompson-Lundmark, Discovery,
Camlaren, Rayrock (uranium), Echo Bay (silver-copper), and **Eldorado at Port Radium on Great Bear Lake —
radium, then uranium for the Manhattan Project, plus silver and copper — 1933–1940, 1942–1960 and 1976–1982.**

**Giant Mine is a hazard that a collapse would activate, and it should be in the story.** The roasting process
that freed gold from arsenopyrite ore left **237,000 tonnes of arsenic trioxide dust**, about 60% arsenic by
weight, **sealed in fifteen underground stopes and chambers roughly 76 m below the surface**, immediately
adjacent to **Yellowknife Bay on Great Slave Lake**. Arsenic trioxide is odorless, tasteless, lethal in small
doses, and **soluble in water.** The federally approved remediation plan — costed at roughly **C$900 million to
C$1 billion** and approved in August 2014 — is to **permanently freeze the chambers using artificial ground
refrigeration**, the same basic technology as an ice rink, to keep groundwater out. A 2006 flooding event
around one chamber holding **16,946 tonnes** of the dust raised exactly this threat.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_Mine

**Restate that plainly: the containment of a quarter of a million tonnes of soluble arsenic next to the
territory's largest city depends on a refrigeration plant that has to run forever.** It is not a passive seal.
It is a machine. **This is the single best post-collapse hazard in the region, and it is entirely real.**

# PART 3 — OIL AND GAS

## Norman Wells — a century of oil that ended this month

**Norman Wells, NWT**, sits on the **Mackenzie River** in the Sahtu region; the Slavey name, *Tłegǫ́hłı̨*, means
"where there is oil." Alexander Mackenzie noted bituminous seeps near Bear Rock and Fort Good Hope in 1789. An
oil-bearing formation was identified in **1911** and **Imperial Oil drilled the first wells in 1919**,
producing from **1920 onward** — one of the longest continuously producing oil fields in Canada. Part of the
field is worked from **artificial islands built out into the Mackenzie River**.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Wells

**The wartime episode is worth knowing** because it is the clearest historical test of moving northern oil by
force of will. Norman Wells crude is **light enough to flow at −62 °C**, and in 1942–44 the US Army built the
**Canol Project**: a ~1,000 km pipeline and the **Canol Road** to carry Norman Wells oil to a refinery at
**Whitehorse**, to fuel operations in Alaska and the Yukon. Oil flowed from **1944**. It was, in the standard
assessment, **a fiasco — five times over budget, losing as much as 20% of the oil to bad construction, and shut
down after thirteen months in 1945.** The road was abandoned; abandoned equipment still litters the corridor,
now the **Canol Heritage Trail**.

The field was tied into the continental grid in **1985 by a pipeline from Norman Wells to Zama City, Alberta**,
built through Dehcho territory where land claims remain unsettled. Governance has run since 1944 under the
**Norman Wells Proven Area Agreement**, a partnership between Imperial Oil and the federal government.

**It is over.** In **January 2026 Imperial Oil announced it would wind down Norman Wells in the third quarter
of 2026**, citing declining production and low oil prices. The April 2026 timeline put shutdown in a **four-week
window between mid-August and mid-September 2026** — that is, immediately before this file's date. Scale of the
loss to the town: **about 80 jobs and roughly $6 million in taxes, about 70% of the municipal budget**, in a
town of **673 people (2021 census; ~750 commonly cited).** Imperial is also **the town's main electricity
supplier**, so the NWT Power Corporation has had to install extra generation and plan a diesel plant upgrade
to take over. After shutdown: equipment secured through summer 2027, then **three to five years of interim care
and maintenance**, then a larger closure phase. **There is no approved final closure plan.** (Cabin Radio, 30
April 2026: https://cabinradio.ca/288486/news/economy/mining/imperial-oil-sets-out-norman-wells-closure-timeline/)

⚠️ **Norman Wells production volumes (peak and recent barrels per day, cumulative production) were not
obtained.** Get them from the Canada Energy Regulator or Imperial's filings before using a number.

**The important structural point: with Norman Wells shut, the Northwest Territories has no oil production, and
neither territory refines anything.** Every liter of diesel, gasoline, jet fuel and heating oil in both
territories is refined in the south and hauled north by barge, ice road, highway or sealift. That dependency
is total and it is the backbone of Part 9.

## The Mackenzie Delta and Beaufort Sea — enormous, stranded, and never developed

Exploration moved north from Norman Wells in the 1970s into the **Mackenzie Delta and the Beaufort Sea shelf**,
and found a great deal of gas. **By 1995 more than 1,900 wells had been drilled above the 60th parallel.**

- **Probabilistic estimates for the Mackenzie Delta and Beaufort region: about 1.9 trillion cubic meters
  (~67 trillion cubic feet) of natural gas.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackenzie_Valley_Pipeline
- The **Mallik** site in the Mackenzie Delta is one of the world's best-studied **gas hydrate** test locations;
  a 2008 experiment extracted gas by depressurization rather than heating, using far less energy than the 2002
  thermal test. This is frontier science, not a resource base.
- ⚠️ A figure of **"166 billion barrels of oil reserves" for the delta and coastal region as of 2016** appears
  on the Mackenzie River page. **That number is implausibly large for the region as a reserve figure and is
  almost certainly a misstatement or a resource-in-place estimate. Do not use it.** Flagged rather than
  repeated.

**None of it is produced.** There is no pipeline, no LNG plant, and no year-round marine terminal. The gas has
sat known and untouched for fifty years.

## Why the Mackenzie Valley Pipeline failed — twice

This is the defining infrastructure non-event of the territory and it is worth understanding properly, because
the same logic governs every other big northern project.

**Round one, the 1970s.** A pipeline from the Beaufort down the Mackenzie Valley to northern Alberta was
proposed in the early 1970s. The **Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry under Justice Thomas Berger** traveled the
valley and heard testimony — notably, and for the first time at that scale, from the Dene, Métis and Inuvialuit
whose land it crossed. **Berger recommended a ten-year postponement**, estimating that was how long land claims
would take to settle. The Trudeau government fell in 1979 before acting; the Clark government did not act; the
re-elected Liberals in 1980 instead approved **the Norman Wells–Zama oil line**. The pipeline itself was
shelved. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackenzie_Valley_Pipeline

**Round two, 2001–2017.** Land claims did settle — **Inuvialuit 1984, then Gwich'in and Sahtu** — and in
**October 2001** ConocoPhillips, Shell, ExxonMobil and Imperial Oil signed a memorandum with the newly formed
**Aboriginal Pipeline Group**, which in **June 2003** took a **one-third ownership stake** in the project
alongside TransCanada. The design: **1,220 km, ~18.5 billion m³ (650 Bcf) per year**, running south from the
delta past Fort Simpson into Alberta. Final consortium: Imperial 34.4%, APG 33.3%, ConocoPhillips 15.7%, Shell
11.4%, ExxonMobil 5.2%.

**It received federal cabinet approval and an NEB Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity on 11 March
2011 — and was never built.** Costs had risen to roughly **C$16 billion** by 2016. The **North American shale
gas boom** made Arctic gas worthless in the meantime, and regulatory approval had taken so long that the market
it was designed for no longer existed. Despite permits valid to 2022, **the partners abandoned the project in
2017.**

**The moral, which generalizes to every asset in this file:** in this region the deposit is never the
constraint. The constraint is a transport link that costs billions, takes thirty years to permit, and has to be
justified against a commodity price that will have moved twice before it is finished. **The North's gas and its
diamonds and its iron all failed or are failing for the same reason, and none of those reasons is geology.**


# PART 4 — THE MACKENZIE RIVER SYSTEM

## The river

The **Mackenzie** (Slavey *Deh-Cho*, "big river") is **the largest river flowing into the Arctic Ocean from
North America.**

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Main stem length | **1,738 km (1,080 mi)** — entirely within the NWT |
| Total system length with tributaries | **4,241 km (2,635 mi)** — 13th longest river system on Earth |
| Drainage basin | **~20% of Canada**; 12th largest basin on Earth |
| Annual discharge | **>325 km³**, about **11% of all river flow into the Arctic Ocean** |
| Mean flow at the mouth | **9,910 m³/s** — second largest in Canada after the St. Lawrence, 14th in the world |
| Flow at the outlet of Great Slave Lake | **4,269 m³/s** |
| Flow at Fort Simpson, below the Liard | **6,769 m³/s** |
| Sediment load | **~128 million tonnes/year** delivered to the delta |

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackenzie_River

**Where the water comes from matters.** About **60% of the flow originates in the western, mountainous half of
the basin** — the Rockies, Selwyn and Mackenzie ranges — with the **Peace contributing 23% and the Liard 27%**.
The eastern half, all marsh and enormous lakes, supplies only about 25%, because **Great Slave Lake and Lake
Athabasca act as natural flow regulators**, holding back the spring melt and releasing it slowly. The ultimate
headwater is **Thutade Lake in interior British Columbia** — which, note, is outside the Alaska Republic
entirely, as are the **W.A.C. Bennett, Peace Canyon and John Horgan dams on the upper Peace (combined >4,700
MW, BC Hydro)** that already regulate a quarter of the river's flow. **A foreign power controls a large
fraction of the Mackenzie's hydrograph.** That is a genuine strategic fact and it is not fiction.

**The delta** begins at **Point Separation**, northwest of Tsiigehtchic: a maze of branching channels, ponds and
wetlands, **the second largest Arctic delta in the world after the Lena**, built almost entirely of
**permafrost that reaches down to bedrock** and studded with roughly **1,400 pingos** — earth-covered ice
hills. The **Peel River**, draining much of the northern Yukon, joins inside the delta.

## The freeze — the fact that governs everything

**The Mackenzie is frozen for most of the year.** Break-up runs **early-to-mid May in the south and late May to
early June in the north**; the river **freezes again in late October or November, starting at the northern
end.** Ice can persist in the middle of **Great Slave Lake into mid-June.** Tributaries break up earlier than
the main stem, which produces **ice jams and flooding** where they meet it.

**So the navigable season on the Mackenzie is roughly mid-June to mid-October — about four months, and shorter
at the northern end than the southern.** Everything that moves in bulk in the western NWT moves in that window
or does not move.

## The barge system

**Canada's northernmost major railhead is Hay River, on the south shore of Great Slave Lake.** Goods arrive
there by **rail (the CN line up from Alberta, originally the Great Slave Railway built for Pine Point) and by
highway**, and are transferred to barges.

- The operator is **Marine Transportation Services**, which is **owned by the Government of the Northwest
  Territories.** It exists in that form because the private **Northern Transportation Company Limited went
  bankrupt in 2016 and the territorial government bought its assets.** ⚠️ NTCL was previously owned by Norterra,
  jointly held by the **Inuvialuit** and the **Inuit of Nunavut**, until April 2014.
- **Barges run in "trains" of up to fifteen shallow-draft vessels pushed or pulled by tugboats**, down the full
  length of the river.
- The northern terminus is **Tuktoyaktuk**, on the eastern edge of the delta, where cargo transfers to
  **oceangoing vessels** for delivery along the **Arctic coast and out to the islands**.
- **Navigation is described as "notoriously difficult" despite the river's width and depth**, because sandbars
  and shallows shift from year to year. In narrow reaches, **barge trains must be uncoupled and the units towed
  through one at a time.** Blasting has been used to widen and deepen the channel.
- **In winter the frozen river, especially in the delta, becomes an ice road** carrying heavy trucks.

**Historical depth, which matters for a low-technology future.** **Steamboat service on the Mackenzie began in
the 1880s** and surged during the Klondike rush; **sternwheelers** carried people and freight from as far up as
the Athabasca all the way to the delta, **with portages around obstacles, above all the rapids on the Slave
River.** The Klondike-era all-water route ran Edmonton → Athabasca → Slave → Mackenzie → Peel → Rat → Porcupine
→ Yukon, roughly **3,200 km**, and killed or turned back many who tried it. **Steamboats were replaced by
diesel craft in the 1940s.**

**This is the most reconstruction-relevant fact in the whole supplement: the Mackenzie was a working commercial
artery for sixty years using wood-fired and coal-fired shallow-draft sternwheelers, before diesel, before
radar, before dredging.** The portages are known. The route is known. It is the one piece of heavy transport
infrastructure in the region that a low-technology society can actually operate, because the river does the
work.

## Great Slave and Great Bear

**Great Slave Lake** — **27,200 km²**, **469 km long and 20–203 km wide**, **614 m deep: the deepest lake in
North America**, tenth largest in the world by area, ~1,070 km³ of water. **Partially frozen an average of
eight months a year.** Its East Arm is split by the Pethei Peninsula into McLeod Bay and Christie Bay. On its
shores: **Yellowknife, Hay River, Fort Resolution, Behchokǫ̀, Dettah and N'Dilǫ**, plus the abandoned Pine
Point. An **ice road (the Dettah ice road)** crosses Yellowknife Bay each winter.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Slave_Lake

**Great Bear Lake** — **31,153 km²**, **the largest lake entirely within Canada**, fourth largest in North
America, eighth largest in the world. **Ice-covered from late November to July** — eight months. It holds a
**world-record lake trout**. Its only community is **Délı̨nę**; **Port Radium**, on its eastern shore, was the
uranium and radium mine. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Bear_Lake

⚠️ **Great Bear Lake's outflow, the Great Bear River, and whether it is navigable, were not researched.**

**Together these two lakes hold an enormous freshwater fishery and an enormous thermal mass.** The latter is
not a figure of speech: Great Slave's regulating effect on the Mackenzie's spring flood, and both lakes'
eight-month ice cover, are the same physical fact seen from two sides.


# PART 5 — TRANSPORT AND ACCESS

**The organizing fact: in the NWT and Nunavut, nothing is more expensive or more fragile than the act of
moving mass.** The deposits, the fish and the people are all real. The connections between them are thin,
seasonal, and in several cases were built for a single mine that has since closed.

## ⚑ A boundary problem as severe as the Panhandle

Per `../New North America - Territory Definitions (read from map).md`, **Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba
belong to the Prairie Federation, not the Alaska Republic.** Every existing land connection from the NWT to
the continental network crosses that border:

1. **The Mackenzie Highway** enters the NWT from **Alberta Highway 35** at the 60th parallel.
2. **The rail line to Hay River** — the Mackenzie Northern / CN, originally the Great Slave Railway — comes up
   from **Grimshaw, Alberta.** This is the **northernmost major railhead in Canada** and it is the head of the
   entire Mackenzie barge system.
3. **The Norman Wells oil pipeline** runs to **Zama City, Alberta.**
4. **The Port of Churchill and the Hudson Bay Railway**, the only deep-water port and railhead on Hudson Bay,
   are in **Manitoba.**

**So the Alaska Republic's entire eastern and southern logistics depend on a neighboring state**, exactly as
its Pacific logistics depend on Cascadia holding the Panhandle. The one exception is the western road, the
**Dempster**, which runs Yukon → NWT and is therefore internal.

## Roads that exist

**The Mackenzie Highway (NWT Highway 1)** — the NWT's longest, from the Alberta border to **Wrigley**. The
**Yellowknife Highway** branches off it. The NWT has **nine numbered highways** in total.

**Yellowknife had no all-season road to the rest of Canada until 2012.** The **Deh Cho Bridge** over the
Mackenzie opened that year. Before it, crossings were by **ferry in summer and ice road in winter** — and
during freeze-up and break-up, when the ice would not carry a truck and the ferry could not push through it,
**everything from fuel to groceries had to be flown into the territorial capital.** That is a recent memory,
not history.

**The Dempster Highway (Yukon Highway 5 / NWT Highway 8)** — **736 km (457 mi)**, from the Klondike Highway
40 km east of **Dawson City** to **Inuvik**, with **no intersections along its entire length.** Opened **18
August 1979** after two decades of stop-start construction that began in January 1959 and halted in 1962 when
the Eagle Plains oil play proved uncommercial. Built **on a gravel berm** specifically so that heat from the
road would not thaw the permafrost beneath it. It crosses the **Peel and Mackenzie rivers by seasonal ferry in
summer and ice bridge in winter** — which means the Dempster, too, has a shoulder season during which it does
not function. **One hotel on the whole route, at Eagle Plains.** It roughly follows an old dog-sled route and
is named for the Mountie who in March 1911 found the frozen bodies of the "Lost Patrol."
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dempster_Highway

**The Inuvik–Tuktoyaktuk Highway** — **138 km (86 mi)**, two-lane gravel, **eight bridges**, opened **15
November 2017** after approval in 2013 and construction from 2014. **It is the first all-weather road to
Canada's Arctic coast**, and with the Dempster it completes the **first year-round road link between the
continental network and the Arctic Ocean.** Permafrost thaw during construction repeatedly put the work area
under water; crews worked 24 hours a day through the first three winters. **The old Tuktoyaktuk winter road
closed permanently at the end of the 2017 season** — last used 29 April 2017. Its practical effect has been
**tourism more than resource access**, plus the predictable second-order problem of easier alcohol and drug
smuggling into a community with possession limits.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuvik%E2%80%93Tuktoyaktuk_Highway

## The Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road — and its collapse this year

**The longest heavy-haul ice road in the world**, and as of September 2026, **it is not going to be built.**

**What it was:**

- First built **1982**, on the line of an older road cleared to the Tundra Mine in 1960–61 and reopened in 1979
  for the Lupin Mine at Contwoyto Lake. Licensed by Echo Bay Mines until 1998; since 1999 run by the **Tibbitt
  to Contwoyto Winter Road Joint Venture (BHP, Diavik/Rio Tinto, De Beers Canada)**, engineered by Nor-Ex Ice
  Engineering, **constructed and operated by Nuna Logistics — 51% Inuit-owned** (Nunasi Corporation, Kitikmeot
  Corporation, Nuna Management Group) — with security by **Det'on Cho Scarlet Security**, a Yellowknives Dene
  company.
- **400–600 km long**, of which **495 km (85–87%) is built on frozen lake ice** and **73 km crosses 64 land
  portages.**
- Takes about **six weeks to build each January**. The first vehicle each season is a **Swedish Hägglunds
  tracked vehicle designed to float if it breaks through**, towing **ground-penetrating radar** to measure ice.
- **50 m wide on the ice**, 12–15 m on portages, built wide so trucks can pass and so blizzards cannot block
  it. **Snow is removed continuously** because snow insulates the ice and slows freezing.
- **Operational in February and March only — an average of 67 days a year.** Light vehicles at **70 cm of ice**;
  **107 cm** is required for a **Super B tanker** carrying **50,000 liters** and weighing up to **42 tonnes**.
- Loaded trucks are limited to **25 km/h** on the ice, some sections 10 km/h; empty trucks 60 km/h. Speeds are
  enforced by radar. **Trucks are not permitted to travel alone — convoys of up to four leave Yellowknife every
  20 minutes.** Three camps: Dome Lake, Lockhart Lake, Lac de Gras.
- **What it carries: diesel above all, then cement, tires, ammonium nitrate prill for explosives, and
  construction materials.** Empty trucks haul waste south on the return leg.
- Cost to build: **$20 million or more per year** for 300+ km (GNWT figures via Cabin Radio).

**The 2006 near-miss is the canonical warning:** the road **froze late and thawed early**, and Diavik could not
haul in a full year's supplies before it closed. **The balance had to be flown in.** One warm winter, one
airlift.

**And now it has stopped.** On **14 September 2026** the De Beers-led winter road management committee confirmed
it is **"not planning a winter road program in 2027."** The trigger was notice from **PwC, Ekati's receiver**,
to the Wek'èezhìi Land and Water Board that **"a dedicated winter road to the Ekati Mine will not be
constructed for the 2026–2027 season,"** since there is enough fuel already stockpiled on site to carry care,
maintenance and reclamation through 2027–28. Diavik, in closure, does not need it either. Gahcho Kué is in poor
financial health. **The road camps go into care and maintenance; planning is expected to resume "for the 2028
season."**
https://cabinradio.ca/309201/news/travel/as-things-stand-therell-be-no-tibbitt-contwoyto-winter-road-this-year/

**Note the second-order effect, which is not intuitive:** the missing road is being studied for its effect on
the **Bathurst caribou herd**, whose range it crosses. Hunters have long argued the road disturbs the herd.
2027 will be **the first year in decades without it** — an accidental controlled experiment.

## Nunavut: no roads at all

**Nunavut has roughly 850 km of road in total and is the only province or territory in Canada with no road
connection to the rest of the country.** The roads that exist are local, unnumbered, and inside communities.
**Nunavut is also Canada's only geopolitical region not connected to the Pan-American Highway.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highways_in_Nunavut

Consequences, all of them verified and all of them severe:

- **Vehicles arrive by barge during the summer shipping season**, or occasionally by cargo aircraft.
- **Aircraft are the normal means of travel between communities**, supplemented by **ATVs most of the year,
  snowmobiles in winter, and boats in summer.**
- Vehicle maintenance is brutally expensive: rough roads, harsh weather, parts that take months, very few
  mechanics, and **fuel among the most expensive in Canada even after subsidy.**

**Annual sealift is how Nunavut is supplied.** Once a year, in the open-water window, ships deliver a
community's fuel, building materials, vehicles, non-perishable food and heavy goods. Anything missed waits a
year or comes by air at air-freight prices. ⚠️ **Sealift operators, tonnages, schedules and the per-community
open-water windows were not verified in this pass — a real gap, and one worth closing, because the sealift is
the single load-bearing logistics fact of Nunavut life.**

## Hudson Bay and the Port of Churchill

**⚑ Churchill is in Manitoba — Prairie Federation territory.** It is included here because the Kivalliq
communities (Rankin Inlet, Arviat, Baker Lake, Whale Cove, Chesterfield Inlet, Coral Harbour, Naujaat) face
Hudson Bay and it is their natural port.

- Built by the federal government in the **late 1920s**; first grain export **1931**; four deep-sea berths
  handling **Panamax** vessels.
- **Northern terminus of the Hudson Bay Railway**, connecting to CN at **The Pas**. **It is the only port of
  its size in Canada with no road connection at all — everything overland moves by rail.**
- **Iced in most of the year: accessible roughly late July to early November** (2010 season: 28 July to 2
  November). **Shallow water limits it.**
- Sold by Ottawa to **OmniTRAX for $10 in 1997**. When the Canadian Wheat Board's monopsony ended, the traffic
  base disappeared — **wheat had been 90% of throughput** — and OmniTRAX **shut the port and freight rail in
  August 2016**. **Flooding cut the rail line on 23 May 2017.** The port and railway were bought in **2018 by
  Arctic Gateway Group**, a consortium of First Nations, local governments and investors, which **repaired the
  line** and **came under complete community and Indigenous ownership in 2021.** Grain shipping resumed 7
  September 2019; from **9 July 2019** Arctic resupply ships began calling for additional cargo.
- Currently the subject of a political revival: **C$175 million over five years from 2025–26** for railway and
  port expansion, and a 2026 Manitoba proposal to **extend the season by nine months using icebreakers**
  (icebreakers at C$100–410 million each, ice-class bulkers at C$60–70 million each). **Ottawa is considering
  it but has not prioritized it.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Churchill

**Churchill's history is the cleanest available demonstration of northern logistics economics: a
federally-built port, sold for ten dollars, kept alive by a grain monopoly, killed within five years of that
monopoly ending, cut off by one flood, and revived only by public money and community ownership.**

## Air

**Yellowknife Airport** is the territorial hub, in the National Airports System. **Iqaluit Airport** serves
Nunavut. Mine sites carry their own strips — **Diavik's gravel runway is 1,595 m and takes Boeing 737s and
C-130s**; Ekati, Gahcho Kué, Snap Lake, Mary River, Meadowbank and Hope Bay all have aerodromes. **Regional
carriers: Canadian North, Buffalo Airways, and others.** Aviation is not a convenience here; in Nunavut it is
the primary mode of intercommunity transport and the only year-round one.


# PART 6 — THE NORTHWEST PASSAGE

## Where it actually goes

The Passage is not one route. It has **three sections — an eastern approach, a central traverse of the Arctic
Archipelago, and a western coastal run to the Bering Strait — and five to seven documented ways through the
middle**, not all of which take a large ship.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage

**East (two options):**
1. **The practical one:** Baffin Bay, between Greenland and Baffin Island, into **Lancaster Sound** at the north
   end of Baffin Island.
2. **The impractical one:** **Hudson Strait** south of Baffin Island, north through **Foxe Basin**, west through
   **Fury and Hecla Strait**, north via the Gulf of Boothia and Prince Regent Inlet. **Fury and Hecla is
   usually closed by ice** — the reason centuries of attempts to find a saltwater exit west from Hudson Bay
   all failed.

**Center (two broad options):**
- **North:** Lancaster Sound west along the **Parry Channel** to the **Prince of Wales Strait** on the
  northwest side of Victoria Island. **M'Clure Strait beyond is ice-filled**; Prince of Wales Strait, between
  Victoria and Banks Islands, may be passable. **This is the deep-water route, when it is open at all.**
- **South:** Lancaster Sound, past Prince Regent Inlet (a cul-de-sac, unless one takes **Bellot Strait**), down
  **Peel Sound** between Somerset and Prince of Wales Islands, then either southwest through the **ice-choked
  Victoria Strait** or south through **Rae Strait and James Ross Strait** and west through the **shallow
  Simpson Strait** south of King William Island into **Queen Maud Gulf**, then west along the mainland coast.
  **This is Amundsen's route and it is the shallow one.**

**West:** with no major islands in the way, follow the mainland coast to the **Bering Strait**. The approach
from the west is awkward because of **ice off Point Barrow**; east of Point Barrow the coast is usually clear
in summer.

## The historical lesson, which is the useful one

**Franklin (1845) and McClure (1850) ran the well-funded-expedition model — hundreds of men, multiple ships,
the best technology of the day. Franklin lost two ships and every man.** McClure got through by ship and
sledge and had to be rescued. **John Rae, working overland with fewer than ten people, Inuit techniques and
snowshoes, mapped more and lost one man in years of travel.** **Amundsen made the first complete passage by
ship, 1903–1906, in the converted 45-ton *Gjøa* with a crew of six**, specifically choosing a tiny crew and a
**shallow draft** so he could hug the coast and live off the country.

**That contrast — the big expedition that dies versus the small shallow-draft party that lives off the land —
is the single most transferable fact in this section for a post-collapse story.** The Passage has always been
navigable by small, shallow, locally-provisioned craft. It has never reliably been navigable by big ones.

## Modern navigability

- **21 August 2007: the Passage was open to ships without icebreaker assistance for the first time since
  record-keeping began in 1972** (Norwegian Polar Institute). It opened again on 25 August 2008.
- **First commercial cargo ship through: SS *Manhattan*, August 1969, 115,000 dwt — still the largest
  commercial vessel ever to navigate it.** Largest passenger ship: the cruise liner ***Crystal Serenity***,
  69,000 GT, Vancouver to New York with 1,500 aboard in 28 days from 10 August 2016.
- **September 2013: the *Nordic Orion* (73,500 dwt) made a successful transit** — fully laden she was too deep
  for the Panama Canal — and in 2016 **COSCO expressed interest in regular cargo voyages.**
- **But the depth problem is real.** A Canadian mining executive has stated that **parts of the eastern end are
  barely 15 m deep**, which would cap the route's usefulness for modern deep-draft shipping. And Amundsen's
  southern route is shallow by construction.
- **Charting is the other hard limit: as of 2014, only 12% of Canada's Arctic waters had been charted to modern
  standards.**
- **Thick multiyear ice drifting into and clogging straits, especially in spring, remains the practical
  hazard** — it can block a channel entirely or hole a hull. Industry skepticism is not ideological: container
  shipping runs just-in-time and cannot absorb uncertain delays, there are no intermediate stops to optimize
  around, and **insurance prices the risk accordingly.** A 2025 study concluded that navigability is increasing
  but that reliable planning still requires explicitly modeling highly variable ice.
- **Experts project the Passage may be traversable about four months a year by the end of the 21st century.**
- **Revealing detail:** in 2018, two freighters leaving Baffinland's Milne Inlet port bound for Asia **did not
  sail west through the rest of the Northwest Passage. They sailed east, around Greenland, and took Russia's
  Northern Sea Route.** Even the operator on the Passage's doorstep judged the Russian route the better bet.

## Sovereignty

**Canada holds that the Northwestern Passages are internal waters; the United States holds they are an
international strait with a right of transit passage.** The dispute is unresolved. In a post-collapse
continental setting this is worth noting precisely because **the legal question is downstream of a physical
one: whoever can actually keep icebreakers and charts and search-and-rescue in the Archipelago controls it,
and nobody else's claim will matter.**


# PART 7 — FOOD, FISHERIES AND SUBSISTENCE

## The commercial fisheries

**Arctic char (*Salvelinus alpinus*)** is the region's signature fish: anadromous, running out of and back into
Arctic river systems, and the one species that is genuinely everywhere across both territories. **Numerous
commercial char fisheries operate on river systems across the Canadian Arctic, the majority in Nunavut —
notably Cumberland Sound and Cambridge Bay** — alongside exploratory fisheries in prospective areas.
**Commercial char fishing on the Sylvia Grinnell River at Iqaluit has been banned since 1965**, which is a
useful reminder that local stocks can be fished out quickly. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_char

**Greenland halibut (turbot) and northern shrimp** are the offshore money fisheries, worked in **Davis Strait
and Baffin Bay** by a small fleet, much of it through Inuit-owned companies (Baffin Fisheries and others).
⚠️ **Quotas, landings, vessel counts and the Nunavut allocation share could not be verified in this pass.** The
corporate sites returned 403s and the DFO integrated fisheries management plan index did not yield the Arctic
groundfish document. **This is the largest single evidentiary gap in the file.** Sources to try: DFO's Arctic
IFMPs, NAFO Subarea 0 quota decisions, and the Nunavut Fisheries Association.

## Caribou — and this is the emergency

**Barren-ground caribou are the land-mammal foundation of the whole region's food system.** Roughly **1.2
million** of them live in **eight large migratory herds** running from Alaska to Hudson Bay, with about
**120,000 more in smaller non-migratory herds, half of those confined to Baffin Island.** From west to east:
**Porcupine, Cape Bathurst, Bluenose West, Bluenose East, Bathurst, Ahiak, Beverly, Qamanirjuaq.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barren-ground_caribou

**The Bathurst herd is the one to use, and its numbers are almost unbelievable:**

| Year | Estimate |
|---|---|
| **1986** | **~470,000** |
| 2022 | 6,000–7,000 |
| **2025** | **3,609** (GNWT) |

**That is a decline of better than 99%, and it fell another 47% in the three years after 2022.** The GNWT lists
industrial development, hunting, and climate/habitat change as candidate pressures and states openly that the
drivers of the continued decline are **not fully understood.** (Cabin Radio, 17 September 2026:
https://cabinradio.ca/309666/news/environment/wildlife/what-the-missing-winter-road-could-mean-for-bathurst-caribou/)

The **Beverly** and **Qamanirjuaq** herds are co-managed by the **Beverly and Qamanirjuaq Caribou Management
Board**; a 2011 aerial survey put Beverly at roughly **124,000** and the neighboring **Ahiak** at **83,300**,
though there has been genuine scientific dispute about whether Beverly declined or simply **shifted its calving
grounds** from the Gary Lakes area toward the western Queen Maud Gulf coast. ⚠️ **Current Beverly, Qamanirjuaq,
Bluenose and Porcupine numbers were not obtained. Do not assume the Bathurst collapse is universal — the
Porcupine herd in particular has historically done well.** A proposed mechanism for the broader decline is
**trophic mismatch**: warming shifts the timing of green-up relative to calving, so cows calve out of step with
peak forage and calves are malnourished.

**Why this matters more than any commercial fishery number: caribou is the calorie and fat source.** The
companion Alaska file's central subsistence finding — that harvest supplies ~176% of protein requirement and
only ~25% of calories — applies here with the same force. **A collapsing caribou herd is not a protein problem.
It is a fat and calorie problem, and it is the binding one.**

## Marine mammals

**Ringed and bearded seal, beluga, narwhal, walrus, and bowhead** are harvested across Nunavut and the
Inuvialuit Settlement Region. Eclipse Sound, which Baffinland's ore ships transit, is **the summering ground of
Nunavut's largest narwhal population**, and the herd is described as only beginning to return after a century's
absence — which is why the Mary River expansion fight was fought on marine-mammal grounds and won there.
**Seal and whale fat is the calorie-dense component of the traditional diet**, and it is the part that cannot
be replaced by fish.

⚠️ **Harvest levels, quotas and per-community take for marine mammals were not researched.** Gap.

## The cost of imported food, and food insecurity

**Nunavut has the highest rate of food insecurity of any jurisdiction in Canada. A 2017–18 survey put it at
57% of households — and nearly half of those were *severely* food insecure.** By comparison the lowest province
was Québec at 11.1%. Other reporting puts **56% of the Inuit population** as food insecure.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_security

**The federal response is a freight subsidy, not a food program. Nutrition North Canada** pays a per-kilogram
subsidy to registered retailers, suppliers, country-food processors and local growers on a list of eligible
nutritious foods (plus some non-food essentials like diapers and non-prescription drugs), at **high, medium and
low rates** depending on the item, and with **a separate, lower rate schedule for goods moved by seasonal
ground transportation rather than air.** It covers **124 isolated northern communities**, of which **112** also
qualify for the **Harvesters Support Grant** — money to support hunting and fishing rather than retail.
https://www.nutritionnorthcanada.gc.ca/eng/1415385762263/1415385790537

**Read the structure of that program carefully, because it tells you exactly what is going on.** The subsidy is
**per kilogram and higher for air freight**, and it has to distinguish between air and "seasonal ground
transportation," because **for most of these communities there is no ground transportation at all.** The
federal government is, in effect, **paying the airfare on lettuce.** Strip out that subsidy and the sealift and
the scheduled air service, and the retail food system of Nunavut does not become expensive. **It ceases to
exist.**

⚠️ **Specific price comparisons (the Revised Northern Food Basket cost by community, the classic
$20-for-a-cabbage figures) were not retrieved in this pass.** They are published by Nutrition North and by the
Nunavut Bureau of Statistics; **gov.nu.ca returned 403 from this environment.**

## Agriculture

⚠️ **Not researched, and almost certainly negligible.** The NWT has small-scale growing around Hay River and
the Mackenzie Valley and a community-garden and greenhouse movement; **Inuvik has a well-known community
greenhouse in a converted hockey arena.** Nunavut is **entirely above the tree line** and has no agricultural
soil to speak of. ⚠️ **Treat any claim about northern agriculture in this region as unverified until checked.**
**The honest structural statement: this is not farmland, at any latitude in either territory, and the growing
season at Hay River (60° N) is the only one worth discussing at all.**

## Indigenous majority and governance

**Nunavut is 84.3% Inuit** — 30,865 of 36,858 people in 2021, plus small First Nations and Métis populations,
against **5,210 non-Indigenous residents (14.2%).** The NWT is more mixed but still has a large Indigenous
majority outside Yellowknife; the territory is **most of Denendeh, the traditional land of the Dene**, plus
**most of the Inuvialuit Settlement Region**, with Cree and Métis lands as well.

**Nunavut was not carved out administratively — it was created by a land claim.** Negotiations began in 1976.
**The land claims agreement was completed in September 1992 and ratified by nearly 85% of voters in Nunavut**;
the **Nunavut Act** and **Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act** followed, and **the territory came into being on
1 April 1999** — the first change to Canada's political map since Newfoundland joined in 1949. Boundaries were
drawn in 1993. **Nunavut has no reserves; it has an Inuit-majority public government and an Inuit land-claim
organization (Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated) holding Inuit-owned lands and subsurface rights in parallel.**
Regional Inuit associations — **Qikiqtani (QIA), Kivalliq (KIA), Kitikmeot** — are the bodies that actually
negotiate with mines; Mary River operates under an **Inuit Impact and Benefit Agreement** with QIA on
Inuit-owned land.

**In the NWT the equivalent structures are the settled comprehensive claims:** **Inuvialuit (1984)**, then
**Gwich'in** and **Sahtu**, plus the **Tłı̨chǫ Agreement** (the Tłı̨chǫ Government signed Diavik's closure
agreement in February 2026) and the **Akaitcho Treaty 8** process, in whose claim block Gahcho Kué sits. **The
Dehcho claim remains unsettled — and the Norman Wells–Zama pipeline was pushed through Dehcho territory anyway
in 1980, which is one reason the Berger Inquiry's warning is still live politics.**

**Devolution is still in progress.** The **Nunavut Lands and Resources Devolution Agreement was signed 18
January 2024**; control of public lands, natural resources and water rights transfers from Ottawa to the
Government of Nunavut on **1 April 2027**, with the Canada Energy Regulator staying on as oil and gas regulator
for at least five years after that. (Canada Energy Regulator, Nunavut profile.)

**Two things follow, and both should shape the fiction:**

1. **This is not a settler-majority territory with an Indigenous minority. It is the reverse, and the
   governing institutions reflect that in law, not just in demographics.** Any post-collapse polity here is an
   Indigenous polity by default.
2. **The institutional layer — land claim boards, impact review boards, co-management boards, Inuit
   development corporations — is where the actual decisions are made**, and much of it is only twenty-five to
   forty years old. It is also the part most likely to *survive* a collapse, because it is built on kinship,
   land tenure and regional association rather than on capital.

## NWT: two small hydro grids and everything else on diesel

**The Northwest Territories Power Corporation (NTPC)** generates and distributes to **26 of the 33
communities**, across **565 km of transmission and 375 km of distribution line**. **Naka Power** (formerly
Northland Utilities) serves Yellowknife, N'Dilo, Hay River and several small communities. Both are regulated by
the NWT Public Utilities Board.

**The hydro:**
- **Snare system — 30 MW**, serving the **North Slave (Snare) grid** around Yellowknife
- **Taltson — 18 MW**, serving the **South Slave (Taltson) grid**
- **Bluefish — 6.6 MW**
- **Eight communities around Great Slave Lake run on hydro. The other 25 communities in the territory do not:
  23 run on diesel, Norman Wells runs on natural gas, and Inuvik runs on both diesel and natural gas.**

**The Taltson Hydroelectric Expansion Project** would **add 60 MW** and — in its first phase — **connect the
Snare and Taltson grids to each other**, which they currently are not. A later phase contemplates tying the
whole thing into **Alberta or Saskatchewan** (⚑ Prairie Federation, in-world). **This is the single most
consequential energy project in the territory and it has not been built.**

**Gas and LNG:** the **Inuvik gas-fired plant** ran on the local **Ikhil field from 1999 to 2012**; since
January 2014 it has run on **LNG trucked up from Alberta and British Columbia**, with a third LNG tank approved
in December 2021. NTPC has looked at extending trucked LNG to other road-accessible communities. **Note what
that means: Inuvik's "natural gas" power is a highway logistics operation.**

**Wind:** about **4% of NWT capacity.** The two real installations are **Diavik's four turbines (9.2 MW, 2012)**
— see Part 1, the world's largest wind-diesel hybrid at a remote off-grid mine, saving ~5 million liters of
diesel a year, rated to −40 °C — and the **3.5 MW Inuvik High Point Wind Project**, operational in fall 2023
with a transformer and battery storage.

**Solar:** **under 1% of NWT electricity in 2021**, but there are more installations than one would expect: a
100 kW array at Fort Simpson (2012, then the largest in northern Canada), **Colville Lake — north of the Arctic
Circle — running on a solar/battery/diesel hybrid since 2016** where it previously ran on pure diesel, arrays
at Fort Liard, Wrigley and Aklavik, a 165 kW array in Inuvik and a **1 MW solar farm** on a former Inuvik
industrial site. **Rio Tinto also announced northern Canada's largest solar farm at Diavik, sized to supply up
to 25% of the mine's power for operations and for closure work through 2029.**

## Nunavut: 25 diesel plants, and that is the entire system

**Qulliq Energy Corporation (QEC)**, owned by the territorial government, **operates 25 diesel plants in 25
communities. Those communities are connected to each other by neither roads nor power lines, and there is no
backup grid.** If a plant fails, that community has no alternative source.

**"Almost all of Nunavut's electricity is generated from diesel fuel imported during the summer and then stored
for year-round use."** That sentence, from the Canada Energy Regulator, is the whole energy system of a
territory the size of Mexico.

Renewable progress is real but small: a planned **500 kW hybrid solar/diesel plant at Kugluktuk** (announced
August 2019, repeatedly delayed), **$4.1 million shared among Arctic Bay, Clyde River, Pond Inlet, Whale Cove
and Grise Fiord in 2023**, and solar modules at the Iqaluit power plant since March 2016. New diesel plants
capable of integrating renewables were installed at **Grise Fiord and Cape Dorset (2019)** and **Arctic Bay
(2021)**, with Cambridge Bay, Igloolik and Gjoa Haven queued for 2023–26.

**The proposed Kivalliq Hydro-Fibre Link** — a **150 MW transmission line plus broadband** from northern
Manitoba into Kivalliq communities — is under federal feasibility study. ⚑ **In-world, that line would cross
into the Prairie Federation**, which changes its meaning entirely.

## Nunavut hydrocarbons: enormous on paper, zero in practice

- **No commercial crude oil production.** The only oil ever produced in Nunavut was **3.0 million barrels from
  the Bent Horn field on Cameron Island, 1985–1996** — light crude used to run generators at **Resolute Bay and
  the Polaris zinc mine.** A self-contained Arctic energy loop that lasted eleven years.
- **No natural gas production. No refineries. No pipelines. No crude-by-rail.**
- **Estimated resources: 18.3 billion barrels of crude oil and 181.4 trillion cubic feet of natural gas.**
  (Canada Energy Regulator.) **Resources, not reserves — nothing is developed, and none of it is accessible.**
- **The Canadian Arctic offshore has been closed to new oil and gas licensing since December 2016**, with a
  prohibition order on all activity most recently extended to **31 December 2028.**

## Why renewables are genuinely hard here — the honest version

Not ideology; physics and logistics.

1. **Solar output collapses exactly when demand peaks.** Above the Arctic Circle there is no sun for weeks in
   midwinter, which is when heating and lighting load is maximal. Solar in this region displaces summer diesel,
   not winter diesel. That is worth doing — Colville Lake does it — but it does not replace the plant.
2. **Wind works, but only with engineering.** Diavik proved a 9.2 MW array can run at −40 °C at 98%
   availability, and it still only covered **11%** of one mine's load. Cold-rated turbines, icing management
   and a diesel or battery backbone are all mandatory.
3. **Storage is a cold-weather problem**, and batteries lose capacity in the cold.
4. **Hydro is geographically stranded.** The Snare and Taltson systems exist and work — but they serve eight
   communities around one lake, they are **not connected to each other**, and connecting them plus adding 60 MW
   has been a proposal for years.
5. **Every component arrives by barge, ice road or sealift.** A turbine blade, a transformer or a solar array
   has to fit on a barge or a Hercules and arrive in the window. **The same constraint that makes diesel
   expensive makes the alternative to diesel expensive to install.**
6. **And diesel has one property nothing else here has: it stores indefinitely in a tank, arrives once a year,
   and works at any temperature.** That is precisely why the system is built on it.


# PART 10 — POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

⚠️ **Everything in this Part that is not a cited figure is reasoning, and is labeled as such.** The facts above
are sourced. The conclusions drawn from them are mine, and an author should feel free to overrule them — but
should know which is which.

## (a) What survives regardless

**The geology.** Mary River's four iron deposits, Pine Point's eighty-odd lead-zinc bodies, Izok and High Lake,
Mactung's tungsten, the Hope Bay greenstone belt, the kimberlite pipes. None of it moves. **Ore in an open pit
is ore in an open pit whether or not anyone owns it.**

**The river.** The Mackenzie runs 1,738 km at 9,910 m³/s regardless of who is watching, freezes in late October
and breaks up in May, and delivers 128 million tonnes of silt a year to the delta. **It worked as a commercial
artery for sixty years on wood- and coal-fired sternwheelers with portages around the Slave River rapids.** It
will do so again. This is the most important surviving asset in the two territories and it is the one nobody
has to rebuild.

**Great Slave and Great Bear.** 27,200 km² and 31,153 km², both with substantial freshwater fisheries — lake
trout, whitefish, cisco, inconnu, northern pike. Great Bear holds the world-record lake trout. ⚠️ **The
commercial Great Slave Lake fishery was not researched and is a real gap**, but the resource is unambiguous.

**Arctic char.** Anadromous, distributed across essentially every river system in both territories, harvestable
at the weir and the river mouth with nets, traps and spears. **Char is the single most collapse-proof food
resource in Nunavut**, because it comes to you, on a schedule, in a place your grandparents can name.

**Marine mammals.** Ringed and bearded seal, beluga, narwhal, walrus, bowhead. These require boats, harpoons
and knowledge, not industry. Seal in particular is the **fat** source, which — per the companion file's
subsistence arithmetic — is the actual binding nutritional constraint, not protein.

**Muskox, ptarmigan, geese, eggs, berries, and the sea itself.**

**The knowledge.** This is not sentimentality. **84.3% of Nunavut's population is Inuit, the territory has a
land-claim-based Inuit government, and the shift from land camps to permanent settlements happened within
living memory** — the 1950s and 1960s, not the nineteenth century. **The techniques for living here off the
land are two generations old, not two hundred.** Compare this to almost any southern region in a collapse
scenario and the difference is decisive.

## (b) What depends on infrastructure but could be repaired

**The Mackenzie barge system — the top priority, and the most achievable.** Shallow-draft hulls pushed by tugs,
from Hay River to Tuktoyaktuk, in a June-to-October window. **The route is charted, the portages are known, the
hazard is shifting sandbars rather than anything structural, and the historical precedent is steam.** Rebuilding
some form of river transport is the single highest-leverage thing a recovering polity in this region could do.
[Reasoning.]

**The Taltson and Snare hydro systems — 30 MW + 18 MW + 6.6 MW Bluefish.** Small dams, mechanically simple,
serving the eight communities around Great Slave Lake, with **565 km of transmission line** already strung.
Hydro is the one generating technology here that does not need a fuel supply chain. **If anything electrical
survives in the NWT, it is this, and it is around Yellowknife, Hay River and Fort Smith.** [Reasoning, built on
CER figures.]

**Pine Point.** Shallow, open-pittable, on a highway, on a rail grade, beside a lake, next to the Taltson dam,
and split into eighty separate orebodies so a small operation can work one at a time. Lead and zinc are the
metals of ammunition, solder, sheet roofing, batteries and galvanizing. **Of every mineral asset in the two
territories, this is the one a low-technology society could most plausibly work.** [Reasoning.]

**The Dempster and the Inuvik–Tuktoyaktuk Highway.** Gravel roads on gravel berms — which is exactly the kind
of road that degrades gracefully. They will wash out at the river crossings (the Peel and the Mackenzie are
ferry/ice-bridge crossings, not bridged) and the culverts will fail, but **the grade will still be there**, and
a gravel grade is a usable route for carts, horses and snow machines long after it stops carrying trucks.
[Reasoning.] The **Deh Cho Bridge** at Fort Providence is a different matter — a modern cable-stayed structure
that will need maintenance.

**The Great Slave Railway grade** from Alberta to Hay River. ⚑ It crosses into Prairie Federation territory, but
a rail grade is a rail grade.

**Scrap steel, which is the territory's real iron supply in the medium term.** In rough order of accessibility:
the **ice-road truck fleet and the Mackenzie barge fleet**; **four diamond mine processing plants** full of
crushers, mills, conveyors and separation circuits, standing at the end of a winter road; **Pine Point's
remains and the Great Slave rail line**; the Yellowknife headframes; **Cantung**; the **Polaris and Nanisivik**
sites; the **Canol Trail**, still littered with abandoned WWII pipeline, vehicles and equipment along a
1,000 km corridor; and **the DEW Line / North Warning System sites strung across the Arctic coast**. ⚠️ **The
DEW Line sites were not researched — number, condition, remediation status, materials — and they are a real
gap, because they are precisely the kind of thing a scavenging economy would target.**

## (c) What simply vanishes

**Diamonds. Entirely, and first.** Covered in Part 1: a luxury good with no use value, sold into cutting
centers in Antwerp, Surat and Gaborone, produced by billion-dollar underground mines built into lakebeds,
resupplied by a two-month ice road, **and already dying in 2026 before any imagined catastrophe.** Diavik
ceased production 26 March 2026; Ekati went into receivership 14 July 2026; De Beers expects Gahcho Kué to
close in 2028; **and the Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road is not being built for the 2027 season at all.**
A collapse would not destroy the NWT diamond industry. It would arrive to find it already gone.

**The sealift, and with it the retail economy of Nunavut.** Twenty-five communities, no roads, no grid, one
annual ship. **Everything in a Nunavut store came on that ship or on an aircraft with a federal freight subsidy
attached.** Remove the ships and the subsidy and there is no store. Not an expensive store — no store.

**Nutrition North.** A per-kilogram federal freight subsidy, weighted higher for air, across 124 isolated
communities. It is a pure artifact of a functioning national state with money.

**Diesel electricity.** **Nunavut: 25 diesel plants in 25 communities, no grid, no interties, no backup, and
all the fuel imported in summer and stored.** NWT: 23 of 33 communities on diesel, Inuvik on **LNG trucked from
Alberta**, Norman Wells on gas that just stopped being produced. **There is no refinery in either territory.**
When the last tank farm runs dry, the lights go out in every community that is not on the Snare or Taltson
grid — which is most of them, and all of Nunavut.

**Mary River as an export operation.** Ice-class panamax bulkers, chartered icebreakers, a 90-day open-water
window, and a customer base of European and Asian blast furnaces. **Also, note, Baffinland entered creditor
protection in May 2026 and is standing on a $660 million federal loan.** The *mine as a going concern* is
already contingent on the Canadian state.

**Meliadine, Meadowbank, Hope Bay.** Fly-in gold mines running on 31.3 MW diesel plants, producing a metal with
no use value in a subsistence economy except as currency.

**Air service.** In Nunavut this is not a convenience — it is the only year-round intercommunity transport, and
the only medical evacuation. **Its loss is not an inconvenience; it is a change in who survives an appendicitis
in Grise Fiord in February.**

**The territorial governments as they currently function**, which are overwhelmingly transfer-funded. Nunavut's
GDP per capita is the highest in Canada and that figure means almost nothing about local productive capacity.

**And — the hazard, not the asset — the Giant Mine arsenic containment.** **237,000 tonnes of soluble arsenic
trioxide, sealed in fifteen chambers 76 m below the surface next to Yellowknife Bay, kept in place by an
artificial ground-freezing plant that must run indefinitely.** It is a machine, not a seal. When it stops,
groundwater enters. **That is the most usable single post-collapse fact in this file, and it is entirely real.**

## (d) Carrying capacity — the blunt answer

**Current population of the two territories: roughly 83,000 people, almost none of whom are fed from the land.**

Here is the arithmetic. ⚠️ **This is my own calculation from sourced inputs, not a sourced conclusion. The
assumptions are stated so they can be argued with.**

*Inputs:* barren-ground caribou across the whole range from Alaska to Hudson Bay number about **1.2 million in
eight migratory herds plus ~120,000 in smaller herds** (Wikipedia, sourced). A conservative share lying inside
the NWT and Nunavut — excluding the Porcupine herd, which is Yukon and Alaska — might be **700,000–900,000**.
Adult females average about **90 kg** and males about **150 kg**; call it **~100 kg live weight** and **~50%
usable**, so **~50 kg of usable meat per animal.** A sustainable harvest rate for caribou is conventionally in
the range of **3–8% of the herd per year**; take **5%**.

*Result:* 800,000 × 5% = **40,000 animals a year**, or about **2 million kg of caribou meat.** At a
whole-diet meat-and-fat requirement of roughly **250–300 kg per person per year**, that is **on the order of
7,000 people fed on caribou alone.**

That figure is not the answer, because caribou is not the only food. Add **char, lake trout and whitefish from
two of the largest lakes on Earth and ten thousand smaller ones; seal, beluga, narwhal and walrus along
thousands of kilometers of coast; muskox; birds and eggs; and berries**, and the number rises substantially —
plausibly by a factor of two to four.

**So: the honest range is somewhere in the low tens of thousands — perhaps 15,000 to 35,000 people — living
fully off the land across both territories.** That is roughly **the order of magnitude of the pre-contact
Indigenous population of the same area**, which is exactly what one would expect, and it is **well under half
the present population.** ⚠️ **Pre-contact population estimates were not verified in this pass; the
correspondence is offered as a sanity check, not as evidence.**

**Two refinements matter more than the headline number:**

**1. The settlements are the wrong shape, and that is a bigger problem than the total.** **20,340 people in
Yellowknife cannot be fed from the country around Yellowknife. 7,429 in Iqaluit cannot be fed from Frobisher
Bay.** A subsistence economy does not support towns; it supports **small groups dispersed across a very large
area, moving with the resource.** That is precisely the settlement pattern that existed here **before federal
centralization in the 1950s and 1960s** — within living memory. **The collapse scenario is not "people learn to
hunt." It is "people move back out onto the land, and the towns shrink to whatever the local country can
carry."** [Reasoning.]

**2. The caribou may not be there.** **The Bathurst herd fell from ~470,000 in 1986 to 3,609 in 2025 — a 99%+
decline, and the causes are not understood.** If the collapse of a civilization arrives on top of a caribou
collapse already in progress, the land-based ceiling is **far lower than the calculation above**, and the
burden falls onto fish and marine mammals, which are coastal. **That would push the surviving population toward
the coasts and the big lakes and away from the interior barrens entirely.** [Reasoning, on sourced numbers.]

**The one thing that is not a constraint: protein.** As in Alaska, the northern problem is **calories and fat**,
not meat. Seal oil, fish oil and caribou back fat are the strategic resources. Carbohydrate essentially does
not exist here without trade.

## (e) Mary River iron — the central question, answered carefully

**The companion file establishes that Alaska and the Yukon have no verified commercial iron ore resource. If
that holds, Mary River is the Alaska Republic's only iron ore — and it is a world-class one.** The question is
whether a low-technology society could actually use it. The answer is **yes, but only at tiny volume, and only
by moving ore to fuel rather than fuel to ore.** Here is the reasoning, step by step.

**What makes it uniquely promising:**

1. **65–70% Fe, direct-shipping.** This is the whole argument. DSO needs **no concentrator, no flotation
   circuit, no pelletizing plant, no magnetic separation** — nothing but crushing and screening, which is all
   that was ever built on site. **Most of the world's iron ore is 30–40% Fe and requires an industrial
   beneficiation plant that a post-collapse society cannot build. Mary River's does not.** A hammer and a
   screen turn it into furnace feed.
2. **It is in an open pit.** The overburden is off. The ore is at surface, in four bodies, visible from the air
   as black circles on the tundra — which is literally how it was found in 1962.
3. **There is stockpiled, already-crushed ore on site**, plus a 100 km graded road to a port.
4. **The volumes a pre-industrial society needs are trivially small.** This is the point everyone gets wrong.
   **100 tonnes of 65% ore contains 65 tonnes of iron**; even at the poor yields of a bloomery, that is tens of
   tonnes of workable iron. A village blacksmith consumes on the order of a tonne a year. **One boatload,
   perhaps a few hundred tonnes, would supply a territory's smiths for a generation.** [Reasoning — the
   arithmetic is mine.]

**What makes it nearly impossible:**

1. **There is no fuel anywhere near it.** Mary River is at **71°19′ N on northern Baffin Island — far above
   the tree line.** **There is no wood, therefore no charcoal.** ⚠️ **Exact distance to the nearest boreal
   forest was not computed**; the nearest treed country in any direction is well over a thousand kilometers
   away, and the nearest forest *inside the Alaska Republic* — the Mackenzie Valley — is a Northwest Passage
   transit away. Smelting iron requires carbon, in
   quantity: roughly one to two tonnes of charcoal per tonne of iron in a bloomery. **You cannot smelt at the
   mine.** ⚠️ **Whether Baffin Island or the eastern Arctic Islands hold any workable coal was NOT researched
   and is a genuinely open question that would change this conclusion if answered yes.** The Arctic Islands do
   contain coal-bearing sediments; **verify before assuming either way.**
2. **So the ore must travel to the fuel — and the fuel is in the Mackenzie Valley boreal forest.** That is
   essentially a **full transit of the Northwest Passage under sail**, in a two-to-three-month window, through
   waters **only 12% charted to modern standards**, with multiyear ice able to close a strait without warning.
   **Amundsen did exactly this in a 45-ton sloop with a crew of six — so it is not impossible.** It is an
   expedition, not a trade route. [Reasoning.]
3. **The alternative direction is out of the country.** From Milne Inlet the natural run is **east and south
   through Baffin Bay and Hudson Strait** — toward Labrador, Greenland and Hudson Bay, and **the only port and
   railhead on Hudson Bay is Churchill, which is ⚑ Prairie Federation.** The easy route leads abroad.
4. **Milne Inlet is shallow, wind-exposed, tidal, and open about 90 days a year**, and ore has to be barged out
   to a ship standing off. That was hard with tugs and cranes.

**The synthesis, which is the most useful single paragraph in this file:**

> **Mary River is the Alaska Republic's only iron, it grades within a couple of points of pure hematite, it
> needs no processing — and the nearest forest inside the Republic is most of the Northwest Passage away.**
> (Pure hematite is 69.9% Fe; Mary River runs 65–70%. There is no higher-grade natural iron ore.)
> A recovering society does not restart a six-million-
> tonne-a-year export mine. It sends a ship north in July, fills it by hand and hammer from an open pit at the
> end of a standing gravel road, and sails it back before freeze-up — **once, and then not again for years,
> because one hold of that ore is more iron than the whole territory can forge in a decade.** The iron
> question in this nation is not "is there ore." It is **"who owns the ships, and who controls the Passage."**
> [Reasoning. The facts beneath it are sourced above.]

**Meanwhile, the near-term iron supply is scrap**, exactly as in Alaska — but the NWT and Nunavut have far less
of it than Alaska does, because there is no railroad, no Trans-Alaska Pipeline, no Anchorage, and far fewer
people. The scrap inventory is the barge fleet, the truck fleet, four diamond-mine mills, the closed mines, and
the DEW Line.

## (f) The structural position, stated plainly

**These two territories are not the Republic's breadbasket, its industrial base, or its population center.
They are its area.** Roughly 2.96 million km² holding about 83,000 people, whose entire material existence
rests on four things a collapse removes: **the annual sealift, the winter ice roads, the diesel supply chain,
and federal money.**

**What they contribute to the nation that nothing else can:**

1. **Iron. Mary River, and probably nowhere else in the Republic.** Decisive, and discussed above.
2. **A navigable river system with a sixty-year record of pre-industrial commercial operation.**
3. **Lead and zinc at Pine Point**, on a road, on a rail grade, next to a dam.
4. **Tungsten at Mactung and Cantung**, among the very few significant deposits outside China.
5. **A functioning Indigenous-majority political order with land tenure already settled in law**, and a
   population that left the land within living memory rather than centuries ago.
6. **Control of the Northwest Passage's eastern and central sections** — which in a world where the Panhandle
   and the Inside Passage belong to Cascadia, and Hudson Bay's only port belongs to the Prairie Federation, is
   **the Alaska Republic's one uncontested maritime asset.**

**And the bluntest sentence in the file:** the two territories that make up most of the Alaska Republic's
landmass contain, between them, **one thing the nation cannot do without and cannot get anywhere else — an
iron mine on an island at 71° north with no trees on it.**

## Sources that are weaker than they look
- **Wikipedia is the backbone of Parts 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8.** It is well-cited on these topics and the numbers
  cross-check, but **every figure attributed to Wikipedia here should be confirmed against a company filing,
  NRCan, or the Canada Energy Regulator before it is treated as settled.** The NRCan and CER material is
  primary; the Cabin Radio reporting is contemporaneous local journalism and is reliable for 2026 events.
- **The "Cantung and Mactung hold 15% of the world's tungsten" claim is a 2007 company statement** made by the
  CEO in a promotional context. Not independently assessed.
- **A figure of "166 billion barrels of oil reserves" in the Mackenzie Delta region appears on Wikipedia's
  Mackenzie River page and is almost certainly wrong as stated.** It is flagged in Part 3 and deliberately not
  used.
- **Nunavut's "18.3 billion barrels of crude oil resources" and "181.4 trillion cubic feet of gas"** are CER
  figures and are **resources, not reserves** — undeveloped, unpermitted, and offshore-licensing-banned.
