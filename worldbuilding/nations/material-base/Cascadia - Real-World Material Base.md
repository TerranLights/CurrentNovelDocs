## 0. The Shape of the Territory — a Strategic Problem Worth Flagging

This is an unusually **long and thin** country. From Skagway, Alaska (59.5°N) to Cabo San Lucas, Baja
California Sur (22.9°N) is roughly **36.5 degrees of latitude**, on the order of 4,000 km / 2,500 miles as the
crow flies and considerably more by any route a person or a cargo could actually take. Its width is often
measured in tens of kilometers — the Alaska Panhandle strip is in places under 50 km from saltwater to the
Canadian border, and the Baja peninsula averages about 100 km wide.

Four consequences follow directly from geometry, before any politics are added:

1. **There is no interior.** Almost every part of this territory is within a few hundred kilometers of the
   Pacific. That is an enormous advantage for a sea power and a severe vulnerability for a land power: a
   hostile navy, or even a competent commerce raider, cuts the country in half wherever it chooses.
2. **North-south land transport is a chain of chokepoints, not a network.** There is essentially one paved
   spine (I-5 / Mexican Federal Highway 1 / BC Highway 97 and 16) and one or two rail spines, and each has
   named, mappable pinch points — Siskiyou Summit, the Fraser Canyon, the Columbia River Gorge, the
   Transpeninsular Highway's empty middle. Sever any one and the country becomes two countries. **B.3 of
   `Cascadia - Industry, Ports and Transport (supplement).md` enumerates these — and corrects this claim.
   Most of the severable points turn out to cut west-east, not north-south.**
3. **The northern third is roadless and the southern third is waterless.** Southeast Alaska has no road
   connection to anywhere except three small ports; Baja has no water except what is piped in from the
   Colorado River, which originates entirely outside the territory. The productive, populated, self-watering
   middle — the Puget-Willamette trough and the coastal strip of BC — is a relatively small fraction of the
   whole.
4. **The internal cultural fault line runs the wrong way.** Colin Woodard's division between the "Left Coast"
   (the wet strip west of the Cascade and Coast Mountain crests) and the "Far West" (the dry interior) is a
   **west-east** split, but the country's only means of holding itself together is a **north-south** axis. The
   coast can reach the coast; the interior can reach the interior; but the coast and the interior can only
   reach each other through a handful of mountain passes that the interior controls the eastern end of. The
   author's existing note on this tension is materially well-founded — the passes are listed in B.3.7 of
   `Cascadia - Industry, Ports and Transport (supplement).md` and the resource split is flagged throughout.

A practical note for plotting: **the sea is the only thing that makes this country one country.** The Inside
Passage, Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia, and the open Pacific from the Columbia bar to Cabo San Lucas are
the real trunk line. A Cascadia that controls its coastal shipping is coherent; a Cascadia that loses it is a
row of disconnected valleys.

### 1.1 Alaska Panhandle — silver, gold, and an undeveloped rare-earth prospect

**Greens Creek Mine**, Admiralty Island, about 20 miles south of Juneau, operated by **Hecla Mining**. An
underground polymetallic mine and **the fifth-largest silver producer in the world**. 2020 production: **10.5
million troy ounces of silver, 48,500 oz gold, 56,800 tons zinc, 21,400 tons lead**. Proven and probable
reserves (2020): 111.4 Moz silver, 828,000 oz gold, 652,420 tons zinc in about 9 million tons of ore. Employs
about 280 people, roughly three-quarters of them Southeast Alaska residents, who commute daily by company
ferry. It is the largest single taxpayer in the Juneau Borough. Note for the author: the mine sits **inside
Admiralty Island National Monument** and disposes of tailings on two 29-acre surface sites — it is a working
industrial operation embedded in protected wilderness with no road to it.

**Kensington Gold Mine**, north of Juneau, operated by **Coeur Mining** — an underground gold mine reachable
only by boat or air. **[UNVERIFIED]** production figures; the dedicated Wikipedia article returned a 404 in
this session and no primary Coeur or Alaska DGGS figure was retrieved. Order of magnitude is roughly
100,000-150,000 oz gold per year; confirm at coeur.com or dggs.alaska.gov before relying on it.

**Historic:** the **Alaska-Juneau (A-J)** and **Treadwell** mines on Gastineau Channel were among the largest
low-grade gold operations in the world in their day and are the reason Juneau exists. The **Klondike** routes
inland — the White Pass out of Skagway and the Chilkoot out of Dyea — are the historic land gateways from
tidewater to the Yukon interior.

**Undeveloped prospects worth knowing about:**
- **Bokan Mountain**, Prince of Wales Island — a **rare-earth element** deposit (heavy REE-enriched,
  associated with a peralkaline granite), plus historic uranium production. Undeveloped. This is one of very
  few identified REE resources on the North American Pacific coast and is strategically interesting for the
  author precisely because it is *not* developed. **[UNVERIFIED]** tonnage/grade — confirm at dggs.alaska.gov.
- **Palmer Project** near Haines — copper-zinc-silver-gold volcanogenic massive sulfide, undeveloped.
- **Niblack**, Prince of Wales Island — copper-gold-zinc-silver VMS, undeveloped.

**[UNVERIFIED]** A regional total value for Southeast Alaska mineral production was not located in this
session. The Alaska Department of Natural Resources publishes an annual *Alaska's Mineral Industry* report
that carries it.

### 1.5 California — borates and rare earths are the strategically decisive items

California's nonfuel mineral economy is dominated in *tonnage* by construction materials (sand and gravel,
crushed stone, cement) and in *strategic importance* by two specific deposits that are globally significant.
**[UNVERIFIED]** A current California total nonfuel mineral production value and national rank could not be
retrieved in this session; the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries state statistics tables carry it.

**Boron, Kern County — Rio Tinto Borax.** The **largest open-pit mine in California** and the world's largest
single source of **boric acid**; it supplies **close to half of the world's refined borate** demand. Employs
over 800 people. Borates are not a luxury commodity: they go into glass and fiberglass, ceramic glazes,
detergents, fertilizer micronutrients, wood preservatives, and — relevant to the author — **neutron-absorbing
materials and heat-resistant borosilicate glass**. A reconstruction-era power in possession of Boron has
something almost nobody else on the continent has.

**Mountain Pass, San Bernardino County — MP Materials.** The **only operating rare-earth mining and
processing facility in the United States**. A Precambrian **bastnäsite carbonatite** ore body running **8-12%
rare-earth oxides** — extraordinarily rich by world standards — roughly 200 ft thick and 2,300 ft long. In
2020 it supplied about **15.8% of global rare-earth production**, around 38,000 tonnes of REO in concentrate.
Mined 1952 onward, dominant globally through the 1980s, **closed in 2002, reopened 2018**, still operating as
of 2025. **Critical caveat the author should not miss:** as of 2025 much of the concentrate was still being
**separated and refined in China**. Mining rare earths and *using* rare earths are two different industrial
capabilities, and the second one is the hard one. Rare-earth separation is a solvent-extraction cascade
requiring large volumes of industrial acids and bases, precise process control, and a lot of specialist
chemistry.

**Other California commodities:**
- **Construction aggregate and cement** — large, statewide, and the single most important mineral input for
  any reconstruction economy.
- **Salt** — solar evaporation on San Francisco Bay and at Searles Lake / Trona (which also yields borax,
  soda ash, sodium sulfate, and potash from brine).
- **Diatomite** (Lompoc — among the world's largest deposits), **magnesium compounds**, **gypsum**, **pumice**.
- **Gold** — the Mother Lode of the Sierra foothills, the historic basis of the state. Modern hard-rock gold
  production is small; most historic mines are closed. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether any significant lode gold mine
  is currently operating in California — check USGS.
- **Historic and instructive:** **mercury** at **New Almaden** (Santa Clara County) and the Coast Range
  cinnabar belt — mercury is what made Sierra gold recovery possible in the 19th century, and a
  reconstruction-era gold economy would rediscover the same pairing; **tungsten** near Bishop (Pine Creek);
  **iron** at Eagle Mountain (Riverside County, now closed) — the ore that fed the Kaiser steel mill at
  Fontana, the only integrated steelworks ever built on the West Coast.

### 1.6 Baja California peninsula — salt, gypsum, copper, and phosphate

**Exportadora de Sal (ESSA), Guerrero Negro, Baja California Sur.** The **largest solar salt works in the
world**, producing on the order of **7 million tonnes of salt a year** from evaporation ponds on Laguna Ojo de
Liebre. Founded 1957 by Daniel K. Ludwig; since 1973 owned 51% by the Mexican state and 49% by Mitsubishi
Corporation. Exports go to Japan, Korea, the US, Canada, Taiwan, and New Zealand. **[UNVERIFIED]** the pond
acreage and the barge-to-Isla-de-Cedros transshipment detail were not confirmed from a retrievable source in
this session, though the transshipment arrangement is well attested in industry reporting.

Salt deserves emphasis for a post-collapse economy well out of proportion to its modern price. It is the
feedstock for **chlorine and caustic soda** (chlor-alkali), for **soda ash**, for food preservation without
refrigeration, for hide tanning, and for animal husbandry. And critically, **solar salt requires sunlight, a
flat impermeable basin, and seawater — not electricity.** It is one of the very few industrial-scale chemical
operations in this entire territory that would keep working with no grid at all.

**Isla San Marcos, BCS** — one of the world's larger **gypsum** operations. Gypsum means plaster and, blended
with limestone, it is an input to **Portland cement**.

**El Boleo, Santa Rosalía, BCS** — copper-cobalt-zinc-manganese. A historic French-run copper district
(Compagnie du Boléo, from the 1880s) redeveloped in the modern era. **[UNVERIFIED]** current operating status
and production; ownership has passed through Baja Mining and Korean state interests (KORES). Confirm before
use.

**El Arco, Baja California** — a very large **undeveloped copper porphyry** held by Grupo México.
**[UNVERIFIED]** reserve figures; this is a real and significant deposit but the numbers could not be
confirmed here.

**San Juan de la Costa, BCS — phosphate rock.** Flag this one. **Phosphate is the one major fertilizer
nutrient that cannot be manufactured from air and energy** the way nitrogen can (Haber-Bosch) — it must be
mined. There is very little phosphate rock anywhere on the North American Pacific slope. A Cascadia that holds
San Juan de la Costa holds the only obvious domestic phosphate source in the entire country, which makes a
remote, hot, dry corner of Baja California Sur strategically disproportionate. **[UNVERIFIED]** reserve and
production figures — check the Servicio Geológico Mexicano (sgm.gob.mx).

**Gold:** **Los Cardones** near La Paz is a proposed open-pit gold project that has been blocked on
environmental and water grounds. Note for the author: local opposition to mining in BCS is specifically about
**water**, not aesthetics.

## 2. Agriculture

### 2.0 The organizing fact: the Cascade crest splits this territory into two agricultures

Everything in this section follows from one geographic mechanism. The Cascade Range (and, further south, the
Sierra Nevada; further north, the Coast Mountains) wrings moisture out of Pacific air and leaves a rain shadow
behind it.

**West of the crest** — the Fraser Valley, the Puget lowland, the Skagit Valley, the Willamette Valley — is
cool, wet, and **rainfed**, on deep alluvial and glaciolacustrine soils. It grows grass seed, hazelnuts,
berries, vegetable seed, nursery stock, potatoes, vegetables, and pasture-based dairy, and it does so without
a single pump.

**East of the crest** — the Columbia Basin, the Yakima and Wenatchee valleys, the Okanagan, the Treasure
Valley, the Klamath Basin — receives on the order of **7 to 10 inches of rain a year** and is shrub-steppe. Its
enormous output exists **only** because water is impounded, diverted, and in central Washington's case lifted
280 feet uphill with electricity.

**The one great exception east of the mountains is the Palouse**, and it is important enough to state twice:
15-25 inches of rain falling on loess up to **75 meters (246 feet) deep** produces one of the most valuable
dryland wheat regions on Earth with essentially **zero irrigation**.

Sources for this section: USDA NASS 2022 Census of Agriculture and state statistical bulletins, Statistics
Canada 2021 Census of Agriculture, the Bureau of Reclamation, and provincial/state extension services. Full
URLs in Section 8.

### 2.1 Alaska Panhandle — essentially no farming, and a world-class fishery instead

There is no meaningful agriculture in Southeast Alaska and there is no prospect of any. The reasons are
physical and permanent: the terrain is mountains dropping straight into fjords with almost no flat land, the
soils are thin, acidic, podzolized forest soils over bedrock or glacial till, the growing season is short, and
the rainfall is extreme — Ketchikan averages on the order of 150 inches a year, which leaches soils and rots
grain. Historically the region supported small market gardens, potatoes, and hay near the towns, plus
subsistence berry picking. That is the realistic ceiling.

**What the Panhandle has instead is protein.** Commercial fisheries — all five Pacific salmon species (pink,
chum, sockeye, coho, Chinook), Pacific halibut, sablefish (black cod), Dungeness crab, shrimp, and herring —
together with a substantial state and private non-profit **hatchery** system that releases fry to supplement
wild runs, and shore processing plants at Ketchikan, Petersburg, Sitka, and elsewhere.

**[UNVERIFIED]** Southeast-Alaska-specific harvest tonnage and ex-vessel value figures were not retrieved from
a primary source in this session. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game (adfg.alaska.gov) publishes annual
commercial salmon harvest and value tables broken out by region, and NOAA Fisheries publishes landings data —
the author should pull the Southeast/Region 1 numbers directly.

Two points matter for the post-collapse analysis and are worth stating plainly here:

1. **Salmon is a self-renewing, self-delivering protein supply.** The fish swim to the fisherman. Harvesting
   them needs boats, nets, and knowledge — not fuel-intensive industry, not fertilizer, not a grid. Smoking,
   salting, and drying preserve them without refrigeration, and salt is available (see Section 1.6).
2. **The hatchery component is not self-renewing.** Hatcheries need facilities, water control, feed, and
   trained staff. If they fail, runs fall back to whatever the wild habitat supports — still substantial, but
   lower, and it would take years to find the new level.

#### 2.2.1 The Palouse — the most important dryland fact in the north

The Palouse is covered in **wind-deposited loess up to 75 m (246 ft) thick**, the uppermost layer laid down
within the last 15,000 years, on rolling hills at 1,000-3,000 ft. The soil is finely textured and **highly
moisture-retentive**. Annual precipitation is roughly **15-25 inches**, with reliable winter snow cover
protecting the winter wheat crop from freeze kill.

**Soft white winter wheat** dominates — **[SOURCES DISAGREE]**: 80% or 85% of state production depending on
source. Soft white is low-protein; it is bought for Asian noodles, sponge cake, crackers, and Middle Eastern
flatbread, **not** for Western bread loaves. Roughly **87% of Washington wheat is dryland-farmed**
(**[UNVERIFIED]** — a 1990s figure with no current NASS confirmation) on a **summer-fallow rotation**, in which
about half the acreage sits idle each year to bank two years of soil moisture for one crop.

**Whitman County is the single sharpest data point in this whole file.** 2022 Census: 933 farms,
**1,218,301 acres** in farms, average farm 1,306 acres, **$465,358,000 in sales** (up 67% since 2017), 96% of
it crops. Grains, oilseeds, dry beans, and dry peas alone: **$431,640,000, first in Washington and 19th among
all 2,917 US counties reporting that category.**

**Acres irrigated in Whitman County: 4,583.** The Census prints the percentage of farmland irrigated as
"(Z)" — statistically zero.

Half a billion dollars of crops off 1.2 million acres, with essentially no irrigation. The Palouse is also the
most important **lentil, dry pea, and chickpea** region in the United States — pulses that **fix their own
nitrogen** and rotate with the wheat, which matters enormously for the post-collapse analysis.

**The export catch:** roughly **90% of Washington wheat is exported** (Japan, South Korea, Egypt, the
Philippines, India), and **over 60% of that moves by barge** down the Snake-Columbia system to Portland and
Vancouver terminals. A Palouse harvest that cannot reach Asia is a harvest of a specialty wheat class with
limited domestic demand at scale.

#### 2.2.2 The Columbia Basin Project — the load-bearing machine of central Washington

**The acreage gap.** Reclamation's own history gives the irrigable lands as originally estimated at
**1,029,000 acres**, revised upward by better survey to **1,095,000 acres**. Reclamation's current web page
says the project "serves about **671,000 acres**."

**[SOURCES DISAGREE]** on the developed figure: Reclamation's 1998 project history says "just over 500,000
acres"; its current page says ~671,000; the Columbia Basin Development League says "nearly 700,000 acres have
access to Project water" with "over 300,000 acres await a reliable source." Best reading: ~500,000 in the late
1990s, ~671,000 now, against an authorized ~1,029,000-1,095,000. **The project is about 60% built, eighty
years on.**

**Why the second half was never built — and this is a genuinely good story.** The **East High Canal** was
never constructed. Reclamation's history is blunt: eastern-basin dryland wheat farmers, enjoying high wartime
and postwar prices plus a wet cycle that began in 1940, **lost interest in irrigation** and objected to the
small farm-size caps that came with project water. "About three-quarters of the lands in the eastern part of
the project were petitioned out of the project. So many landowners withdrew their lands... that construction
of the East High Canal was determined impractical before it was even designed." By the 1970s those same
farmers *did* need water, and it was too late. They now mine the **Odessa Subarea aquifer**, which is
depleting. The Columbia Basin Development League estimates up to **87,700 acres** of deep-well-irrigated land
needs to be shifted to surface water, and that failure would cost **3,600 jobs and $840 million a year**.

**The pumping.** Water does not flow to the Columbia Basin; it is lifted.

- **Twelve pumps** at the John W. Keys III Pump-Generating Plant lift water **280 feet** from Lake Roosevelt
  into a 1.6-mile feeder canal, each rated **65,000-70,000 horsepower**.
- **When pumping, they consume about 600 MW of electricity** — the entire output of a mid-sized power station,
  dedicated to lifting water uphill.
- Six of the twelve are reversible pump-generators with **314 MW** of generating capability.
- **Banks Lake**: 27 miles long, about 500 feet above the Columbia, **715,000 acre-feet** of active storage.
- **O'Sullivan Dam**: 19,000 ft long, 200 ft high — the fourth-longest dam in the world when completed in
  1947-49 — impounding **Potholes Reservoir**.
- **West Canal** 88 miles / 5,100 cfs; **East Low Canal** 87 miles / 4,500 cfs; **Potholes Canal** ~62-70
  miles / 3,900 cfs serving 278,816 acres.
- Overall: **300+ miles of main canal, ~2,000 miles of laterals, 3,500 miles of drains and wasteways**.
  Irrigators use about **2.5 million acre-feet** of Columbia water a year, plus roughly **1 million acre-feet**
  recovered by reusing drain and wasteway return flow.
- Three districts operate the local works: Quincy-Columbia Basin, East Columbia Basin, South Columbia Basin.

#### 2.2.4 Dairy and the maritime valleys

Statewide milk sales $1.59B (9th in the US), cattle and calves $1.29B (14th), 1,123,261 cattle. **Yakima
County is Washington's top dairy county** at **$630 million** in milk — 11th among all US counties — on an
irrigated-forage confinement model. **Whatcom County is second** at $205 million on only 102,886 acres with an
average farm size of **65 acres** — a pasture-and-forage maritime model that is structurally a completely
different kind of agriculture, and a far more resilient one.

**Skagit County is a global bottleneck hiding in a small valley.** Per WSU Extension: about **75% of the US
spinach seed supply** (~8% of world production), **~75% of US cabbage seed** (~25% of world), and **~95% of US
table beet seed** (~25% of world). Also ~500 acres of tulips, about **75% of US commercial tulip production**.
Note the distinction: Skagit produces vegetable **seed**, not vegetables. Biennial seed crops need a specific
cool-wet-winter / dry-enough-late-summer sequence that exists in only a handful of places on Earth. Losing
Skagit would not be felt as a local food shortage — it would be felt as a **continental vegetable-seed
shortage the following year.**

#### 2.3.1 The Willamette Valley and grass seed

- **Over 400,000 acres** of grass seed statewide; **more than half of the Willamette Valley's harvested
  cropland**.
- **Nearly two-thirds of total US cool-season grass seed production**, and Oregon growers produce
  **essentially all US supplies of annual ryegrass, perennial ryegrass, bentgrass, and fine fescue**. OSU
  describes Oregon as the world's major producer of cool-season forage and turf grass seed.
- Over 950 varieties across eight species; ~600 million lb in 2017 across ~1,500 farms.

**Why it works there:** mild wet winters and a reliably **dry early summer**. Grass seed needs winter rain to
build vegetative mass and then a dry ripening window so the seed sets and combines without shattering or
sprouting in the head. Only about **15% of Willamette Valley annual rainfall falls April through September**.
That same summer drought is why grass seed took over the poorly-drained valley-floor soils that are too wet in
winter for row crops.

**A pointed observation for the author:** grass seed is a **non-food crop**. Its demand is lawns, golf
courses, sports turf, erosion control, and forage. In any scenario where discretionary turf demand collapses,
roughly **half the Willamette Valley's cropland is growing something nobody eats** — and those same fields
would grow wheat, oats, clover, and vegetables perfectly well, as they did in the 19th century when the Valley
was a wheat exporter. The transition is agronomically easy and economically violent.

Other Willamette Valley output: **nursery and greenhouse $1.22 billion** (Oregon's #1 commodity, 3rd in the
US); **hazelnuts** (87,128 acres, essentially all of it here, 100% of US production); **Pinot noir and other
wine grapes**; **Christmas trees, 1st in the US**. Marion County is Oregon's top agricultural county at $875
million; **Linn County ($342M) is the grass-seed heartland and has only 12% of its farmland irrigated** —
which is the point.

**[UNVERIFIED]** — Willamette Valley vegetable seed production is significant but no primary-source acreage or
world-share figure comparable to Skagit's was located.

- **Umatilla County** $526M, 1,491,922 acres (Oregon's largest), 132,558 irrigated (9%).
- **Malheur County** $511M, 1,130,142 acres, **146,174 irrigated (13%)** — 60% livestock by value.
- **Harney County** 1,479,684 acres in farms, almost entirely rangeland.

- **Hay is consistently Oregon's #1 or #2 commodity** — 900,000 acres, 2.78 million tons, $714M in 2023 —

**The Owyhee Project** (USBR) delivers full irrigation to **over 105,000 acres** plus supplemental water to
13,000 more along the west side of the Snake, **about 72% in Oregon and 28% in Idaho**. Owyhee Dam, canals,
pipelines, tunnels, and **nine pumping plants**. Oregon produced **18.9% of US dry onions in 2023 (3rd

#### 2.3.3 The Klamath Basin — the region's live case study in switching irrigation off

The **Klamath Project** straddles the Oregon-California line: **seven dams, 717+ miles of canals, ~728 miles
of drains, 28 pumping stations**, storing water in Clear Lake, Upper Klamath Lake, Lower Klamath Lake, and
Tule Lake. **[SOURCES DISAGREE]** on served acreage — 170,000 acres is the figure usually used for currently
irrigated Oregon-side land, while 200,000-225,000 includes California-side and historically reclaimed lakebed.
Crops: barley, alfalfa, hay, oats, potatoes, wheat. Klamath County sold $170 million in 2022, **down 12% since
2017** — one of the few Pacific Northwest counties to fall.

**The conflict, in sequence:** Lost River and shortnose suckers were listed as endangered in 1988, and coho
salmon are also protected. In **2001** Reclamation shut irrigation off to roughly 170,000 acres for the first
time to hold lake levels and river flows, producing a national political crisis. Deliveries were restored in
2002 and a massive lower-river fish kill followed. In the **2021-22 drought** Reclamation allocated roughly
**50,000 acre-feet against a normal project supply of about 350,000** — about 14% — and irrigation districts
publicly defied the shutoff order.

For the author this is the best available real-world rehearsal of an irrigation-dependent farm economy being
switched off by an authority upstream, complete with the politics.

### 2.4 California — the agricultural center of gravity, and the most fragile thing in the territory

California is, by a wide margin, the most productive farmland in this country and possibly the most productive
on Earth by value per acre. It is also the part of the territory that most completely depends on machinery to
exist. Both facts need to be held at once.

**Scale.** California farms and ranches took in **$61.2 billion in cash receipts in 2024**, up about 3.6% on
2023 (CDFA). The state grows something like half of all US vegetables and over three-quarters of US fruits and
nuts (CDFA). Top commodities by 2024 value: milk ($8.61B), almonds ($5.66B), grapes ($5.64B), cattle and
calves ($4.98B), lettuce ($3.67B), strawberries ($3.46B), pistachios ($2.05B), tomatoes ($1.64B), carrots
($1.57B), broilers ($1.37B).

**[SOURCES DISAGREE]** The Wikipedia "Economy of California" article cites roughly **$42.6 billion** in
agricultural sales, against CDFA's $61.2B for 2024 and ~$59.4B for 2023. The lower figure is probably an older
year or a narrower definition (crop sales only, excluding livestock, or value-added versus cash receipts).
**Use the CDFA figure**; it is the primary source.

**Near-monopolies.** California supplies effectively **100% of the US commercial almond crop and roughly 80%
of the world's**; ~86% of US fresh strawberries and ~98% of frozen; ~90% of US-grown avocados; over 90% of US
dates; ~96% of prunes; over 80% of raspberries; nearly all US broccoli; roughly 70% of peaches. It is the
second-largest US rice producer after Arkansas. (Wikipedia, *Agriculture in California*, compiling USDA
figures — mostly 2017 vintage, so treat percentages as approximate and the underlying USDA NASS series as the
authority.)

**Land.** About **25.3 million acres** of farmland across roughly **77,100 farms**, average size 328 acres,
notably smaller than the US average of 444 — a signature of high-value irrigated specialty cropping rather
than extensive grain. Agriculture uses roughly **40% of California's total water consumption** (2017 figures).

#### 2.4.1 The water machine

This is the single most important body of fact in the file. California's agricultural output is not a property
of California's soil and climate alone; it is a property of soil, climate, **and roughly a century of pumping
plants, canals, and dams**, most of which move water uphill.

**The Sierra Nevada snowpack is the reservoir behind the reservoirs.** California has a Mediterranean climate:
wet winters, essentially rainless summers. The Sierra snowpack stores winter precipitation as ice and releases
it through the dry season. **[UNVERIFIED]** The commonly quoted figure is that snowmelt supplies roughly 30%
of California's water supply; the California DWR Snow Surveys page could not be retrieved in this session (it
had been moved), so the author should confirm the exact share at water.ca.gov before using a number. The
*mechanism* — snow as free seasonal storage, with April 1 snow-water content as the standard benchmark — is
not in dispute.

**Central Valley Project (US Bureau of Reclamation).** About **20 dams and reservoirs**, ~**13.4 million
acre-feet** of combined storage, delivering roughly **7 million acre-feet a year** and irrigating about
**3 million acres**. Roughly **644 miles of canals**, including the Delta-Mendota Canal, the Friant-Kern
Canal, the Madera Canal, and the Contra Costa Canal. Principal powerhouses: Shasta (676 MW), New Melones
(300 MW), Folsom (199 MW), Trinity (140 MW), Friant (25 MW). Total CVP generating capacity about **2,254 MW**,
annual generation ~5.18 TWh (2004 figure — dated; flag it).

**State Water Project (California DWR).** Delivers roughly **2.3-2.4 million acre-feet a year**, supplying
drinking water to **over 27 million people** and irrigating about **750,000 acres**. Storage includes **Lake
Oroville (3.54 million acre-feet)** behind **Oroville Dam, at 770 ft the tallest dam in the United States**;
the SWP share of San Luis Reservoir (1,020,500 AF); Castaic (325,000 AF); Pyramid (180,000 AF); Perris
(131,400 AF); Silverwood (73,000 AF).

**The California Aqueduct** runs **304 miles** in its main section, **444 miles** including branches, with a
maximum design flow of about **13,100 cubic feet per second**. It starts at the **Harvey O. Banks Pumping
Plant**, which lifts Delta water roughly **224-240 feet** (sources give both) at up to 10,670 cfs, and it
crosses the Tehachapi Mountains at the **Edmonston Pumping Plant**, which lifts water **1,926 feet in a single
stage** through fourteen units at 4,480 cfs. South of the Tehachapis it splits into the West Branch (to
Castaic and the Los Angeles Basin), the East Branch (140 miles, to Lake Perris), and the Coastal Branch (116
miles, to San Luis Obispo and Santa Barbara counties).

**The decisive number:** the State Water Project **consumes about 11,500 GWh of electricity a year and
generates about 6,500 GWh**, making it **the single largest consumer of electricity in California**. It is a
net electricity sink of roughly 5,000 GWh annually. Southern California's water supply is, physically, an
electrical product. If the grid stops, the water stops — not gradually, but within the residence time of the
local reservoirs.

**The Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta is the hub and the weak point.** ~1,153 square miles, ~200 islands, about
**1,100 miles of levees** around 700 miles of waterways. Because the levees exposed the former tidal marsh's
peat soils to oxygen, the peat oxidized and the islands sank: the central and western Delta is **at least 15
feet below sea level**, with up to 25 feet of subsidence in places. Over **5 million acre-feet a year** are
exported through the Delta to the San Joaquin Valley and Southern California, supporting ~1,130,000 acres of
farmland and water supply for roughly **25 million people**. One cited estimate puts the probability of a
catastrophic multi-island levee failure (20 islands at once) at **62% within two decades** absent action. A
failure of that kind pulls brackish Bay water into the export pumps and stops the exports — the levees are not
holding water out of farmland so much as holding salt away from the intake of the entire southern half of the
state.

**Water that comes from outside the territory.** Two of California's largest supplies do not originate in
Cascadia at all:

- The **Colorado River Aqueduct** (Metropolitan Water District) and the **All-American Canal** (Imperial
  Valley) draw on the Colorado, whose watershed is in the Rockies and the Southwest — outside this country's
  borders on the author's map.
- The **Los Angeles Aqueduct** draws from the Owens Valley on the east side of the Sierra, which is inside
  California but on the far side of the range and dependent on its own long conveyance.
- The Bay Area's **Hetch Hetchy** and **Mokelumne** aqueducts are internal but are still long gravity
  conveyances from the Sierra.

For a self-contained Cascadia, the Colorado dependency is a standing strategic problem: the Imperial Valley
and a large share of Southern California and **all of the Mexicali Valley** drink from a river the country
does not control the headwaters of.

**Groundwater and subsidence.** Decades of overdraft in the San Joaquin Valley have physically lowered the
ground. Subsidence around Corcoran and the Tulare Basin has been severe enough to require levee raising to
protect the town. The **Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA)** requires basins to reach balance and
is widely projected to force several hundred thousand acres out of production. **[UNVERIFIED]** — the exact
retirement acreage projections (PPIC has published estimates in the range of 500,000-900,000 acres) could not
be confirmed from a primary source in this session; check ppic.org/water.

#### 2.4.2 What California grew before the pumps — the useful historical analogue

This is the most directly useful precedent in the whole file, because it is a real, documented case of the
same land being farmed at scale without modern water engineering.

**The natural hydrology was a flood-and-dry system, not a desert.** **Tulare Lake**, in the southern San
Joaquin Valley, was the largest freshwater lake west of the Mississippi by surface area — roughly **690 square
miles** at full extent, holding as much as **6.5 million acre-feet**, fed by the Kings, Kaweah, Tule, Kern,
and White Rivers off the Sierra. Diversion for irrigation from the mid-19th century onward, plus upstream
damming, dried it. Its bed is now prime farmland growing cotton, tomatoes, pistachios, almonds, walnuts,
alfalfa, wheat, barley, and dairy forage. In the wet winter of **2023 the lake came back over 114,000 acres**,
flooding farms and buildings. The lesson for the author: the basin's default state is a terminal lake, and it
will revert if the diversions stop.

**The wheat boom.** By the 1890s California was the **second-largest wheat-producing state in the US, over one
million tons a year**, and it was grown with **almost no irrigation** — as late as 1899 only about **12% of
California's improved farmland was irrigated**, up from essentially nothing in 1850. Dry-farmed winter wheat
on the Sacramento and San Joaquin plains worked because the Mediterranean climate delivers rain in the winter
growing season, which is exactly when a winter cereal needs it. The boom declined as continuous mono-cropping
depleted soils, and the state shifted toward irrigated specialty crops.

**Read this straight across to the post-collapse scenario:** a Cascadia that has lost Edmonston, Banks, and
the Delta export pumps does **not** get a dead Central Valley. It gets a **19th-century Central Valley** —
dry-farmed winter grain, rangeland cattle, riparian orchards and gardens along the rivers, and a seasonal lake
in the Tulare Basin. That is a real and substantial agricultural economy. It is simply not an almond-and-
pistachio export economy, and it supports a fraction of the population.

#### 2.4.3 Climate and soil gradients

- **North to south rainfall collapse:** Redding gets substantial winter rain; Bakersfield is semi-arid; the
  Imperial Valley is true desert, below sea level, and is farmed **entirely** on Colorado River water.
- **Central Valley alluvium** is deep and fertile; the **Salinas Valley** ("the salad bowl") is coastal,
  fog-cooled, and irrigated from its own groundwater basin; **Delta peat soils** are organic and subsiding.
- Long frost-free seasons in the south support the citrus belt and double-cropping; the coastal fog belt from
  Monterey to Santa Maria is what makes California dominant in lettuce, broccoli, artichokes, and strawberries
  and is a *climatic*, not an engineered, advantage.

### 2.5 Baja California peninsula — desert farming on borrowed water

The peninsula is desert nearly end to end (Sonoran in the northeast, Vizcaíno in the center, with a
Mediterranean fringe in the far northwest around Ensenada and the Guadalupe Valley, and a subtropical dry
climate in the Cape region). Baja California Sur averages **under 200 mm (about 8 inches) of rain a year**,
highly variable between years. Every significant agricultural district on the peninsula exists because water
was brought to it or pumped out from under it.

**The Mexicali Valley** is the big one, and it is the clearest case in the whole territory of agriculture as a
pure artifact of interstate plumbing. It is irrigated **entirely from the Colorado River**, delivered across
the border at **Morelos Dam** under the **1944 US-Mexico water treaty**, which allocates Mexico **1,500,000
acre-feet a year** (about 1.9 km³). The valley grows over fifty crops — wheat, cotton, alfalfa, asparagus,
broccoli, carrots, green onions, lettuce, peas, peppers, radishes, tomatoes — and is a major fresh-produce
exporter. Note the vulnerability already visible in the real world: US canal-lining projects that reduce
seepage also reduce the incidental recharge that Mexican farmland has been drawing on.

**San Quintín**, on the Pacific coast south of Ensenada, produces strawberries, tomatoes, and cucumbers for
the US market on **overdrawn groundwater**, to the point that seawater intrusion has forced growers toward
**desalination**. This is a district that is already, in the real world, farming on desalinated seawater — a
useful signal of exactly how thin the margin is.

**The Guadalupe Valley** inland of Ensenada is Mexico's principal wine region, and is also in aquifer
overdraft.

**In Baja California Sur**, the Santo Domingo / Ciudad Constitución district (Valle de Santo Domingo) grows
alfalfa, wheat, and vegetables on groundwater that has been drawn down and has suffered saline intrusion.
Date palms at **San Ignacio** and **Mulegé** are oasis agriculture on natural springs — small, ancient, and
genuinely sustainable, which makes them a good model for what the peninsula supports without machinery.

**Urban water is also imported.** **Tijuana**, with roughly 1.8 million people, has almost no local supply; it
is fed principally by the **Colorado River-Tijuana aqueduct**, which lifts water over the peninsular ranges
from the Mexicali side. **[UNVERIFIED]** exact aqueduct length, capacity, and pumping lift were not confirmed
in this session — CONAGUA and the Comisión Estatal del Agua de Baja California publish them. The structural
point is verified and is what matters: **Tijuana drinks pumped Colorado River water carried over a mountain
range.**

Desalination exists at Los Cabos, Cabo San Lucas, and elsewhere, and a large plant at Rosarito has been
repeatedly proposed. **[UNVERIFIED]** capacities.

**Fisheries are the peninsula's genuine renewable wealth.** The **Gulf of California** is among the most
biologically productive seas on Earth — sardine, shrimp, tuna, squid. **Ensenada** is Mexico's principal
Pacific fishing port. Bluefin tuna ranching operates offshore of Ensenada, and Pacific-side cooperatives
(notably on the Vizcaíno coast) hold lobster and abalone concessions that are internationally cited as
well-managed. **[UNVERIFIED]** tonnages — CONAPESCA publishes them.

### 2.6 British Columbia — very little arable land, very high value per acre

**The central fact is scarcity of land, not scarcity of output.**

- BC total farm area, 2021 Census: **5,648,160 acres** (~2.29 million ha), **down 11.8% from 2016**, across
  **15,841 farms** (down 9.6%).
- BC holds about **8.3% of Canada's farms but only about 3.7% of its farmland** (arithmetic from StatCan
  figures, not a published statistic).
- Land actually in farms is about **2.5% of the province**.
- **Only about 5% of BC's land base is considered suitable for agriculture at all, and only 2.7% is capable of
  growing a reasonable range of crops** (Canada Land Inventory Classes 1-4).
- Roughly **three-quarters of the province sits above 1,000 meters elevation.**

**The Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR).** A provincial land-use designation created in **1973** under the
Barrett NDP government, in which agriculture is the priority use and non-agricultural uses are restricted,
administered by the independent **Agricultural Land Commission**. It covers approximately **4.6 million
hectares**, about **4.9% of BC's land base**. **[UNVERIFIED]** — less than a quarter of ALR land is prime
(CLI Class 1-3), only about **1.1% of BC's territory**; that percentage comes from Wikipedia rather than a
government source, though the 4.6 million hectare total is from the BC government directly. The ALR was
created because an estimated **4,000-6,000 hectares of prime farmland was being lost to urbanization every
year** before it existed. One report found roughly half of ALR land currently lying unused.

**The ALR is a remarkably useful piece of real-world worldbuilding.** It is a fifty-year-old, legally
entrenched, province-wide decision to **freeze the best land against development** — a polity acting on the
knowledge that its arable base is tiny and irreplaceable. That is exactly the kind of institution a
reconstruction-era state would need, and the author has a real precedent for it.

**Farm finances, 2025 (StatCan):** cash receipts **$5,505.2 million** (up 9.0%), operating expenses
**$5,223.1 million**, and **realized net farm income of NEGATIVE $318.8 million.** BC agriculture in aggregate
does not turn a realized profit. It is a high-value, high-cost, land-constrained sector propped up by scale
economies and export markets.

**Fraser Valley — dairy, poultry, berries.** BC holds **27,008 acres of highbush blueberries, 88.6% of
Canada's total**, and leads the country in farms reporting highbush blueberries, grapes, and sweet cherries.
Canada exported **127.7 million kg of blueberries worth $533.5 million** in 2021, 85.9 million kg of it to the
US. The Fraser Valley has **more than half the dairy producers in BC and about a third of the poultry and hog
operations**. Abbotsford alone produces roughly **a sixth of BC's agricultural value** (**[UNVERIFIED]** — the
underlying commodity figures are from 2010). BC also has **66.0 million sq ft of greenhouse** (20% of Canada)
and **3.4 million sq ft of mushroom growing, 47.2% of Canada's total and the largest in the country**, plus
22.9 million hens and chickens.

**Okanagan Valley — the Canadian Yakima.** Officially **semi-arid**; the majority of BC's tree fruit is grown
here, **with irrigation**. Precipitation falls from about **400 mm (16 in) at Vernon to 250-300 mm (10-12 in)
at Osoyoos**. "Irrigation is needed for most production in the region due to lack of precipitation during the
growing season." Province-wide BC has **12,829 acres of grapes, 36.7% of Canada's total** (up 32.9% since
2016), and **7,046 acres of sweet cherries, 94.7% of Canada's total** (up 43.5%). **[UNVERIFIED]** — wine
trade sources give ~9,617 acres of Okanagan vineyard across 11 sub-appellations spanning ~175 km from Vernon
to Osoyoos, about 86% of BC's planted vineyard, with ~186 licensed wineries.

**Peace River — BC's only real field-crop country.** High, prairie-like flatland in the northeast with long
summer days; soils erodible and acidic in places. Beef, **canola, forage seed, wheat, barley**, plus
short-season roots and vegetables. It is agriculturally continuous with the Alberta Peace Country and the
prairie system — **not** with coastal or interior BC. **[UNVERIFIED]** — the commonly cited figure that the
Peace holds about **32% of the province's ALR land** could not be confirmed on the government page.

**Site C / John Horgan Dam and the farmland trade-off. [SOURCES DISAGREE], and badly.** BC Hydro's official
accounting and a farmer-led recount reported by The Narwhal do not reconcile. Figures in circulation: 107 km
of river and tributaries flooded; 13,000 ha of agricultural land impacted in total; 3,800 ha of ALR farmland
flooded; 6,469 ha of farmland destroyed by dam and reservoir per the farmer recount; a further 5,900 ha in a
"stability impact zone"; 174 ha of prime land threatened by landslide-generated wave; 203 ha in a flood impact
zone; 109 ha for the Highway 29 relocation. **These do not add up to a single consistent total. Report the
disagreement rather than picking a number.** Projected microclimate effects include average summer
temperatures dropping **3.3°C at Bear Flat** and an additional **13 hours of fog a year** at Fort St. John
airport. The claim that the Peace Valley "can feed a million people" is an advocacy assertion, not a study
finding.

The Site C trade — **flooding some of the only Class 1-2 arable land in northern British Columbia in order to
build 1,100+ MW of firm hydro** — is the exact dilemma at the heart of this file, already played out in the
real world, with real people on both sides.

#### 2.6.1 BC food self-sufficiency — there is a real study

The BC Ministry of Agriculture and Lands' **2006 *B.C.'s Food Self Reliance* report** (2001 data):

- **BC farmers produce 48% of all foods consumed in BC.**
- Measured against **Canada's Food Guide** rather than actual consumption, self-reliance drops to **34%** —
  because a healthy diet calls for more fruit and vegetables than British Columbians actually eat.
- Producing a healthy diet for the 2001 population would require **2.15 million hectares of food-producing
  land, of which 215,000 ha must be irrigated**; only about 189,000 ha of irrigated land existed in 2005.
- Feeding the projected **2025** population a healthy diet would require **2.78 million hectares with 281,000
  ha irrigated — a 49% increase in irrigated land over 2005 levels.**

A peer-reviewed companion study found category self-sufficiency falling between 1986 and 2006: **fruit
72%→49%, non-potato field vegetables 39%→13%, human-consumption cereals 267%→54%.**

**[UNVERIFIED] / dated:** the headline 48% rests on 2001 data and is now about 25 years old. Given that BC's
farm area fell 11.8% between 2016 and 2021 alone while population grew, the current figure is very likely
lower. No updated government study was found.

**This is the most quotable number in the northern half of the file.** British Columbia, in the real world,
with functioning global trade, feeds itself about half — and that was a generation ago.

### 2.7 Cross-cutting: which crops survive without pumps

| | **West of the crest** | **East of the crest (basin)** | **East of the crest (Palouse)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual precipitation | Willamette Valley ~30-45 in | **Tri-Cities 7.24 in**; wine country ~8 in; Okanagan 10-16 in | ~15-25 in (Spokane 16.81 in) |
| Summer rainfall | only ~15% of annual falls Apr-Sep | essentially none | essentially none; crop lives on stored winter moisture |
| Soil | deep alluvium and glaciolacustrine silt from the Missoula floods | glacial outwash, flood-scoured basalt, sand, thin loess | **loess up to 75 m deep**, highly moisture-retentive |
| Irrigation | largely optional | **absolutely required** | **not required** |

**Would fail outright without pumps, dams, and canals:** potatoes (Columbia Basin — 630 cwt/acre needs
full-season water on 7-inch ground); onions (Columbia Basin, Treasure Valley); **hops** (Yakima, perennial,
high demand, 8 inches of rain); **apples, cherries, pears** (Wenatchee, Yakima, Okanogan, Okanagan — perennial
trees, where a single missed season kills the orchard rather than the crop); **wine grapes** (99.9% of
Washington's production is in ~8-inch-rainfall shrub-steppe); alfalfa hay (extremely high water use per ton);
sweet corn, carrots, dry beans, green peas; the entire Klamath Project crop list, which has already failed
twice in living memory.

**Dryland-viable on rainfall alone:** **soft white winter wheat** (Palouse, Columbia Plateau); spring wheat
and barley; **dry peas, lentils, chickpeas** (the Palouse is the most important US lentil region — and pulses
fix their own nitrogen); **grass seed** (Willamette — the dry summer is the asset, not the obstacle);
hazelnuts (historically rainfed, though new plantings increasingly irrigate — flag as shifting); pasture,
grass hay and forage (Fraser Valley, western WA/OR, Peace River); **canola, wheat, barley and forage seed in
the Peace River**; berries in the Fraser Valley and Whatcom County (supplemental irrigation typical but the
climate is fundamentally adequate).

**Dependence on modern industrial inputs — three separate failure modes:**

1. **Synthetic nitrogen.** Haber-Bosch ammonia uses natural gas as both feedstock and energy source.
   Roughly a third of global food production uses Haber-Bosch ammonia, supporting nearly half the world's
   population. The Pacific Northwest's heaviest nitrogen users are exactly the irrigated crops — potatoes,
   corn, hops, irrigated wheat — while **Palouse dryland wheat and its pulse rotation are comparatively
   low-input**, which is a second, independent reason the Palouse survives and the Basin does not.
2. **Refrigeration and controlled-atmosphere storage.** Washington's 7.6 billion pounds of apples are marketed
   year-round out of CA storage from a harvest of a few autumn weeks. Basin and Treasure Valley onion and
   potato economics rest entirely on long-term climate-controlled storage. Without it, the marketable window
   for these crops collapses from a year to weeks.
3. **Export markets.** ~90% of Washington wheat exported; Oregon grass seed is turf, not food; Oregon nursery
   stock and Christmas trees ($1.39 billion combined) are pure discretionary goods; BC's blueberry sector runs
   on US demand.

**The single most important sentence in this section:** the Columbia Basin Project draws **600 MW while
pumping**. If that stops, roughly **671,000 acres** of the most productive irrigated ground in North America
reverts to shrub-steppe within one season — and **Whitman County, 150 miles away, does not notice.**

#### 3.1.2 Grand Coulee in detail — the single most important structure in the territory

- **550 feet tall, 5,223 feet along the crest, roughly 11.98 million cubic yards of concrete.** For scale,
  that is more concrete than any other structure in the United States.
- **6,809 MW nameplate** (up to about 7,079 MW maximum) across three powerhouses: Left (1,155 MW, nine main
  plus three service units), Right (1,125 MW, nine main units), and the Third Power Plant (4,215 MW, six
  units, including individual 600 MW and 805 MW machines). Annual output up to about **21 billion kWh**.
- **Lake Roosevelt**: 9,562,000 acre-feet total storage, 5,185,400 acre-feet active.
- **Spillway** 1,650 feet long, rated at 1,000,000 cubic feet per second.
- **The John W. Keys III Pump-Generating Plant**: twelve pumps rated 65,000-70,000 horsepower each, six of
  which are reversible **pump-generators** at 50-53.5 MW apiece; about 314 MW combined, moving up to 1,605
  cubic feet per second, lifting water roughly **280 feet** out of Lake Roosevelt into the feeder canal and
  thence to Banks Lake.

**This is the hinge fact for central Washington.** The Columbia Basin Project irrigates about **680,000 acres**
through **330 miles of main canals and roughly 2,000 miles of laterals**, supporting crops valued at around
$1.2 billion a year — and the water gets there because electric pumps lift it 280 feet uphill. **While
pumping, those twelve units draw about 600 MW** — the entire output of a mid-sized power station, spent on
lifting water. The project was
authorized for roughly **1,029,000-1,100,000 acres**; only about two-thirds was ever built. Completion into
the **Odessa subarea** (including the East High Canal) was judged economically infeasible in the 1980s and
never happened, which is why Odessa-area farms mine groundwater instead. So: the Columbia Basin Project is
**already** a half-finished system running partly on an aquifer that is being drawn down.

#### 3.1.3 British Columbia

BC Hydro operates **about 30 hydroelectric generating plants totaling roughly 9,716 MW** (per BC Hydro's own
facilities page; **[SOURCES DISAGREE]** slightly with other totals depending on whether thermal and purchased
resources are included). The giants:

- **G.M. Shrum Generating Station** at the **W.A.C. Bennett Dam** on the Peace River — **2,907 MW**, ten
  units, about 15 TWh a year. Williston Lake behind it is the largest reservoir in BC by a wide margin.
- **Mica** — 2,805 MW, six units, ~7,200 GWh/yr (Columbia).
- **Revelstoke** — five units, over 2,400 MW, with a sixth 500 MW unit under construction (Columbia).
- **Site C / John Horgan Dam** on the Peace — six units, roughly **1,100-1,230 MW**, about 5,100 GWh a year.
  **[SOURCES DISAGREE]** on the capacity figure; also note the dam was **renamed the John Horgan Dam**, so
  older sources say "Site C." It flooded a substantial stretch of Peace Valley bottomland that was among the
  only Class 1-2 arable land in northern BC — a real and bitterly contested trade of farmland for firm power.
- **Kemano Generating Station** — **896 MW**, an underground powerhouse fed by the **Nechako Reservoir**
  through a tunnel bored through the Coast Mountains, with an 80 km transmission line to **Rio Tinto's
  aluminum smelter at Kitimat**. This is privately owned generation built for one industrial customer. Rio
  Tinto completed a second 16 km tunnel in 2022 to secure the water conveyance.

Hydro supplied roughly **92% of BC's electricity** as of the early 2010s (EIA, citing Canadian data); the
share remains dominant.

#### 3.1.4 Non-federal US hydro worth naming

- **Boundary Dam** on the Pend Oreille River, **Seattle City Light** — about **1,117 MW**, City Light's
  largest single resource. (An older 1,003 MW figure circulates; the higher number reflects uprates.)
- **Skagit River Hydroelectric Project** (Ross, Diablo, Gorge) — Seattle City Light, in the North Cascades.
- **Hells Canyon Complex** (Brownlee, Oxbow, Hells Canyon) on the Snake, **Idaho Power** — **1,167 MW
  combined**, and it sits on the Oregon-Idaho border, so part of it is arguably in-territory. It blocks
  anadromous fish above Hells Canyon Dam.
- Numerous Willamette Valley USACE projects (Detroit, Lookout Point, Green Peter, Cougar) — individually
  modest, collectively useful, and primarily flood-control structures.

#### 3.1.5 The Columbia River Treaty — a cross-border dependency

The 1964 Columbia River Treaty is the arrangement under which Canada built storage (Mica, Duncan, Keenleyside)
to regulate flows for US flood control and power generation, in exchange for the "Canadian Entitlement," a
share of the downstream power benefit.

- A **modernization agreement-in-principle was announced 11 July 2024**; interim measures were implemented in
  **November 2024**. As of this research it is an agreement in principle, **not** a ratified treaty amendment
  — the author should check the current status, because this is politically live.
- The **Canadian Entitlement dropped 37% on 1 August 2024** and is on a glide path to stabilize in 2033-34 at
  **550 MW of capacity and 225 average MW of energy**, running to 2044.
- The US is to retain access to **3.6 million acre-feet** of pre-planned flood storage behind **Keenleyside
  Dam**, compensating Canada at about **$37.6 million a year** (inflation-indexed) from spring 2025 through
  2044, plus a further **$16.6 million a year** once the new agreement enters force.

**Why the author should care:** this is a documented, real-world case of the upstream party (British Columbia)
holding physical control of the water that makes the downstream party's (Washington/Oregon) power and flood
safety work, mediated entirely by a negotiated paper instrument. Remove the paper and the relationship becomes
raw leverage. In a post-collapse Cascadia that has annexed both ends of this system, the internal politics of
who operates Mica and Keenleyside — and for whose benefit — is a ready-made source of conflict between the
northern interior and the southern lowlands.

#### 3.1.6 Transmission

- **Bonneville Power Administration** owns **more than 15,000 circuit miles** of high-voltage line and
  controls roughly **75% of the region's 230 kV-and-above transmission capacity**. It markets power from
  **31 federal dams** plus one nuclear plant, supplies about **28%** of Pacific Northwest electricity, and has
  a sustained peak capability around 11,680 MW, roughly 87% of it hydro.
- **The Pacific DC Intertie** — a ±500 kV HVDC line running about **800 miles** from the **Celilo Converter
  Station** near The Dalles, Oregon, to Sylmar in Los Angeles. **Path 66** adds three 500 kV AC lines to
  California. These are what let Northwest hydro and California thermal/solar trade seasonal surpluses.
- **HVDC is worth flagging specifically for the post-collapse section.** Converter stations are
  high-technology installations full of thyristor or IGBT valves, transformers, and control electronics. They
  are much harder to rebuild than an AC line. An AC transmission line is, at bottom, wire on towers; an HVDC
  terminal is a semiconductor factory's output.

### 3.4 California energy

California is the pivot where the territory stops being hydro-dominant and starts being
combustion-and-sunlight dominant.

**Oil.** California was historically a top-three US oil state; production peaked around **424 million barrels
a year in the mid-1980s** and has declined steadily since. By 2012 it was about 197 million barrels (8.3% of
US output, third nationally); it has fallen further since. **As of 2022 California produced less than 25% of
the crude it consumed**, importing the balance — in recent years principally from Ecuador, Saudi Arabia, Iraq,
and Colombia, plus Alaska North Slope crude by tanker.

The four "super-giant" fields, all in the **southern San Joaquin Valley, overwhelmingly in Kern County**:

| Field | Approximate cumulative production |
|---|---|
| Midway-Sunset | ~3.5 billion bbl |
| Elk Hills | ~2.2 billion bbl |
| South Belridge | ~2.1 billion bbl |
| Kern River | ~2.6 billion bbl |

Plus **Wilmington** under Long Beach, **Coalinga**, **Cymric**, **Lost Hills**, **Huntington Beach**, and
**Ventura**. Kern County alone peaked at 256 million barrels in 1985.

**Critical technical caveat:** most remaining San Joaquin oil is **heavy crude produced by cyclic steam
injection and steamflood** — you burn natural gas to raise steam, inject it to thin the oil, and pump it out.
That is an energy-intensive, capital-intensive, continuously-maintained process. **Heavy oil does not flow out
of the ground on its own.** A collapse that stops the steam generators effectively stops these fields, and
restarting a steamflood after decades of cooling is not trivial. Light oil from Ventura and the LA Basin is a
different and easier case, but the volumes are far smaller.

**The Monterey Shale is a cautionary tale worth using.** Early promotional estimates ran to hundreds of
billions of barrels; the **EIA's 2014 revision cut recoverable unconventional tight oil to a tiny fraction**
of the original claim (the figure quoted in the source consulted here is dramatically lower — **[SOURCES
DISAGREE]**, and the "21 million barrels" number the article gives looks like a transcription of a much larger
EIA figure; the author should check EIA directly rather than trust either number). The structural point holds:
California's shale is folded and faulted rather than flat-lying, so horizontal drilling and fracturing do not
work the way they do in Texas or North Dakota.

**Natural gas.** California produces very little and **imports roughly 85% of its gas** through six major
pipelines from the Rockies, the Southwest, and Canada. For a self-contained Cascadia, this matters enormously:
California's gas comes from **outside the territory**, but **British Columbia's Montney gas is inside it** —
so a unified Cascadia is, on paper, gas-sufficient, provided the pipelines from northeast BC to California
exist and are maintained. That is a very long pipeline dependency running the whole length of the country.

**Refining.** California has a substantial refinery complex concentrated in the **San Francisco Bay Area
(Richmond, Martinez, Benicia, Rodeo)** and the **Los Angeles Basin (Carson, Torrance, Wilmington, El
Segundo)**. **[UNVERIFIED]** current refinery count and aggregate capacity in barrels per day — the EIA state
profile pages could not be parsed in this session, and several California refineries have announced closures
or conversions to renewable diesel recently, so any number is a moving target. Check
eia.gov/dnav/pet/pet_pnp_cap1_dcu_SCA_a.htm. **Since resolved:** A.6 of `Cascadia - Industry, Ports and
Transport (supplement).md` carries the California Energy Commission's refinery list — **11 operating
refineries, 1,338,171 b/d**, data current 8 July 2026 — plus Washington, British Columbia, and the fact that
Oregon has none.

**Nuclear.** **Diablo Canyon**, San Luis Obispo County, PG&E — two Westinghouse PWRs, **2,256 MW** combined
(Unit 1 1,138 MW, Unit 2 1,118 MW). Roughly **8.6% of California's generation** and about 23% of its
carbon-free generation. Originally slated to close 2024-25; **SB 846 (2022) extended operation through 2030**.
It is **the only operating nuclear plant in California** following the permanent shutdown of **San Onofre in
2013** (Rancho Seco closed in 1989).

**Geothermal.** **The Geysers**, in the Mayacamas Mountains about 72 miles north of San Francisco, is **the
largest developed geothermal field in the world** — 18 plants, **1,590 MW nameplate / 1,517 MW active**,
roughly **6,516 GWh a year** at about a 53% capacity factor, drawing from over 350 active wells, mostly
operated by Calpine. A detail the author will like: the steam field was being depleted, and it is now
sustained by **injecting roughly 20 million gallons a day of treated municipal wastewater**, including 11
million gpd piped 42 miles from Santa Rosa. **The largest geothermal complex on Earth runs on sewage.** The
Imperial Valley's Salton Sea geothermal field is the other major California resource, and is associated with
lithium-rich brines.

**Solar and wind** are large and growing in California, concentrated in the Mojave, the Imperial Valley, the
southern San Joaquin, and the Tehachapi and Altamont passes. **[UNVERIFIED]** current installed capacities.

### 3.5 Baja California peninsula energy — two islanded grids

Three facts here, and all three are strategically interesting.

**1. Cerro Prieto.** South of Mexicali, operated by the **Comisión Federal de Electricidad**, **820 MW
installed** across five stations (CP1 180 MW, CP2 220 MW, CP3 220 MW, CP4 100 MW, CP5 100 MW), built
progressively from 1973. It is described as **the world's largest geothermal power complex by overall size and
second-largest by output**. It sits in the **Salton Trough** on the Cerro Prieto fault — the same
continental-rift geology that produces the Salton Sea field on the US side. It supplies roughly **80% of Baja
California state's electricity** and exports surplus into California.

Geothermal deserves a flag in the post-collapse section for a reason that is easy to miss: **it is the one
large-scale, high-capacity-factor, weather-independent generating resource in the territory that does not
require a fuel supply chain and does not require a dam.** It needs wells, turbines, and maintenance. The heat
is simply there.

**2. The Baja California grid is not part of Mexico's grid — it is part of California's.** CFE's transmission
system in northern Baja California is **within the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC)**,
synchronized with the US Western Interconnection rather than with Mexico's **Sistema Interconectado Nacional
(SIN)**. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether a SIN interconnection has since been completed — the source consulted
described studies recommending it with an expected commissioning of 2013, which may or may not have happened.
For the author's purposes the historical and probably current position is the useful one: **electrically,
Baja California already belongs to the Cascadian side of the border and not to Mexico.** That is a remarkably
convenient real-world fact for the premise.

**3. Baja California Sur is an isolated island grid.** It is not connected to the peninsula's northern system
and not connected to the SIN; it runs on its own generation, historically fuel-oil and diesel. **[UNVERIFIED]**
from a primary source in this session — confirm at cenace.gob.mx or cfe.mx — but it is consistent with
everything else about the peninsula's geography. If true, and it almost certainly is, it means the southern
tip of this country is energetically on its own.

Other peninsular generation: thermal plants at **Rosarito** and the **Presidente Juárez** complex, **wind at
La Rumorosa** in the Sierra de Juárez, and scattered solar. **[UNVERIFIED]** capacities.

### 3.6 Alaska Panhandle energy — a chain of electrical islands

Southeast Alaska is hydro-rich relative to its tiny population, and **not connected to any continental power
grid**. Each community, or small cluster of communities, is its own electrical island. This is unusual enough
that it deserves to be stated flatly: **you cannot get a kilowatt from Vancouver to Juneau.**

- **Snettisham**, 28 miles south of Juneau — **78 MW**, in service since 1973, supplying about **65% of Alaska
  Electric Light & Power's** energy. It uses a subsurface lake intake rather than a conventional dam. The
  powerhouse is reachable only by boat or floatplane, and it reaches Juneau by a **44-mile transmission
  line**. That line has been severed by avalanches, dropping Juneau onto diesel backup at brutal cost — a
  precise, real-world illustration of how a single corridor failure isolates a capital city.
- **Swan Lake** and **Lake Tyee**, linked by the **Swan-Tyee Intertie**, serve Ketchikan.
- **Blue Lake** serves Sitka; **Green Lake**, **Long Lake**, and others serve smaller communities.

**[UNVERIFIED]** individual capacities other than Snettisham. The Alaska Energy Authority
(akenergyauthority.org) publishes them.

### 4.4 California industry

California's economy is enormous and, for the author's purposes, **misleading**. Gross state product was about
**$4.05 trillion in 2024** (rising toward $4.3 trillion in 2025 estimates), which would make it roughly the
**fourth-largest economy in the world** if independent. But look at what that output actually is:

| Sector | Approximate 2025 GDP contribution |
|---|---|
| Information (tech + entertainment) | $538 billion |
| Real estate | $460 billion |
| Professional services | $391 billion |

Largest employment sectors: government (~2.7 million), healthcare (~2.6 million), retail (~2.5 million),
professional services (~2.1 million).

**None of the top three output sectors produces a physical good.** Information, real estate, and professional
services are, in post-collapse terms, close to zero. Silicon Valley's value is in design, software, and
intellectual property — and its hardware is fabricated elsewhere. Hollywood's value is in distribution to a
world market. Real estate value is a claim on future rents in a functioning economy.

What California actually **makes**:

- **Semiconductor design and some fabrication.** Design is concentrated in Santa Clara County; leading-edge
  fabrication has largely left the state. **[UNVERIFIED]** — the author should not assume a leading-edge fab
  survives in California. Even if one did, a modern fab needs ultrapure water, uninterrupted power measured in
  tens of megawatts, and a global supply chain of photoresists, gases, and lithography equipment that no
  single country possesses.
- **Aerospace and defense.** A deep historical cluster in Southern California (Palmdale, El Segundo, Long
  Beach, Redondo Beach) — airframes, satellites, missiles, rocket engines. **[UNVERIFIED]** current
  employment and output figures.
- **Food processing** — an enormous canning, freezing, drying, milling, and winemaking industry attached to
  Central Valley agriculture. This is the most **post-collapse-relevant** manufacturing in the state, because
  the inputs are local and the technology is 19th- and 20th-century.
- **Petroleum refining and petrochemicals** in the Bay Area and LA Basin.
- **Shipbuilding** is much diminished; NASSCO at San Diego is the surviving major yard. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- **Cement, aggregate, and steel fabrication.** **Kaiser Steel at Fontana** deserves a paragraph of its own,
  because its whole history is a preview of the author's problem. Henry Kaiser founded it on 1 December 1941;
  the first blast furnace, "Big Bess," was lit on 30 December 1942, built inland specifically because a
  wartime coastal steelworks was thought too exposed to attack. By 1953 it reached roughly **2.3 million tons
  a year**. Iron ore came first from the Vulcan Mine near Kelso and then from Kaiser's own **Eagle Mountain**
  mine; **metallurgical coal had to come from the Sunnyside mine in Utah**, which Kaiser bought outright in
  1950, because — in the words of the source — there were no usable deposits in Southern California, Arizona,
  or Nevada. **The mill closed in December 1983, and in that same year the still-modern steelmaking equipment
  was sold to China's Shougang for $15 million and shipped across the Pacific.**

  Kaiser and Geneva Steel (a US Steel plant in Utah) between them had captured most of the Pacific Coast
  steel market by the 1960s. Both are gone. **[SOURCES DISAGREE]** slightly with the common claim that Kaiser
  was the *only* integrated mill on the West Coast — it was the dominant one, and the only one in California,
  but smaller works existed historically. The practical conclusion holds and should be flagged hard: **there
  is effectively no integrated iron-and-steel making capacity on the Pacific coast of North America today.**
  Everything is electric-arc minimill scrap recycling or imported steel. Note also the geographic lesson
  embedded in Kaiser's supply chain — **California has iron ore but not coking coal**, and Cascadia's coking
  coal is 1,500 miles north in the Elk Valley of British Columbia. Putting those two together is a
  nation-scale logistics problem and therefore a nation-scale political one.

### 4.5 Baja California peninsula industry — maquiladoras, which is to say, nothing durable

**Tijuana, Mexicali, and Tecate** host one of the largest **maquiladora** clusters in Mexico. Baja California
state has over 900 companies operating under federal export-manufacturing programs. **Mexicali alone had
about 180 maquiladoras employing roughly 70,000 people** as of December 2018, in automotive, aerospace,
telecommunications, and electronics. Tijuana is a major center for televisions and display assembly, medical
devices, and electronics.

**The author should treat this sector as evaporating completely in a collapse scenario, and should understand
exactly why.** A maquiladora is by definition a tariff-advantaged **assembly** operation: components are
imported, labor is applied, and the finished good is exported. The plants hold no upstream capability — no
foundries, no polymer plants, no semiconductor fabrication, no machine-tool industry. Remove either the
component supply or the export market and the buildings are just buildings. What *does* survive is the
population that those plants concentrated in the desert border cities: **Tijuana at roughly 1.8 million
people, Mexicali at roughly 854,000 (city) / 1.2 million (metro)**, all of them drinking pumped Colorado River
water and working in an industry that only exists because a border runs through it.

That combination — a very large, very thirsty population with no local water, no local food base, and an
industry that cannot function without global trade — is the single worst demographic problem in the entire
territory. It is worth making it a plot problem.

**Smaller but more durable industry** on the peninsula: fish processing at Ensenada, wineries in the Guadalupe
Valley, salt at Guerrero Negro, gypsum at Isla San Marcos, and the Baja Studios film facility at Ensenada.

### 4.6 Alaska Panhandle industry

Fish processing (Ketchikan, Petersburg, Sitka), mining (Greens Creek, Kensington), state and federal
government employment concentrated in Juneau as the state capital, and tourism — overwhelmingly **cruise
ships**, which is a pure discretionary-global-economy industry and would disappear entirely.

**Timber and pulp: a completed collapse the author can study directly.** The **Tongass National Forest** is
the largest national forest in the United States at **16.7 million acres** — bigger than ten states — carrying
Sitka spruce, western hemlock, western red cedar, and yellow cedar, some of the finest softwood timber in the
world. Industrial logging began in the 1950s under two 50-year contracts with the **Ketchikan Pulp Company**
and the **Alaska Pulp Corporation** at Sitka. By the mid-1990s over half the old-growth on Prince of Wales
Island had been cut. **Both pulp mills closed — Alaska Pulp's Sitka mill in 1993 and Ketchikan Pulp's in
1997** — and the regional timber industry has never recovered. The Forest Service is reported to have lost
over a billion dollars on Tongass timber sales since 1980 because the cost of building roads into that terrain
exceeds what the logs are worth. The 2001 **Roadless Rule** was restored in January 2023.

**The lesson is precise and useful:** the timber is still standing. The trees did not go anywhere. What
vanished was the **capital-intensive processing and the export market**. A reconstruction-era Cascadia would
find the Panhandle's forest wealth entirely intact and entirely inaccessible at any scale, because getting it
out requires roads through mountains, and milling it requires plants that no longer exist.

### 5.5 California ports and corridors

**San Pedro Bay — Los Angeles and Long Beach.** The **Port of Los Angeles** handled about **10.2 million TEU
in 2025** and around **216 million metric revenue tons** of cargo in 2024, across 7,500 acres of land and
water with 43 miles of waterfront, 25 cargo terminals, eight container terminals, 82 container cranes, and
113 miles of on-dock rail. It is **the busiest container port in the United States** and about the 19th
busiest in the world; combined with the adjacent **Port of Long Beach**, the San Pedro Bay complex ranks
roughly **10th busiest worldwide**. California as a whole is described as handling **about 40% of US
containerized imports**.

The complex is connected inland by the **Alameda Corridor**, a 20-mile grade-separated freight rail trench
running north from the harbor to the downtown Los Angeles rail yards, from which BNSF and Union Pacific reach
the rest of the continent — crucially, eastward over **Cajon Pass** and **Tehachapi Pass**, both of which lie
inside California but point out of the territory.

**Port of Oakland** — the Bay Area's container port, and the principal **export** gateway for Central Valley
agricultural products. **[UNVERIFIED]** current TEU throughput; the Wikipedia article consulted gave only an
operating revenue figure ($409 million, 2023). Check portofoakland.com for the cargo statistics.

**Other California ports:** San Diego (cruise, vehicles, and the US Navy's principal Pacific surface fleet
base), Port Hueneme (vehicles and produce, plus Naval Base Ventura County), Richmond and Benicia (petroleum
and bulk), Stockton and Sacramento (deepwater channels dredged inland off the Delta — these are genuinely
interesting for the author, because they put ocean-capable berths in the middle of the Central Valley).

**The southern land boundary.** I-5 crosses from Oregon into California over **Siskiyou Summit** and then runs
the full length of the state. Inland, the **Tehachapi Mountains** separate the San Joaquin Valley from the
Mojave and the Los Angeles Basin, crossed by **Tejon Pass (the Grapevine, I-5)** and the **Tehachapi Loop**
rail crossing. **Everything Southern California eats, drinks, and burns crosses one of those two passes or
comes by sea.**

### 5.6 The Baja peninsula — one road, no railroad

This is the most fragile transport situation in the entire territory, and it is worth stating starkly.

- **Mexican Federal Highway 1, the Benito Juárez Transpeninsular Highway**, runs **1,711 km (1,063 miles)**
  from Tijuana to Cabo San Lucas. It was **completed only in 1973**. South of Ensenada it is, for most of its
  length, **a two-lane rural road** with no shoulder, crossing open desert and the Sierra de la Giganta. A
  toll alternative (Fed. 1D) exists only on the short Tijuana-Ensenada coastal stretch.
- **There is no railroad down the peninsula.** The only rail is the short **Tijuana-Tecate** line in the far
  north. Confirmed by absence in every source consulted; **[UNVERIFIED]** only in the sense that no source
  explicitly says "there is no railroad," but there demonstrably is not one.
- **Ports:** Ensenada (containers, cruise, and Mexico's main Pacific fishing port), La Paz/Pichilingue (the
  ferry terminal), Cabo San Lucas (cruise), and the Guerrero Negro salt works' barge link to the deepwater
  transshipment anchorage at **Isla de Cedros**.
- **Ferries across the Gulf of California** connect La Paz/Pichilingue to Mazatlán and Topolobampo on the
  mainland — which, on the author's map, means they connect Cascadia to a foreign country.

**Consequence:** the southern 1,000 miles of this nation hang off a single two-lane road and a handful of
small harbors. Supplying Los Cabos and La Paz overland from Tijuana is a multi-day drive across desert. In a
post-collapse world, **Baja California Sur is functionally an island** and will be supplied by sea or not at
all.

### 5.7 The Alaska Panhandle — no roads at all

Southeast Alaska's communities are, with three exceptions, **unreachable by road from anywhere on Earth**.
Juneau is the only US state capital with no road connection to the outside. The exceptions:

- **Skagway**, via the **Klondike Highway** to Whitehorse, Yukon.
- **Haines**, via the **Haines Highway** to Haines Junction, Yukon.
- **Hyder**, a tiny settlement connected to Stewart, BC.

Everything else — Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan, Petersburg, Wrangell, the whole Alexander Archipelago — moves by
**water or air**. The backbone is the **Alaska Marine Highway System**, the state-operated ferry network, plus
floatplanes and small aircraft.

The only railroad is the **White Pass and Yukon Route**, a 3-foot narrow-gauge line from **Skagway** over the
White Pass toward **Whitehorse**, built for the Klondike rush and now operated as a tourist railway.
**[UNVERIFIED]** how much of the line north of Fraser/Bennett is currently operable; it has been restored in
stages. For a reconstruction-era Cascadia, this is potentially the only rail link from tidewater into the
northern interior, and that makes an obscure tourist railway strategically interesting.

**The Inside Passage** is the region's real highway: a sheltered, largely ice-free channel running from Puget
Sound and the Strait of Georgia north through the BC coast and the Alexander Archipelago to Skagway. A vessel
can run nearly the whole length of the Cascadian north without entering open ocean. **Whoever holds the Inside
Passage holds the north.**

## 7. Post-Collapse Assessment

This is the analytical section. Everything above is what exists in 2026. This is what would still be there
after a global nuclear exchange in 2083 and 150-250 years of reconstruction.

The organizing principle is simple and the author should apply it ruthlessly: **ask what a thing is made of,
and who has to keep showing up for it to keep working.**

---

### 7.1 Tier A — Survives regardless: geology, soil, climate, water in its natural channels

These things do not care about the war. They are the permanent endowment, and they define the ceiling of what
any Cascadia can ever be.

**Geology.** The ore bodies are still in the ground and will be for geological time. In particular:

- **Coal** in the Elk Valley of southeastern BC and in northeast BC — metallurgical coal, which is to say
  **the reducing agent for making iron**. This is arguably the single most important Tier A asset in the
  territory, because without a carbon reductant there is no steel, and without steel there is no industrial
  recovery. Coal at shallow depth can be mined with hand tools, horses, and inclined planes. It was, for two
  centuries.
- **Copper** across the BC interior (Highland Valley, Gibraltar, Copper Mountain, Red Chris) and the
  Washington/Oregon Cascades — copper is the metal of electrification and is recoverable at modest scale by
  pre-industrial methods.
- **Silver, lead, zinc, gold** at Greens Creek and across the BC/Washington interior belts. Lead and zinc
  matter more than gold for a rebuilding economy: lead for batteries and solder, zinc for galvanizing.
- **Borates at Boron, California.** Nearly half the world's refined borate supply sits in one open pit in the
  Mojave. Borosilicate glass, glazes, fluxes, wood preservative, neutron absorbers.
- **Rare earths at Mountain Pass, California** and **Bokan Mountain, Alaska**. The ore is Tier A; the
  *separation* is not (see Tier C).
- **Phosphate at San Juan de la Costa, BCS** — the only obvious domestic source of the one fertilizer nutrient
  that must be mined rather than synthesized.
- **Salt at Guerrero Negro** and on San Francisco Bay — solar evaporation needs sun, flat ground, and
  seawater. Nothing else.
- **Limestone, gypsum, aggregate, clay** — the inputs to cement, plaster, mortar, and brick, distributed
  throughout the territory. Cement can be made in a vertical shaft kiln with coal or wood. This is how you
  rebuild.
- **Geothermal heat** at The Geysers, the Salton Sea, Cerro Prieto, and along the Cascade arc. The heat does
  not deplete on human timescales and does not depend on weather.

**Soil and climate.** These are the agricultural Tier A assets, and they are more valuable than most of the
mines:

- **The Palouse** of southeastern Washington — deep wind-deposited loess, farmed as **dryland winter wheat
  with no irrigation at all.** This is the most important single agricultural fact in the northern half of the
  territory. It does not need a pump, a canal, or a dam. It needs a plow, seed, and rain that arrives in the
  right season. It produced wheat before the dams and will produce wheat after them.
- **The Willamette Valley** — alluvial soil, reliable winter rain, mild maritime climate, long growing season.
  It fed the Oregon Trail migration without any irrigation infrastructure whatsoever, and it will do so again.
- **The Fraser Valley and the Puget lowland** — the same maritime pattern: wet, mild, fertile, and rainfed.
- **The Sacramento Valley and the eastern San Joaquin** — recall Section 2.4.2: California was the
  **second-largest wheat state in the Union by the 1890s with only about 12% of its farmland irrigated.** The
  Mediterranean climate puts the rain in the winter, which is when a winter cereal wants it. Dry-farmed grain,
  rangeland cattle, olives, figs, and riparian orchards along the rivers are all Tier A in California.
- **The coastal fog belt** from Monterey to Santa Maria — the climatic reason California dominates cool-season
  vegetables. Fog is free.
- **Pacific salmon runs, halibut, sablefish, crab, herring, and the Gulf of California's sardine and shrimp
  fisheries.** Protein that delivers itself. The single most reliable calorie source in the territory and the
  one that needs the least capital.
- **The forests** — Douglas fir, Sitka spruce, western hemlock, western red cedar, coast redwood. Among the
  most productive timber-growing ground on Earth, and it regrows. Note that the *trees* are Tier A but the
  *access roads and mills* are not.

**Water in its natural channels.** The Columbia, Fraser, Skeena, Sacramento, San Joaquin, Klamath, and
Willamette will keep flowing. The Sierra and Cascade snowpacks will keep accumulating and melting. Gravity
still works. Anything that moves water **downhill** is Tier A-adjacent; anything that moves it **uphill** is
Tier B at best.

**Harbors.** Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia, Burrard Inlet, the Columbia estuary, San Francisco Bay, San
Diego Bay, and the fjords of the Inside Passage are geological facts. **Deep, sheltered, ice-free anchorage is
Tier A. Container cranes are not.**

### 7.2 Tier B — Survives only if maintained or repaired: the built infrastructure

This is where the author's real drama lives, because Tier B is the contested middle — things that are
enormously valuable, clearly worth fighting over, and slowly falling apart.

#### 7.2.1 The dams — the central case

The hydroelectric system is the crown jewel and the ticking clock at the same time. Treat it carefully,
because the naive assumptions in both directions are wrong.

**What survives well:**

- **The concrete and earth is durable.** A well-built, well-monitored concrete gravity dam has a service life
  that "can easily reach 100 years," and that is a *maintenance* figure, not a structural expiry date — mass
  concrete gains strength for decades. Grand Coulee is ~12 million cubic yards of concrete. Nobody is going to
  remove it and it is not going to fall down on its own within the author's timeframe.
- **The water and the head are permanent.** The river still drops the same distance.
- **A low-tech dam is still a useful dam.** Even with every generator dead, a dam is a **water-storage
  structure, a flood-control structure, a road across the river, and a source of controllable head** that
  could drive mechanical mills directly. Pre-electric industry ran on water wheels and line shafts for
  centuries.

**What degrades and why:**

- **Turbine runners** typically need replacement on the order of **every 25 years** of service, and generators
  need rewinding on a similar cycle. A runner for a 600-805 MW Francis turbine is a multi-tonne precision
  stainless casting several meters across, dynamically balanced. **Casting and machining one requires a heavy
  foundry, large machine tools, and metallurgical control that a reconstruction economy would take generations
  to rebuild** — and there is, as noted, no integrated steelmaking on the Pacific coast even today.
- **Generator windings** need copper conductor, insulation systems, and rewinding capability.
- **Excitation, protection, and control systems** are electronics. They fail and they are not repairable
  without a semiconductor industry.
- **Gates, hoists, penstock valves, and bulkheads** are large steel mechanisms needing machining and hydraulics.
- **Sedimentation** is a slow problem but a real one. The consensus in the dam-engineering literature is that
  sediment does not usually threaten the *structure* so long as bottom outlets and spillway gates can still be
  operated — but it **fills storage, blocks intakes, and abrades turbine runners and wicket gates rapidly**.
  Over 150-250 years of unmanaged accumulation, storage capacity degrades meaningfully, and abrasive sediment
  passing through turbines destroys them faster than clean water does.
- **Spillways are the real safety issue, and there is a perfect case study.** In February 2017, at
  **Oroville — the tallest dam in the United States**, under full modern management with engineers, money,
  and helicopters available — the concrete main spillway opened a crater that grew from 250 to 300 feet
  during test flows. Operators throttled releases, the lake rose, and on 11 February water went over the
  **never-before-used earthen emergency spillway**, which eroded far faster than predicted and threatened to
  undermine the concrete weir. **Approximately 188,000 people were evacuated** from Butte, Yuba, and Sutter
  counties on 12-13 February. Repairs took over two million work hours and ran to 2019. The root cause is the
  detail the author should keep: in 2005 environmental groups had asked for the emergency spillway to be
  concrete-lined, at a cost of over $100 million, and regulators declined, relying on outdated geological
  surveys that mischaracterized the hillside.

  **That is deferred maintenance failing in a rich, competent, fully staffed society in peacetime.** Multiply
  the deferral by two centuries and the author has their answer about which dams are still standing.

**The realistic 150-250-year picture, and this is the useful answer:**

A reconstruction-era Cascadia would almost certainly have **some** hydroelectricity, at **a small fraction of
2026 capacity**, concentrated at a few sites that a surviving polity chose to keep alive. The pattern would
look like:

- **Small and old units are easier to keep running than large modern ones.** A 125 MW 1940s Grand Coulee
  Left-Powerhouse machine, or a Rock Island unit, is mechanically simpler and more repairable than an 805 MW
  Third Power Plant unit with modern electronics.
- **Run-of-river plants on the mid-Columbia — Rock Island, Rocky Reach, Wanapum, Priest Rapids, Wells — are
  good candidates**, because they have modest head, relatively accessible powerhouses, and no enormous
  storage structure to police.
- **The dams closest to population and industry survive; remote ones are abandoned.** Bonneville and The
  Dalles are an hour from Portland. Mica is in the Columbia Mountains.
- **Grand Coulee is a special case in both directions.** It is the biggest prize and the biggest liability:
  6,809 MW if you could run it, a 550-foot structure with a million-cfs spillway to maintain if you cannot.
  Expect a reconstruction polity to fight over it, garrison it, and run a handful of its twenty-odd units.

**The pumps are a separate and harsher question.** Even a Grand Coulee running a few units could, in
principle, power the **John W. Keys III** pump-generators and keep some water flowing to Banks Lake. But those
pumps are 65,000-70,000 hp machines; the canal system is 330 miles of main canal and 2,000 miles of laterals
that silt, slump, and grow vegetation; and the siphons and check structures are concrete that cracks. **A
partial Columbia Basin Project is plausible. The full 680,000 acres is not.** Expect a fraction — the acreage
closest to Banks Lake, on the gravity-fed reaches, with the far ends of the system abandoned first.

#### 7.2.2 Other Tier B infrastructure, ranked by how likely it is to survive

**Most likely to be kept working:**

1. **Harbor quays, breakwaters, and dry docks.** Masonry and concrete, repairable with local materials.
   Bremerton's and Esquimalt's graving docks are potentially the most valuable single industrial facilities in
   the territory, because they let you repair ships — and ships are how this country holds together.
2. **Canals and irrigation ditches fed by gravity.** These are the oldest technology in the file and the
   easiest to maintain with hand labor. **Every gravity-fed district survives; every pumped district is in
   question.** In practice this favors the Okanagan, the Yakima Valley, the Willamette, the Umatilla, the
   Friant-Kern service area, and the east-side San Joaquin over the west-side San Joaquin and the Columbia
   Basin Project.
3. **Rail grades and tunnels.** The **alignment** — the cuts, fills, grades, bridge abutments, and bores — is
   the expensive part and it is essentially permanent. The **Cascade Tunnel** through Stevens Pass and the
   **Connaught/Mount Macdonald** tunnels under Rogers Pass will still be holes through mountains in 2300.
   Relaying track is comparatively easy; **boring a new tunnel is not.** Expect rail to come back, slowly,
   along the exact alignments that exist now, quite possibly with different gauge and certainly with different
   motive power.
4. **Roads as roadbeds.** Asphalt fails in 20-30 years unmaintained; the graded, drained, bridged right-of-way
   underneath it lasts far longer. Mountain highways fail first — washouts, slides, and avalanche chutes take
   the Coquihalla and Rogers Pass out quickly. Valley routes persist.
5. **Locks on the Columbia and Snake.** Big, simple, concrete, and hugely valuable: they open **465 miles of
   inland navigation to Lewiston, Idaho**. A lock can be operated by hand if you have to. Expect these to be
   contested and, where a polity can manage it, kept.
6. **Low-head and small hydro.** Dozens of small plants, mill dams, and municipal systems. These are the
   realistic backbone of a reconstruction grid — a few megawatts here and there, locally maintained.
7. **Geothermal wells and turbines.** Steam turbines are large, simple, repairable rotating machinery. If any
   thermal generation survives anywhere in this territory, bet on **The Geysers and Cerro Prieto** before
   anything that needs a fuel supply chain.

**Contested middle — repairable in principle, probably not in practice at scale:**

8. **High-voltage AC transmission.** Wire on towers is conceptually simple; the **transformers** are not. A
   large power transformer is a bespoke, multi-hundred-tonne object with a 12-24 month lead time even in a
   functioning global economy, and there are few places on Earth that make the largest ones. Expect local
   grids around surviving generation, not a regional interconnection. **The BPA backbone as a 15,000-circuit-
   mile system does not come back.**
9. **Refineries.** Distillation is old technology and a crude still is buildable. **Catalytic cracking,
   reforming, and hydrotreating are not** — they need catalysts, hydrogen plants, and alloy pressure vessels.
   Expect kerosene, fuel oil, lubricants, and asphalt from simple distillation of light crude; do not expect
   modern gasoline or diesel specifications, and do not expect aviation fuel.
10. **Pulp and paper mills.** Chemical pulping (kraft) needs a recovery boiler, causticizing, and sulfur
    chemistry. Mechanical pulping and simple soda/sulfite processes are more achievable. **Paper survives;
    modern paper industry does not.**
11. **Cement plants.** Rotary kilns are large but comprehensible. Cement is so valuable to reconstruction that
    this is one of the first heavy industries any recovering polity rebuilds. Tier B, and probably succeeds.
12. **Dry-docked naval and merchant hulls.** Steel hulls last a long time. Engines do not.

**Unlikely to survive in any form:**

13. **Pumped water conveyance over mountains.** See 7.3.
14. **HVDC converter stations** (Celilo). Semiconductors.
15. **Anything requiring a controlled atmosphere, high vacuum, or ultrapure inputs.**

### 7.3 Tier C — Vanishes: artifacts of global trade and modern supply chains

These do not degrade. They **stop**, more or less at once, and no amount of local effort brings them back
without a world economy to plug into.

**The Southern California water system.** This is the flagship Tier C item and the author should build plot
around it. The **State Water Project consumes about 11,500 GWh a year and is the largest single electricity
consumer in California**, and its centerpiece — the **Edmonston Pumping Plant** — lifts water **1,926 feet in
one stage over the Tehachapi Mountains**. There is no low-technology substitute for that. You cannot lift
water 1,926 feet with animals or wind or a water wheel at anything like the required volume. When the grid
goes, the lift goes, and the aqueduct south of the Tehachapis runs dry. The same logic applies, with
variations, to:

- The **Harvey O. Banks** and **Jones/Tracy** pumping plants in the Delta — the whole southbound export
  system, over **5 million acre-feet a year** serving **25 million people**, is pumped.
- The **Colorado River Aqueduct**, the **All-American Canal** service area, and the **Colorado
  River-Tijuana aqueduct** — all of which draw on a river outside the territory and, in Tijuana's case, lift
  it over a mountain range.
- The **Los Angeles Aqueduct is the great exception, and it is verified.** Both the original 1913 aqueduct
  and the Second Aqueduct (1970) move water **entirely by gravity** — 419 miles in total, falling from 3,760
  ft at Haiwee Reservoir through two power drops to 1,200 ft at Upper Van Norman, **generating electricity on
  the way down rather than consuming it**. First Aqueduct capacity 422 cfs, Second 290 cfs. This is Tier B,
  not Tier C. **It means a rump Los Angeles supplied by the Owens Valley on gravity flow is physically
  possible in a way that the modern twenty-million-person city is not** — a smaller city, on a line built in
  1913 with mule teams and hand tools, that pays for its own maintenance in hydroelectricity. That is an
  unusually good worldbuilding hook and it rests on verified facts.

**The Delta levee system** belongs here too, or at the boundary of B and C. Eleven hundred miles of earthen
levees holding back the tide around islands sunk 15-25 feet below sea level, maintained continuously by
reclamation districts with heavy equipment. Unmaintained, they fail island by island in the first big flood
or the first significant earthquake, and the Delta reverts to brackish tidal marsh. That is not necessarily a
disaster for a reconstruction Cascadia — a tidal marsh is a productive place — but it permanently ends
freshwater export to the south.

**The San Joaquin Valley's groundwater-irrigated acreage.** Deep well pumps lifting from hundreds of feet.
Tier C. The land does not vanish, but it reverts to what rain and rivers support.

**Almonds, pistachios, and the permanent-crop economy.** This is worth spelling out because it is
counterintuitive. A nut orchard is a **twenty-five-year capital investment that must be watered every single
year, including the years it produces nothing**. Miss two summers and the orchard is dead wood. California's
$5.66 billion almond crop and $2.05 billion pistachio crop are not "agriculture" in a resilience sense at all
— they are a financial product denominated in irrigation water and export demand. They go first, completely,
and they go within a season or two.

**The maquiladora sector.** Component imports in, finished goods out, nothing in between. Gone.

**Aerospace at Boeing scale.** A modern airliner is the assembly of hundreds of thousands of parts from a
global supplier base, certified to standards maintained by a global regulatory apparatus, made of alloys and
composites from specialized plants. The **factories** are Tier B — enormous, well-built halls with overhead
cranes, which any polity would repurpose in a heartbeat. The **product** is Tier C.

**Semiconductors and everything downstream of them.** Computing, modern telecommunications, HVDC, modern
control systems, modern medical imaging. The supply chain for a single leading-edge chip spans a dozen
countries and no nation on Earth is self-sufficient in it today. Assume the stock of surviving electronics
depletes and is never replenished at anything like modern sophistication. **This is the single most important
Tier C item for the texture of the author's world**, because it determines whether the reconstruction era
looks like 1900, 1930, or 1960.

**Aluminum smelting.** Flag this one, because the region's history already shows it. The Pacific Northwest
once ran roughly ten aluminum smelters on cheap BPA hydro; nearly all have closed. Rio Tinto's **Kitimat**
smelter runs on 896 MW from **Kemano** — and it smelts **imported alumina**, because there is no bauxite
anywhere in this territory. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether any US Pacific Northwest smelter is still operating (this
should be confirmed — see Section 4). Aluminum is therefore doubly Tier C: it needs a colossal, uninterrupted
electricity supply *and* an ore that does not exist here. A reconstruction Cascadia is a **copper and iron**
civilization, not an aluminum one — and copper it has, and iron ore it largely does not.

**Rare-earth separation.** The ore at Mountain Pass is Tier A. The solvent-extraction cascade that turns
bastnäsite concentrate into separated neodymium, praseodymium, and dysprosium is Tier C — it needs industrial
volumes of acids and bases, hundreds of mixer-settler stages, and precise process chemistry. Note that even in
2026, with a functioning world economy, **much of Mountain Pass's output was still being refined in China.**

**Nitrogen fertilizer.** Haber-Bosch ammonia synthesis needs high-pressure vessels, catalysts, and a hydrogen
source — normally steam methane reforming from natural gas. It is achievable for a serious industrial state
and impossible for a subsistence one. Its absence caps agricultural yields at roughly pre-1910 levels, which,
combined with the loss of irrigation, is the real ceiling on this country's population.

**Cruise tourism, container logistics, air travel, and the entire information economy.** Gone.

### 7.4 The synthesis — what Cascadia actually is, 200 years after

Pulling it together into a form the author can use directly:

**Cascadia after reconstruction is a maritime, temperate, resource-rich, north-heavy country with an
over-large and unsupportable south.**

- **The core is the wet coastal strip from the Fraser Valley to the Willamette Valley**, plus the Sacramento
  Valley. Rainfed agriculture, immense timber, abundant fish, good harbors, navigable rivers, moderate winter,
  and enough small hydro to run mills and a modest grid. **This region feeds and houses itself with no
  imported inputs whatsoever.** It is the only part of the territory that can say that.
- **The interior is a grain-and-minerals hinterland.** The Palouse produces dryland wheat without a single
  pump. The BC interior and the Kootenays produce coal, copper, lead, zinc, and silver. The Okanagan, Yakima,
  and Wenatchee valleys produce fruit on gravity-fed ditches. This region is genuinely productive but is
  connected to the coast only through the passes — which is exactly the west-east tension Woodard describes,
  now with a material basis: **the interior has the metal and the bread, the coast has the ports and the
  people, and the mountains are the toll booth.**
- **California is halved and inverted.** The Sacramento Valley and the eastern San Joaquin, watered by rivers
  running downhill off the Sierra, remain excellent farmland — a dry-farmed, river-irrigated, 19th-century
  Central Valley supporting a substantial population. The **west side of the San Joaquin, the Tulare Basin,
  the Imperial Valley, and essentially all of Southern California's agriculture revert to desert and
  rangeland**, with Tulare Lake returning as a seasonal inland sea. The Bay Area survives on Sierra gravity
  aqueducts and a superb harbor. **Los Angeles and San Diego are the great demographic catastrophe of this
  world** — twenty million people in a semi-desert supplied by pumped water from two directions, neither of
  which works. What remains is a much smaller coastal city on the Los Angeles River and the gravity flow of
  the old aqueduct, plus the harbor at San Pedro Bay, which is far too valuable to abandon.
- **Baja is an outpost chain.** One road, no rail, no water, one very large salt works, one very large
  geothermal field, phosphate, gypsum, copper, and superb fisheries. The Cape region and Guerrero Negro are
  reachable by sea and nothing else. Tijuana and Mexicali, as currently constituted, are not sustainable at
  anything like their present size.
- **The Panhandle is a fishery, a silver mine, and a sea lane.** Seventy thousand people now; fewer, probably,
  then. Its strategic value is entirely that it closes the **Inside Passage**, which is the sheltered northern
  half of the country's only real trunk route.

**The three things worth fighting over, in order:**

1. **The dams and the passes** — because they are the difference between a mechanical civilization and a
   muscle-powered one, and because whoever holds a pass holds the trade between the two halves of the country.
2. **The coal of the Elk Valley** — because metallurgical coal plus copper plus limestone is a recipe for
   restarting metalworking, and there is no substitute for it within the territory.
3. **The harbors, the dry docks, and the ships** — because in a country 2,500 miles long and 150 miles wide,
   sea power *is* sovereignty. A Cascadia that can build and repair ships is one country. A Cascadia that
   cannot is a dozen valleys that happen to share a flag.

**And the one recurring structural irony to build a politics on:** this territory's greatest engineering
achievements — Grand Coulee, the Columbia Basin Project, the California Aqueduct, the Edmonston lift, the
Colorado conveyances — were all built to **make dry land productive and to move water and power from where
they are to where the people are**. Every one of them is a bet that the machines will keep running. A
reconstruction Cascadia inherits the bet and cannot cover it. Its whole political history would plausibly be a
two-century argument about **which of the old works to keep alive, and who pays for it** — the coast, which
has the water and the people, or the interior, which has the dams and the mines.
