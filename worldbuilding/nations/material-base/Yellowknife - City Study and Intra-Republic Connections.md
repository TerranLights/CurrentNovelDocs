## The eight findings that matter

1. **There is no all-season road from Yellowknife west to the Yukon that stays in the country, and there
   never has been.** Yellowknife has exactly one road, **NWT Highway 3**, which meets **Highway 1**. From
   there the only choices are **south into ⚑ Alberta**, or west along Highway 1 and then **south on Highway 7
   into ⚑ British Columbia.** Highway 1's northward spur dead-ends at Wrigley. See Part 2.1.
2. **The all-season road from Yellowknife to Whitehorse crosses into Cascadia twice** — south over the 60th
   parallel onto the Northern Rockies stretch of the Alaska Highway, then north again on the Liard Highway.
   **~1,920 km / ~1,195 mi, of which ~650 km / ~405 mi is foreign.** This is settled by the author-supplied
   map reference, `../Alaska Republic - Highway Network (read from map reference).md`, and the present file
   independently confirms it road by road. See Part 2.1 and 2.4.
3. **The one route that never leaves the country is not a road.** Great Slave Lake → Mackenzie River → Inuvik
   → Dempster Highway → Whitehorse is **water plus highway**: roughly **3,060 km / 1,900 mi to cover 1,110 km
   / 690 mi of straight-line distance**, open about four months a year. **There is no highway up the Mackenzie
   Valley between Wrigley and Inuvik**, so this is a barge route in summer and, incompletely, a winter road in
   winter — never an overland connection. See Part 2.4.
4. **Yellowknife runs on its own hydroelectricity — about 36 MW of it, on two systems it owns, one about
   140 km out and one on the river at the edge of town.** This is the most important single fact about the city's post-collapse prospects and it is the thing
   that distinguishes Yellowknife from every other settlement of comparable size in the Republic. See Part 1.6.
5. **Yellowknife is not a barge port.** Every scheduled Mackenzie sailing loads at **Hay River**, 200 km across
   the lake and on the ⚑ Alberta rail line. Yellowknife sits on a navigable lake at the head of a navigable
   river and has no place in the freight system that uses them. See Part 2.3.
6. **The Mackenzie Valley Highway is being built right now — but ⚠️ SOURCES CONFLICT on how far the funded
   phase actually goes.** $405 million committed 8 September 2026, "project of national interest" designation
   in process, target completion **2032**, **34 bridges already standing** on the winter road route. **But the
   GNWT's own project page (fetched 2026-09-22) states the current phase runs only from Wrigley to Tulita and
   Norman Wells — approximately 320 km in three segments — and that "Phase 2 would extend the highway from
   Norman Wells to Inuvik."** That is ~800 km short of the Dempster, and Phase 2 has no stated funding or
   timeline. This file's six-segment description, including a Gwich'in Settlement Area portion ending 60 km
   north of Tsiigehtchic, appears to describe the **full corridor concept** rather than the funded phase. **The
   distinction is decisive** — only a highway reaching Inuvik closes the Republic's internal circuit. See
   Part 2.2 and the conflict note there.
7. **The city's drinking water arrives through an 8.5 km submarine pipeline under Yellowknife Bay, from a 1948
   pumphouse "in a state of failure," and the route exists specifically to avoid Giant Mine's arsenic.** See
   Part 1.5 and Part 3.
8. **Yellowknife has been designated a northern operational support hub by the Canadian military, with a
   procurement notice estimating work that "may exceed $5B" at that location alone.** Airfield, hangars,
   ammunition compounds, fuel facilities, warehouses. See Part 1.2 and Part 3.

# PART 1 — YELLOWKNIFE AS A CITY

## 1.1 Founding, and why the city is where it is

**Yellowknife is on the north shore of Great Slave Lake, about 400 km (250 mi) south of the Arctic Circle, on
the west side of Yellowknife Bay near the outlet of the Yellowknife River**, at roughly 62°27′ N, 114°26′ W,
elevation 205.7 m (675 ft). It is the capital, the largest community, and **the only city** in the Northwest
Territories. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowknife

**The city is where it is because of gold, and specifically because of where the gold was.** The sequence:

- **1898:** a Klondike-bound prospector, E.A. Blakeney, found gold in the Yellowknife Bay area. Nobody cared —
  the Klondike was on, and Great Slave Lake was too far from anywhere.
- **Early 1930s:** uranium and silver at Great Bear Lake pulled prospectors north; aircraft made the country
  reachable for the first time. In **1933** Herb Dixon and Johnny Baker canoed down the Yellowknife River from
  Great Bear Lake and found gold at Quyta Lake, ~30 km (19 mi) upriver.
- **1934:** gold found on the **east** side of Yellowknife Bay; the short-lived Burwash Mine. **1935:**
  government geologists found gold in better geology on the **west** side, and a small staking rush followed.
  **The west side is where the city is.**
- **September 1935:** Consolidated Mining and Smelting (Cominco) staked the **Con** property — "Con" for
  "Consolidated." **Con Mine poured its first gold brick on 5 September 1938**, and that is the event that
  created the settlement. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Con_Mine
- **1938–1940:** the boom. Bank of Commerce, Hudson's Bay Company, the Wildcat Cafe (1937), Weaver & Devore.
  **Population 1,000 by 1940; five gold mines producing in the Yellowknife region by 1942.**
- **1943–1946:** Con closed and the town nearly died when the men left for the war.
- **1944:** exploration at the **Giant** property at the north end of town indicated a large deposit, and the
  post-war staking rush followed. **Giant produced from 1948.**

**The move from Old Town to New Town is a physical fact about the site, not an administrative one.** The
original settlement grew on the **Old Town waterfront** — the rocky peninsula and Latham Island at the north
end of Yellowknife Bay, which is where float planes land and where the bedrock comes to the surface. It ran
out of room. **The new townsite was laid out in 1945–1946** on the higher, flatter ground to the southwest,
and that is downtown Yellowknife today. Old Town survives as a neighborhood: the Wildcat Cafe, the Bush Pilots
Monument, Ragged Ass Road, and **a community of houseboats around Jolliffe Island in the bay, off-grid since
1978** and outside the city's tax jurisdiction, which has produced recurring lawsuits.

**Governance, in order:** administered **1939–1953 by the federal Northern Affairs department** through a
partly-elected, partly-appointed council; **incorporated as a municipality in 1953** with its own council and
town hall (first mayor Jock McNiven); and **named capital of the Northwest Territories in September 1967.**

**What it was before 1967 is the interesting part: the NWT had no capital inside the NWT.** The territory was
administered from **Ottawa** — a commissioner and a largely federal council sitting in the south. The 1967
move brought the seat of government physically into the territory for the first time and **triggered what
local history calls the third boom**, a wave of construction and new subdivisions to house government workers.
⚠️ **The pre-1967 administrative arrangement is stated here from the general historical record and the
Wikipedia account of federal administration 1939–1953; the precise constitutional history of where the NWT was
governed from before 1967 was not verified in detail in this pass.**

**Two later shocks are worth knowing.** On **1 April 1999 Nunavut separated**, and Yellowknife's writ shrank
by roughly two-thirds of the old territory's area. And on **16 August 2023 the entire city was evacuated** as
wildfire approached — the territorial government moved first because **Highway 3, the only road out, was about
to be cut.** By 19 August **87% of the city was gone**: 2,600 people left of 20,000, of whom 1,000 were
essential workers. The order was lifted 6 September. **That is a live, documented rehearsal of Yellowknife
being severed from the continent by a single road failure, and it happened three years ago.**

## 1.2 What the city runs on now

**Population: 20,340 (2021 census)**, in 7,519 occupied dwellings, on a land area of 103.37 km² (39.91 sq mi),
density 196.8/km². That is **roughly 45% of the entire Northwest Territories population** (45,808, April
2026, NWT Bureau of Statistics). Growth is slow: **19,642 in 2005 to 22,858 in 2025 on the city's own count**
— note that the city's figure runs above the census figure, and ⚠️ **the two bases are not the same; do not
mix them.** (City figures via Cabin Radio, 12 April 2026.) Median age 34.6. **Indigenous population 4,810, or
24.2%** (2021): 14.6% First Nations, 5.3% Métis, 3.5% Inuit. Immigrants are **16.4%**, the largest single
origin being the Philippines (965 people).

**Yellowknife specifically:** major employers listed are the territorial government, the federal government,
the three diamond-mine operators, Canadian North, Northwestel, RTL Robinson Trucking and the City itself.
Wikipedia puts **government employment at 7,644 jobs, a large share of them in Yellowknife** — ⚠️ **that figure is undated and almost certainly territory-wide, not municipal; do not treat

population 15 and over of 16,561, an employment rate of 75.3% against 65.7% territory-wide (NWT Bureau of

**The diamond service economy is ending in real time.** Per the companion file: Diavik ceased production 26
March 2026, Ekati went into receivership 14 July 2026, Gahcho Kué is expected to close in 2028, and — the
sharpest signal — **there will be no Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road in 2027**, the first missing season in
decades. Yellowknife has been the dispatch point for that road's convoys, the head office town for Diavik
Diamond Mines Inc., and the transfer point for mine workers, freight and services. **That entire function is
closing.**

**Aurora tourism is the city's main private-sector growth story, and it is real but under-measured.** Tourism
is described by the territory as **the largest renewable industry in the NWT**, with Yellowknife as the main
entry point; visitors to the territory spent **CA$100.5 million in 2004–05** (the figure Wikipedia carries).
The city introduced a **4% accommodation levy in April 2025, projected to raise about $1.5 million a year**,

best-known dedicated operator; and Air North described its own Yellowknife business as historically driven by
"just aurora tourism," which it is now trying to diversify away from.

**And the newest and largest thing in the city's economic future is military.** Yellowknife and Inuvik were
announced as **northern operational support hubs** — an upgrade from their existing status as NORAD **forward

airfield, fuel storage, ammunition storage, heated warehousing and accommodation, in Yellowknife, built in the
decade before the war.**

## 1.3 Physical setting

**Yellowknife sits on the Canadian Shield**, scoured to bare rock by the last glaciation. The landscape is
**rocky and gently rolling, studded with thousands of small lakes**, with spruce and birch, low shrubs, and
wide expanses of bare rock and lichen. **Bedrock is at or near the surface across much of the city** — which
is why Old Town looks the way it does, why foundations are cheap and trenching is expensive, and why the
city's roads must be **regraded and resurfaced every 10 to 20 years.**

**The rock is the Yellowknife greenstone belt, and the gold was in the Kam Group** — Archean volcanic and
sedimentary rocks. **The ore mineral was arsenopyrite**, an iron-arsenic-sulfide, which is the geological
root of the arsenic problem: the bedrock around Yellowknife is naturally arsenic-bearing, and a century of
roasting that ore to free the gold concentrated it catastrophically. Con Mine reached **over 6,000 ft
(1,800 m) deep** and produced **more than 5,000,000 troy ounces of gold from 12,195,585 tons of ore,
1938–2003**; Giant produced **over 7 million ounces, 1948–2004**. The **Robertson headframe over Con's shaft,
the city's landmark, was demolished on 29 October 2016.**

**Great Slave Lake** is the largest thing in the neighborhood by a wide margin:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Area | **27,200 km² (10,500 sq mi)** — 10th largest lake in the world |
| Length × width | **469 km (291 mi) long, 20–203 km (12–126 mi) wide** |
| **Maximum depth** | **614 m (2,014 ft)** in Christie Bay — **deepest lake in North America** |
| Main western basin | area 18,500 km², volume 596 km³, **max depth 187.7 m, mean depth 32.2 m** |
| Total volume | 1,070–2,088 km³ depending on source |
| **Ice cover** | **at least partially frozen an average of eight months a year** |

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Slave_Lake

Note the asymmetry: **the western basin, where Yellowknife is, averages only 32 m deep**; the 614 m figure
belongs to Christie Bay in the East Arm, 200 km away past the Pethei Peninsula. **For navigation out of
Yellowknife the relevant number is the shallow one.** The lake's tributaries are the **Hay, Slave (carrying
the Peace), Lockhart and Taltson**; its outflow is the **Mackenzie**. The western shore is forested; **the
east shore and the North Arm are tundra-like** — Yellowknife sits close to the tree line, with the treeless
barrens beginning roughly 100 km to the northeast.

**Yellowknife Bay** is a north–south arm at the lake's northwest corner, sheltered by **Latham Island**
(N'Dilǫ) and the Old Town peninsula, with **Back Bay** behind them on the west side and **Jolliffe Island**
(houseboats) in the middle. **Dettah is 6.5 km (4.0 mi) directly across the bay** — or 27 km (17 mi) around by
road. The bay is the city's float-plane base, its harbor, and, in winter, its ice road, its snow castle and
its festival ground.

**Permafrost.** Yellowknife lies in the **discontinuous permafrost zone** — permafrost is present in patches,
controlled by vegetation, snow cover, drainage and rock, rather than being continuous as it is at Inuvik or
Tuktoyaktuk. It is documented as a real engineering constraint on city roads. ⚠️ **The mapped extent and
thickness of permafrost under Yellowknife specifically was not obtained.** This matters for a collapse
scenario — buried water and sewer lines, building foundations and road grades all behave differently in
patchy permafrost than in continuous — and it is worth closing. Sources: the NWT Geological Survey and the
Geological Survey of Canada permafrost mapping.

Yellowknife has a **subarctic climate (Köppen Dfc)**. Normals are 1991–2020 at Yellowknife Airport (WMO
71936); extremes run from 1942.

- **Record low: −51.2 °C (−60.2 °F), 31 January 1947. Record high: 32.6 °C (90.7 °F), 2 August 2021.**
  **Record wind chill: −64.0.**
- **Annual precipitation 293.9 mm (11.57 in)** — Yellowknife is in the **rain shadow of the western mountain
  ranges** and is, by precipitation, semi-arid. Most of it falls June–September; **April is the driest month.**
- **Environment and Climate Change Canada ranks Yellowknife as having the coldest winter and the longest
  snow-cover season of any Canadian city — and the sunniest spring and summer.** Summer sunshine
  June–August averages **1,034 hours, the most in Canada.**
- **Frost-free growing season: slightly over 100 days on average**, moderated by the lake. In some years the
  first fall frost holds off until October.
- **Light and dark:** daylight runs from **about 5 hours in December to about 20 hours in June**, and **civil
  twilight lasts all night from late May to mid-July** — there is no true darkness for roughly seven weeks.
  December gets **15.4% of possible sunshine; June gets 63.0%.**
- **Heating degree days (18 °C base): approximately 7,975 per year.** ⚠️ **This is my own calculation from the
  monthly normals above (sum of (18 − monthly mean) × days), not a published figure.** Computed on a 18.3 °C /
  65 °F base it comes to ~8,084. Published Canadian values for Yellowknife generally fall in the
  **8,000–8,300** range; the method here slightly understates because it uses monthly means rather than daily
  ones. **Either way: roughly double Toronto and well above Anchorage. Heat is not optional here for eight
  months of the year, and it is the single largest energy demand in the city.**

**Freeze-up and break-up, which govern the transport calendar:**

- **Dettah ice road across Yellowknife Bay:** opens between about **21 December and 11 January** — 22 December
  in 2025–26, 21 December in 2024–25 (the earliest in a decade), **late January in 2023–24**, and **10 January
  2023**, one day inside the record-latest of 11 January in records going back to 1993. **It generally stays
  open until early to mid-April.**
  https://cabinradio.ca/273232/news/yellowknife/dettah-ice-road-has-opened-for-2025-26-gnwt-says/
- **The main lake:** at least partially frozen an average of **eight months a year**; per the companion file,
  ice can persist in the middle of Great Slave Lake **into mid-June.**
- **The Mackenzie:** break-up early-to-mid May in the south, late May to early June in the north; freeze-up
  late October or November, starting at the northern end. **Practical navigable season roughly mid-June to
  mid-October.**

## 1.5 Water and food

**Drinking water comes from the Yellowknife River, not from the lake the city sits on, and the reason is
Giant Mine.** The City of Yellowknife has drawn its primary supply from the river **since 1968, specifically
to avoid possible arsenic contamination of Great Slave Lake from the mine.** That decision is the origin of a
piece of infrastructure that deserves close attention in any collapse scenario:

- Raw water is taken at **pumphouse two, near the mouth of the Yellowknife River**, and moved to **pumphouse
  one, next to the water treatment plant**, through an **8.5 km submarine pipeline under Yellowknife Bay.**
- **Diver inspections in 2016 found leaks in that pipeline.** Replacement was costed at **$26 million in
  2019**, **$57 million by early 2023**, and **$107.7 million in the 2025 AECOM study** — at which point the
  city stopped planning to replace it and redirected the $26 million of federal money to the pumphouses.
- **Pumphouse one is "in a state of failure"** — its heating system has failed — and **its oldest components
  date from 1948.** The city's public works director has likened the planned work to heart
  surgery on the water system. It is not likely to start before **2028**.
- **A $30 million water treatment plant was completed in 2015.** Treatment is **chlorination plus fluoride
  addition; the water is not otherwise filtered or treated.**
- **Licensed annual draw: 4 million m³. The city has applied to double it to 8 million m³**, partly to
  accommodate the coming military construction.
- **The city has drawn directly from Yellowknife Bay during pipeline work** (2022 and again before February
  2026), and territorial health testing found the water quality high. **The alternative to the pipeline is
  therefore real — but it requires an arsenic-removal process the plant does not currently have, and it is
  only safe while Giant Mine stays contained.**

https://cabinradio.ca/224581/news/politics/new-water-pipeline-could-now-cost-more-than-100m-yk-council-told/ ·
https://cabinradio.ca/286372/news/yellowknife/yellowknife-to-hold-meeting-over-next-phase-of-water-supply-work/

**Sewage** is gravity-fed and pumped through lift stations to **Fiddler's Lake Lagoon**, a series of lakes
where it is held and allowed to decompose naturally. Parts of the city not on piped service use **trucked
water and trucked sewage** — a normal northern arrangement and one that survives a pipe failure.

**Local food production is negligible, and this is not a close question.** The companion file states the
structural position for the territory: **this is not farmland at any latitude in either territory.** For
Yellowknife specifically:

- **The growing season is slightly over 100 frost-free days**, which is enough for brassicas, roots, potatoes
  and hardy greens under favorable conditions — and the soil is the problem, not the season. **Yellowknife is
  on scoured Canadian Shield bedrock with thin, patchy, acidic soil and abundant exposed rock.** There is no
  agricultural land base.
- What exists is **gardens, community gardens and greenhouses**. Historical note with some weight: **Con Mine
  provided garden plots where families grew vegetables** in the 1940s and 1950s — so it is demonstrably
  possible at household scale.
- **Territorial GDP for agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting combined was $25.8 million in 2025 — 0.6%
  of the NWT economy**, and most of that is not agriculture.
- ⚠️ **No commercial farm, market garden or greenhouse operation in or near Yellowknife was verified by
  name or output in this pass.** The Territorial Farmers Association, ITI's agriculture program and the
  Yellowknife Farmers Market are the places to look.

**Food arrives by truck, on one road.** Everything retail comes up **Highway 1 from Alberta and Highway 3 from
Fort Providence**, about **525 km (326 mi) from the border**, or by air. **Yellowknife is the distribution hub
for the rest of the territory** — Food Banks Canada described the goal of making "Yellowknife a hub for food
security" for the Sahtu, and Tulita's mayor put a grocery round-trip from his community to Yellowknife at
**more than $2,000**. What that means in reverse: **cut Highway 3 and you do not just starve Yellowknife, you
starve the communities that restock from it.**
https://cabinradio.ca/213038/news/economy/food/inflation-barge-crisis-push-sahtu-to-look-at-more-food-banks/

**The one genuine local calorie source is fish.** Great Slave Lake holds **lake whitefish, lake trout,
inconnu, northern pike, walleye, cisco, burbot and longnose sucker**, and is described as rich in aquatic life
for its biome because the Slave River delivers high nutrient loads and the lake has essentially no pollution
or invasive species. **Lake whitefish is the most abundant.** There is a commercial Great Slave Lake fishery
based at Hay River, and the GNWT has a Commercial Fishery Revitalization program. ⚠️ **Current landings, quota
and the size of the Yellowknife-area subsistence and commercial catch were not obtained.** **Dettah's name —
"Burnt Point" — refers to a fishing camp the Yellowknives used for hundreds of years, at that exact spot.**

## 1.6 Energy — Snare and Bluefish, and why they change everything

**This is the most consequential section in the file.** A city with its own hydroelectric dams is a
fundamentally different proposition after a collapse than a city running on flown-in diesel, and Yellowknife
has dams.

**The Snare Hydro System** — on the **Snare River, about 140 km (87 mi) northwest of Yellowknife**, owned and
operated by the **Northwest Territories Power Corporation (NTPC)**, a territorially-owned Crown utility. It is
**four separate plants** on one river, tied to Yellowknife by **about 150 km of NTPC transmission line**:

| Plant | Units | Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Snare Rapids | 1 × 8.5 MW + 1 × 500 kW | 9.0 MW |
| Snare Falls | 1 × 7.5 MW | 7.5 MW |
| **Snare Cascades** | 1 × 4.3 MW | 4.3 MW — **leased from the Dogrib Power Corporation** |
| Snare Forks | 2 × 4.5 MW + 150 kW standby | 9.0 MW (+0.15) |
| **Snare total** | | **≈ 29.8 MW** |

**Bluefish Hydro** — at the **headwaters of the Yellowknife River**, much closer to the city. **Two 3.3 MW
generators, 6.6 MW total.** NTPC's own account credits Bluefish alone with **up to 20% of Yellowknife's
electricity demand — the equivalent of roughly 11 million liters of diesel a year.** The present **dam was
completed in
2012, replacing a 70-year-old timber crib dam** — that is, the original dated to roughly **1942**, built in

https://www.ntpc.com/energy-alternatives/how-we-supply-power/hydro-electric

Utilities (majority-owned by ATCO) distributes inside Yellowknife**, serving about **6,350 residential and 800
commercial customers.** **Territory-wide, 76% of NTPC's generation is hydro.**

**The diesel backup is large, and it is the number to hold onto.** Wikipedia's Yellowknife article, citing

28.8 MW from Snare, and 7.5 MW from Miramar Bluefish.** The separate Wikipedia list of NWT generating

⚠️ **The capacity figures disagree across sources and should be treated as approximate.** Snare appears as

Wikipedia pages). **Use "roughly 36 MW of hydro (about 30 MW Snare, about 7 MW Bluefish) against about 28–31
MW of diesel at Jackfish Lake," and do not put a false precision on it.**

**What actually matters for the story, stated plainly:**

1. **Yellowknife runs almost wholly on Snare–Bluefish hydro.** That is Wikipedia's characterization and it
   matches NTPC's own description of the hydro zone. **Jackfish Lake is backup and peaking, not baseload.**
2. **The hydro is close, and one piece of it is very close.** Snare is 140 km out with 150 km of line; but
   **Bluefish is on the Yellowknife River, the same river the city drinks from.** ⚠️ **Its exact distance from
   the city was not verified; it is on the order of a few tens of kilometers, not the 140 km to Snare.**
   A society that can hold 150 km of line can hold Snare. A society that can hold 25 km of line can hold
   Bluefish — **and Bluefish alone is 6.6 MW, roughly a fifth of the modern city's demand.**
3. **The grid is small and self-contained.** **The NWT has no intertie to any other jurisdiction.** The Snare
   (North Slave) and Taltson (South Slave) grids are **not even connected to each other.** In peacetime this
   is a weakness; after a collapse it is close to a virtue — **there is no external grid whose failure takes
   Yellowknife down with it.**
4. **The machinery is not exotic.** Four Snare units are between 4.3 and 8.5 MW; Bluefish is two 3.3 MW units.
   These are small, low-head, mature, mechanically simple machines. **Compare the alternative on offer
   anywhere else in the Republic: 25 unconnected diesel plants in Nunavut burning fuel shipped in once a
   year.**
5. **Fuel independence has a number attached.** Bluefish alone displaces **11 million liters of diesel a year**
   by NTPC's own reckoning. In a country with **no refinery in either territory and no oil production at all
   since Norman Wells shut in 2026**, that is not an environmental statistic. It is the difference between a
   city that works and one that does not.

**The expansion that has not happened:** the **Taltson Hydroelectric Expansion** would add **60 MW** and, in
its first phase, **connect the Snare and Taltson grids** — which would put Yellowknife and Hay River on one
system. It has been a proposal for years. ⚑ Later phases contemplate tying into **Alberta or Saskatchewan**,
which in this fiction means the Prairie Federation.

**A closed opportunity worth noting:** **Con Mine's deep shafts were assessed as a geothermal heat source
with the potential to heat half of Yellowknife.** A memorandum of understanding was signed with Corix
Utilities in June 2011; **a 2011 referendum voted the plan down and it was abandoned.** The shafts are still
there. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Con_Mine

## 1.7 Indigenous geography

**The Yellowknives Dene are the people of this place, and the city is named for them.** The **Yellowknives**
(Tetsǫ́t'ınę; also Copper Indians, Red Knives, T'atsaot'ine) are one of the five main Dene groups of the
Northwest Territories. **The name comes from the color of their tools, made from native copper** traded and
mined from deposits toward the Arctic coast. Their historic range ran **north and northeast of Great Slave
Lake around the Yellowknife River and Yellowknife Bay, north along the Coppermine River, northeast to the
Back River and east to the Thelon** — a corridor from the lake to the Arctic Ocean, traveled by river.
**Samuel Hearne met them in the 1770s and they guided him from Hudson Bay to the Arctic Ocean.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowknives

**Chief Akaitcho** ("Big Foot") guided Franklin's Coppermine Expedition of 1819–22 and kept the survivors
alive when the expedition failed; his name is now carried by the **Akaitcho Territory Government**, the
organization of the Treaty 8 Dene of the Great Slave region. **Chief Snuff of the Yellowknives signed Treaty 8
in 1899.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akaitcho

**The two communities:**

- **Dettah** (T'é?ehdaá, "Burnt Point") — on a point on the **east side of Yellowknife Bay**, **6.5 km (4.0
  mi) from Yellowknife by ice road in winter, 27 km (17 mi) by the Ingraham Trail year-round.** **Population
  192 (2021 census)**, down 12.3% from 219 in 2016, on 1.12 km². **It was a traditional Yellowknives fishing
  camp for hundreds of years and became the first formal settlement in the area in the 1930s** — before
  Yellowknife existed.
- **N'Dilǫ** ("end of the island") — **on the tip of Latham Island, physically inside Yellowknife at the north
  end of Old Town.** Population ~321 (2016). The land was set aside as "Lot 500" in **1947**; the federal
  government built the first ten houses in **1959**, painted in colors that got the place the local name
  **"Rainbow Valley."** It hosts the **K'àlemì Dene School** and the head office of **Det'on Cho**, the First
  Nation's investment company. **Renamed N'Dilǫ officially in 1991.**

**The Yellowknives Dene First Nation** was formed in **1991** (formerly the Yellowknife B Band), after a
territory-wide comprehensive land claim negotiation collapsed. **It is negotiating a settlement through the
Akaitcho land claim process, which is not concluded.** The dialect spoken at Dettah and N'Dilǫ is
**Wíílíídeh**, which developed from intermarriage between Yellowknives and Tłı̨chǫ people. ⚠️ **Current
enrolled membership of the YKDFN and the status of the Akaitcho process as of 2026 were not verified in this
pass.**

**Det'on Cho is not a minor detail.** Its security subsidiary, **Det'on Cho Scarlet Security, provides
security on the Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road** (companion file). **Indigenous corporate structures are
woven through the region's heavy logistics, not adjacent to it** — Nuna Logistics, which builds that road, is
51% Inuit-owned; Snare Cascades is leased from the **Dogrib Power Corporation**; the Diavik closure agreement
was signed with the **Tłı̨chǫ Government**.

**Also on the lake, and relevant to Part 2:** **Behchokǫ̀** (1,874; 90.9% Indigenous, the largest First
Nations community in the NWT) sits on the North Arm, on Highway 3 and on the Snare grid; **Łutselk'e** (~350
Chipewyan) sits far out in the **East Arm**, off the road system entirely — **and it is the one Great Slave
Lake community MTS serves by barge.** The **Tłı̨chǫ Highway (Highway 9, 97.0 km, opened November 2021)** now
connects **Whatì** to Highway 3; **Gamètì and Wekweètì remain winter-road-only.**

nowhere else. **This is the road that mattered to the diamond mines and it is about to matter to nothing,
because the winter road is not being built in 2027.**

> **No all-season road connects Yellowknife to the Yukon without leaving the country.** The only way to

**The Canol is worth one paragraph, because it is the ghost of the link the author is looking for.** The
**Canol Road ran 357 km (222 mi) from Norman Wells to Macmillan Pass on the Yukon–NWT border**, built by the

**The grade exists. The route across the mountains from the Mackenzie Valley to the Yukon is known, surveyed
and once driven.** For a reconstruction story that is a very different thing from open country — and for a
modern one it is a hiking trail littered with rusting trucks.

## 2.2 The Mackenzie Valley Highway — being built right now

**This is the single most important live development for the author's question, and it is moving fast in 2026.**

**What exists today: a winter road.** The **Wrigley–Fort Good Hope Winter Road** runs **486.4 km (302.2 mi)**
from the end of Highway 1 at Wrigley to Fort Good Hope, serving **Tulita, Norman Wells and Fort Good Hope**,
with a **106 km branch to Délı̨nę** on Great Bear Lake and a **165 km branch to Colville Lake**. Critically:

> **Thirty-four permanent bridges have already been built along that winter road route, and they are
> engineered to serve an eventual all-weather highway.**

That is the NWT highway inventory's own note on the route, and it is corroborated elsewhere: the territorial
government has bridged **all but six of the widest river crossings** on the corridor. **The Mackenzie Valley
Highway is not a blank-paper proposal. Most of its bridges are already standing in the bush.**

**Season.** The winter road opens in **mid-to-late December** and runs to **late March or April**. Documented
example, 2024–25: **Wrigley–Tulita opened 19 December 2024, Norman Wells–Fort Good Hope opened 20 December,
the full length was expected open by 21 December.** Tulita's mayor, in the same period, said the winter road
no longer lasts: a short window, warming up too fast.
https://cabinradio.ca/216304/news/economy/parts-of-mackenzie-valley-winter-road-open-for-2024-25-season/

**North of Fort Good Hope there is a gap.** The NWT highway inventory lists the winter road as terminating at
**Fort Good Hope**; the next surface access to the north is the **Dempster Highway** at Tsiigehtchic and Fort
McPherson. ⚠️ **Whether a seasonal winter track is maintained between Fort Good Hope and the Dempster in any
given year could not be confirmed from a primary source in this pass.** The territorial inventory says no.
**Treat the internal winter route as running Wrigley → Fort Good Hope and stopping there, and flag it if the
story needs otherwise.**

**What is being built.** The Mackenzie Valley Highway has been contemplated since **1971**, when work began on
a road grade north from Fort Simpson intended to reach the Dempster south of Inuvik; **it was abandoned in
1977** after the Berger Inquiry. It has restarted:

- **June 2018:** $140 million to build a **Great Bear River bridge** near Tulita and extend the all-weather
  road 15 km north to Mount Gaudet.
- **March 2026:** Prime Minister Mark Carney announced the highway would be extended **all the way to Inuvik**,
  with work to begin in summer 2026.
- **June 2026:** Ottawa began the formal process to list the highway as a **"project of national interest"**
  under the Building Canada Act, which shifts the federal regulatory question **from whether a project should
  proceed to how**. Public comment ran to 1 October 2026.
- **31 July 2026:** Ottawa paused construction because of wildfires.
- **11 August 2026:** approval to redirect **$65 million** to highway work.
- **17 August 2026:** the NWT expanded its environmental assessment to cover the last section.
- **2 September 2026:** the project formally absorbed **bridges over the Liard River near Fort Simpson and the
  Mackenzie River en route to Wrigley** — i.e., the two ferry crossings on Highway 1 are now part of the plan.
- **8 September 2026:** **$405 million announced** — the largest commitment yet, and a large share of the new
  **$1-billion federal Arctic Infrastructure Fund**. It funds the environmental assessment and design for
  **Wrigley to Norman Wells**, the **two-lane Great Bear River Bridge** near Tulita, phase two of the
  **Prohibition Creek Access Road**, and preliminary engineering for the Liard and Mackenzie bridges.
- **15–16 September 2026:** the environmental assessment is being **"rescoped"** to cover the whole
  Wrigley–Inuvik route in one proceeding rather than in two stages, and fresh community meetings were
  scheduled at Délı̨nę (21 Sept), Norman Wells (22 Sept), Fort Good Hope (23 Sept) and Tulita (24 Sept).

https://cabinradio.ca/307892/news/travel/405m-announced-for-mackenzie-valley-highway-assessment-bridges/ ·
https://cabinradio.ca/309441/news/sahtu/fort-good-hope/new-meetings-for-expanded-vision-of-mackenzie-valley-highway/

**The route and the schedule, from documents filed with the Mackenzie Valley Environmental Impact Review

- **As far as Fort Good Hope, the highway roughly follows the existing winter road.**
- **North of Fort Good Hope, into the Gwich'in Settlement Area, four corridor options are on the table:** the

strategically; it simply moves the end of the road 320 km north.**

## 2.3 The water route — and Yellowknife is not on it

**The geography is perfect and the freight system ignores it.**

**Great Slave Lake drains north through the Mackenzie River, 1,738 km (1,080 mi) of main stem entirely inside
the Northwest Territories, to the Beaufort Sea.** Mean flow at the lake outlet is 4,269 m³/s; at the mouth
9,910 m³/s — the second largest discharge in Canada. Navigable season roughly **mid-June to mid-October**.
(Companion file, Part 4.)

**The operator is Marine Transportation Services (MTS)**, a division of the **GNWT Department of
Infrastructure**, formed after the government bought the bankrupt Northern Transportation Company Limited's
assets in **2016**; **first MTS sailing season 2017.** Legislation was introduced in **June 2026 to turn MTS
into a Crown corporation.** The fleet is **eight high-powered shallow-draft tugboats** with barges.
https://www.inf.gov.nt.ca/en/MTS

**The critical structural fact: there is exactly one port of loading on Great Slave Lake, and it is Hay River,
not Yellowknife.** MTS's head office, main terminal, shipyard, Syncrolift and maintenance facility are all at
**Hay River**, which the territorial government describes as being tied to southern
Canada by **the country's northernmost railhead and a paved highway up from Alberta.** ⚑ **Both of those are Prairie Federation
links.** MTS's only other freight terminals are at **Inuvik and Tuktoyaktuk.**

**The 2026 cargo rate card is definitive. Yellowknife does not appear on it.**

| Port of loading | Destinations served |
|---|---|
| **Hay River, NT** | **Great Slave Lake:** Łutselk'e · **Mackenzie River:** Tulita, Norman Wells, Fort Good Hope, Inuvik, Aklavik, Tuktoyaktuk · **Western Arctic:** Sachs Harbour, Paulatuk, Ulukhaktok · **Kitikmeot:** Kugluktuk |
| **Tuktoyaktuk, NT** | Sachs Harbour, Paulatuk, Ulukhaktok, Kugluktuk |

Rates northbound from Hay River, general cargo, per 1,000 kg or 2.5 m³ (whichever is greater), **plus an 18%
fuel surcharge**: Łutselk'e $442; Tulita and Norman Wells $357; Fort Good Hope $427; **Inuvik $511; Tuktoyaktuk
$574; Kugluktuk $685.** A 20-foot container to Tuktoyaktuk is **$8,840** before the surcharge. **Cambridge Bay
is available on request.** https://www.inf.gov.nt.ca/sites/inf/files/mts_-_2026_community_cargo_rates.pdf

**So: Yellowknife, the capital, sitting on a navigable lake at the head of a navigable river, is not a barge
port.** It is served by road. **Everything that moves by water in the western NWT stages through a town 200 km
across the lake whose own supply line runs into Alberta.**

⚠️ **This is a statement about the *scheduled common-carrier* system, not about physical navigability.**
Yellowknife obviously has a working waterfront — float planes, the Great Slave Sailing Club, a boat launch on
the Giant Mine access road, houseboats, and a long history of lake freight. **The Yellowknife Transportation
Company existed and was bought by NTCL in 1965.** MTS also "serves other locations by charter." **The absence
is commercial and institutional, not geographic — and after a collapse the institutional reason evaporates
while the geography stays.** That distinction is the single most useful thing in this section.

**Tonnage.** ⚠️ **Annual MTS tonnage, fuel volumes and barge capacities were not obtained in this pass.** The
companion file records the historical pattern: barge trains of **up to fifteen shallow-draft vessels** pushed
by tugs; navigation "notoriously difficult" because sandbars shift yearly; narrow reaches where trains must be
uncoupled and towed through one unit at a time.

**And the route has been failing.** This is the most important recent development on the water:

- **2023:** low water forced schedule changes; a tugboat **grounded near Fort Providence in September** and
  had to be freed; **final barges to Norman Wells and Tulita were scrapped on 9 September.**
- **2024: the Sahtu barge season failed entirely.** Years of drought and low water on the Mackenzie made the
  usual supply barges impossible. **Fuel had to be flown into Norman Wells, prices spiked, and the town
  council declared a state of emergency.** The 2024–25 winter road was reinforced — thicker ice, fortified
  portages, widened sections — explicitly to carry the load the barges could not.
- **2026:** MTS held its fuel surcharge at **18%** and noted that **"the Mackenzie River's levels have barely
  shifted."** The season ran, with last Hay River departures around **15 September** and last
  Tuktoyaktuk-based sailings around **13 September.**

https://cabinradio.ca/287230/news/travel/mts-keeps-18-fuel-surcharge-baked-into-2026-cargo-rates/

**Do not treat the Mackenzie as a reliable highway.** It is a four-month channel that failed completely within
the last three years, in peacetime, with tugs, radar, hydrologists and a government behind it. ⚠️ **Whether
the low-water problem is a drought cycle or a durable climate shift is not established.**

## 2.4 The synthesis — the only internal route, quantified

the published segment lengths verified in 2.1 above:

| Segment | Distance | Country |
|---|---|---|

## 2.6 Eastward — is there any surface link to Nunavut?

**By all-season road: no, and there never has been.** Per the companion file: **Nunavut has roughly 850 km of
road in total, all of it local, and is the only province or territory with no road connection to anywhere.**

**By winter road: yes, and it has just stopped.** The **Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road** runs **568 km (353
mi)** from the end of **Highway 4, the Ingraham Trail, east of Yellowknife, to the Jericho Diamond Mine in
Nunavut** — **it does cross the territorial boundary.** It is a private industrial road, first built in 1982,
**85–87% of it on lake ice**, open on average **67 days a year in February and March.** **It is not being
built for the 2027 season** (companion file; Cabin Radio, 14 September 2026), because Ekati's receiver told
the land and water board no dedicated road would be constructed and Diavik is in closure. **Planning is
expected to resume "for the 2028 season."**

**By water: yes — and this is the finding people miss.** **MTS sails from Hay River on Great Slave Lake to
Kugluktuk, Nunavut, on a scheduled service every year**, going down the Mackenzie, out through the delta at
Tuktoyaktuk, and then **east along the Arctic coast into Coronation Gulf.** The 2026 schedule shows a Hay
River departure for Kugluktuk with a **12 July** cargo cut-off, and a **Tuktoyaktuk-loaded** Kitikmeot sailing
with a **25 August** cut-off and **30 August** departure. **Cambridge Bay is served on request.** ⚠️ **That is
a genuine internal marine link from the Alaska Republic's eastern interior to its Arctic archipelago, it runs
annually, and it does not touch foreign territory.** Rate: **$685 per revenue tonne northbound Hay
River–Kugluktuk, plus 18% surcharge** — the most expensive destination on the card, which tells you what the
leg costs.

**To Iqaluit and Rankin Inlet: no surface link of any kind, in any season.** Iqaluit is on Baffin Island and
is reached by sea (through Hudson Strait) or by air. Rankin Inlet is on Hudson Bay, reached by the annual
sealift or by air. ⚑ **Their natural port and railhead, Churchill, is in Manitoba — Prairie Federation.**

# PART 3 — POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

⚠️ **Everything in this Part that is not a cited figure is reasoning, and is labeled as such.** The facts in
Parts 1 and 2 are sourced. The conclusions drawn from them are mine, and the author should feel free to
overrule them — but should know which is which.

**Framing note, 2026-09-22: the author has settled Whitehorse as the Alaska Republic's capital.** This Part
is therefore written to assess Yellowknife as the Republic's **eastern anchor** — the question is not whether
it should be the capital but **what an eastern anchor 1,920 km away, reachable overland only through
Cascadia, is actually worth.**

## 3.1 Does the hydroelectricity survive, and what can it power?

**Probably yes, and this is Yellowknife's decisive advantage over every other place of its size in the
Republic.** [Reasoning, on sourced facts.]

**The case for survival:**

- **The machinery is small, mature and mechanically simple.** Four Snare units of 4.3–8.5 MW and two Bluefish
  units of 3.3 MW. Low-head river turbines and generators of this class are nineteenth-century technology
  improved, not twenty-first-century technology. **They can be repaired by machinists rather than replaced by
  supply chains.**
- **The dams are civil works, not machines.** The 2012 Bluefish dam has **a stated design life of at least 100
  years** and no moving parts. Concrete and rock do not need spares.
- **Bluefish is close.** It sits at the **headwaters of the Yellowknife River** — tens of kilometers from the
  city, on the same river the city drinks from. **A society that can patrol one short transmission corridor
  keeps 6.6 MW.**
- **The grid is isolated by design.** **The NWT has no intertie to any other jurisdiction, and the Snare and
  Taltson grids are not even connected to each other.** There is no external grid whose collapse propagates
  in. **Yellowknife's power system cannot be taken down by something happening in Alberta, because it was
  never connected to Alberta.**

**The case against, honestly:**

- **Snare is 140 km out with about 150 km of transmission line** through boreal forest and muskeg, in a region
  with a documented and worsening wildfire problem (2023). **Line maintenance over 150 km is a real burden for
  a reduced population.** [Reasoning.]
- **Snare Cascades is leased from the Dogrib Power Corporation** — an ownership question that becomes a
  political one when the lease has no state to enforce it.
- Control systems, protective relays, excitation and governor electronics are the fragile parts, and **the
  2012 Bluefish rebuild will have installed modern digital control.** ⚠️ **Whether the Snare and Bluefish
  plants can be operated manually, and what the units' black-start capability is, was not researched and is
  a genuinely important gap.**

**What ~36 MW actually powers.** [Reasoning, arithmetic mine.] At a rough **1 kW per person** of average
demand for a Canadian city with electric services, 36 MW is on the order of a modern city of **20,000–35,000**
— which is exactly what it currently does. For a post-collapse society the arithmetic is different and much
more favorable, because the loads change:

- **Electric space heating is the load that cannot be afforded.** With ~8,000 heating degree days, heating the
  existing housing stock electrically is far beyond 36 MW. **Wood and oil heat the buildings; hydro does
  everything else.** The boreal forest west and south of the city is the fuel.
- **What 36 MW buys instead:** lighting; pumping the water system; sawmills; **electric-arc and induction
  melting for scrap metal**; machine shops; **greenhouses under lights** (a real option in a place with a
  100-day season and a five-hour December day); refrigeration and freezing for a fishery; radio transmitters;
  hospital equipment. **A single 1–2 MW arc furnace, run intermittently, would make Yellowknife the only place
  in the eastern Republic that can melt metal at scale.** [Reasoning.]
- **The comparison is the point.** Per the companion file, the alternative in the east is **25 unconnected
  diesel plants in Nunavut burning fuel shipped in once a year, with no refinery in either territory and no
  oil production at all since Norman Wells shut in 2026.** Every one of those communities goes dark within a
  year of the last sealift. **Yellowknife does not.**

> **Stated plainly: after a collapse, Yellowknife is very likely the only settlement between Great Bear Lake
> and Baffin Island with electric light.** That is not a convenience. It is the basis of a regional
> primacy — because in the cold and the dark, the place with the lights is where people go.

## 3.2 Does the lake-and-river position make it a viable trade hub?

**Yes, but it requires the city to do something it does not currently do: be a port.**

**The asset is real.** Yellowknife sits on **Great Slave Lake**, 27,200 km² of navigable water, at the head of
the **Mackenzie**, 1,738 km of navigable river running to the Arctic Ocean, with the companion file's central
finding standing behind it: **the Mackenzie was a working commercial artery for sixty years using wood- and
coal-fired shallow-draft sternwheelers, before diesel, before radar, before dredging.** The portages are
known. The route is known. **It is the one piece of heavy transport infrastructure in the eastern Republic
that a low-technology society can actually operate, because the river does the work.**

**The liability is institutional and it dissolves.** Today **Yellowknife is not a barge port** (2.3) — every
scheduled sailing loads at Hay River, because Hay River has **the rail line from Alberta** and Yellowknife
does not. ⚑ **After a collapse, the rail line from Alberta is a foreign line to a country that also
collapsed.** Hay River's entire reason for being the terminal — that it is where the continental supply chain
touches the water — evaporates. **What remains is geography: a lake, a river, and whichever settlement on
them has power, people and workshops.** That is Yellowknife. [Reasoning, and the single most useful inference
in this file.]

**What that hub would actually move, in order of plausibility:**

1. **Fish, in both directions.** Great Slave and Great Bear are among the largest freshwater fisheries on
   Earth and are essentially unfished by modern standards.
2. **Timber and firewood up the lake and down the river** to the treeless communities. **This is the trade the
   geography most obviously wants**: the Mackenzie runs from forest to tundra, and the demand for wood grows
   the further north you go.
3. **Metal.** Scrap from the diamond mines, the closed gold mines, the barge fleet and the truck fleet;
   worked in Yellowknife because Yellowknife has the power; distributed by water. **Lead and zinc from Pine
   Point** (companion file) are on the same lake, 250 km away, on a road, next to a dam.
4. **Fuel, if any refining is ever recovered** — and note that MTS's core business today is **bulk petroleum
   to the river and coast communities**, so the barge tankage exists.
5. **People.** The Mackenzie is a migration corridor as much as a trade route.

**The constraints, stated without softening:**

- **Four months.** Mid-June to mid-October, and shorter at the northern end. **Eight months of the year the
  hub is a town with an ice road to Dettah.**
- **The river has failed inside the last three years.** The **2024 Sahtu barge season did not happen** because
  of low water; fuel was flown into Norman Wells and the town declared a state of emergency (2.3). **That was
  with tugs, hydrologists and a government. A society with wooden hulls and no dredging faces the same
  sandbars with fewer tools** — though it also draws far less water, which cuts the other way. [Reasoning.]
- **Shallow draft is mandatory.** Yellowknife's own basin averages **32 m**, which is ample; the problem is
  the river, where navigation is described as "notoriously difficult" and narrow reaches require barge trains
  to be uncoupled (companion file).
- **Yellowknife has no barge terminal, no Syncrolift and no shipyard.** **Hay River has all three.** ⚠️
  **Whether Yellowknife has any dock capable of handling freight at scale was not verified. It has a boat
  launch, a sailing club and a float-plane base.** This is a real gap and it matters: **the equipment the
  post-collapse hub would need is 200 km away across the lake, in a town whose reason for existing has just
  been amputated.** The obvious story is that it gets moved — or that Hay River, not Yellowknife, inherits
  the water. **The author should decide this deliberately.**

## 3.3 Yellowknife as the eastern anchor — what it is and is not

**With Whitehorse settled as the capital, the honest assessment is that Yellowknife is a strong eastern
anchor and a poor second capital, and the reason is Part 2.**

**What makes it the obvious anchor:**

1. **It is already the administrative machine of the east.** Legislature, courts, RCMP "G" Division
   headquarters, Stanton Territorial Hospital, Aurora College, the territorial and federal bureaucracies —
   **23.3% of NWT GDP is public administration, and most of that apparatus is physically in Yellowknife.**
   Institutions are the hardest thing to rebuild and the cheapest thing to inherit.
2. **It is already the air hub of the entire eastern Arctic** — Canadian North reaches Inuvik, Norman Wells,
   Cambridge Bay, Kugluktuk, Gjoa Haven, Kugaaruk, Taloyoak, Ulukhaktok, Rankin Inlet and **Iqaluit** from
   Yellowknife (2.5). ⚠️ **But aviation is the first thing a collapse removes, so this is an argument about
   the *pattern* of the east's orientation, not about a surviving capability.**
3. **It has power** (3.1) and **it has water on two independent sources** (1.5).
4. **It is 45% of the NWT's population in one place** — the only real labor pool, workshop cluster and
   institutional memory between Great Bear Lake and Hudson Bay.
5. **It is the only place in the east with a functioning Indigenous–territorial political order already in
   law**: Akaitcho, Tłı̨chǫ, Det'on Cho, the YKDFN at Dettah and N'Dilǫ, and a Yellowknives history on this
   exact bay going back centuries.
6. **The military is about to build it a hardened airfield, hangars, fuel farms, ammunition storage and heated
   warehousing** on a notice estimating "may exceed $5B," with initial capability targeted for 2034 (1.2).
   ⚠️ **Preliminary, but a war fought after 2034 finds those things built.**

**What disqualifies it as a capital, and it is a hard disqualification:**

> **A capital must be reachable by its own government. Yellowknife is reachable overland only through a
> neighbor's country, or by a four-month river.** Whitehorse can reach Anchorage, Fairbanks, Dawson, Inuvik
> and Tuktoyaktuk on internal roads. **Yellowknife can reach none of them.** From Yellowknife, the only
> all-season overland link to the rest of the Republic runs **650 km through Cascadia** — meaning the eastern
> capital's couriers, troops and freight would travel at another state's sufferance. **That is not a capital.
> That is a garrison.** [Reasoning, on the map reference and Part 2.]

**The cleanest way to frame it for the story:** Yellowknife is not the Republic's second city so much as **the
capital of a different half of the country that happens to share a flag** — a Mackenzie-and-Arctic-coast
polity, oriented north and east down the river, with its own power, its own water, its own fisheries and its
own air and barge network, joined to Whitehorse by two thin threads: **an air route that flies twice a week,
and a road that leaves the country.** [Reasoning.]

**And the thing that would change all of it: the Mackenzie Valley Highway.** If it is complete before the war
— target **2032** — then **Yellowknife gains an all-season internal road to Inuvik, and through the Dempster
to Dawson, Whitehorse and Alaska. The Republic becomes one country instead of two.** ⚠️ **The author should
set the war's date against 2032 deliberately, because that single choice decides whether the Alaska Republic
is connected or bisected.** Note also that **34 of the highway's bridges are already standing on the winter
road route**, so even an abandoned, half-built highway leaves a usable corridor behind — which is a very good
piece of scenery.

## 3.4 Giant Mine's arsenic, if the freezing plant stops

**This is the best post-collapse hazard in the region and it is entirely real.** The companion file
establishes the facts: **237,000 tonnes of arsenic trioxide dust, about 60% arsenic by weight, in fifteen
underground stopes and chambers roughly 76 m below the surface, immediately adjacent to Yellowknife Bay** —
odorless, tasteless, lethal in small doses and **soluble in water**. The approved remediation, costed at
roughly **C$900 million to C$1 billion**, is to **freeze the chambers permanently using artificial ground
refrigeration.** **It is a machine, not a seal.** A 2006 flooding event around one chamber holding 16,946
tonnes demonstrated the threat. **The mine is 4 km north of the city limits.**

**This file adds three things the companion file does not.**

**First, the failure is slow, not sudden.** [Reasoning.] The freezing plant maintains a frozen block around
the chambers. If the power and the refrigerant stop, the block does not melt in a week — the ground is in a
discontinuous-permafrost region with mean annual air temperature of **−4.0 °C**, and the thermal mass is
enormous. **What follows is years of gradual thaw, then groundwater ingress, then a rising plume — not an
explosion.** ⚠️ **The thaw timescale was not researched and is the single most story-relevant unknown in this
section.** The Giant Mine Remediation Project's own thermal modeling exists and should be consulted. **For
fiction: a hazard that arrives quietly, a decade or two after the lights go out, into a population that has
half-forgotten why the old people said not to fish the bay, is far better than one that arrives in chapter
one.**

**Second, the irony is already built into the city's water system.** Yellowknife has drunk from the
**Yellowknife River since 1968 specifically to avoid this**, through an **8.5 km submarine pipeline across
Yellowknife Bay** — **the very water body the arsenic would enter.** The pipeline leaks, **pumphouse one dates
in part from 1948 and is "in a state of failure,"** and replacement was priced at **$107.7 million** and
shelved (1.5). **So the collapse scenario finds a city whose safe water depends on a failing pipe laid across
the contaminated bay, whose fallback — drawing directly from the bay — is exactly the option the arsenic
forecloses.** That is not a contrivance. It is the documented present state.

**Third, the bedrock is arsenical anyway.** The Yellowknife greenstone belt's ore mineral was
**arsenopyrite**, and the region has naturally elevated arsenic independent of the mine (1.3). **Historical
roasting also released arsenic trioxide directly to the air and ground across the city.** There are ongoing
programs monitoring residents. **A society that loses laboratory testing loses the ability to know which
lakes and which soils around Yellowknife are safe** — and there are thousands of small lakes. [Reasoning.]

**The usable story shape:** the freezing plant is the last thing the city keeps running, long after it has
stopped running anything else, because everyone understands what it is for — **and the moment it cannot be
kept running is the moment Yellowknife stops being a city on a bay and becomes a city with its back to one.**

## 3.5 Carrying capacity — can 20,000 people on the Shield feed themselves?

**No. Not close. And the arithmetic is not ambiguous.** [Reasoning, on sourced inputs.]

The companion file's territorial calculation lands on **15,000–35,000 people living fully off the land across
the whole of the NWT and Nunavut — 2.96 million km²** — against a present population of about 83,000, and
notes the more important refinement: **the settlements are in the wrong shape.** A subsistence economy
supports small groups dispersed across a very large area, moving with the resource. **It does not support
towns.**

**Applied to Yellowknife specifically:**

- **There is no agricultural land.** Scoured Canadian Shield bedrock, thin acidic soil, extensive bare rock.
  **A 100-day frost-free season is enough; the soil is not there.** (1.5)
- **The caribou may not come.** The **Bathurst herd fell from ~470,000 animals in 1986 to 3,609 in 2025**, a
  99%+ decline whose causes are not understood (companion file), **and its range is precisely the barrens
  northeast of Yellowknife.** The herd that historically fed this country is effectively gone. **Do not write
  a Yellowknife that hunts caribou. Write one that remembers hunting caribou.**
- **Fish is the one genuine local calorie source**, and it is a good one: Great Slave holds lake whitefish,
  lake trout, inconnu, pike, walleye, cisco and burbot, in a lake with essentially no pollution or invasives
  (1.3). Dettah has been a fishing site for centuries. ⚠️ **No sustainable-yield figure for Great Slave Lake
  was obtained, and that is the number that would actually settle this question.** It should be gettable from
  DFO's Great Slave Lake commercial fishery assessments.
- **Wood is abundant and this matters more than it sounds.** Yellowknife is **below the tree line** with
  spruce and birch — unlike Iqaluit, Cambridge Bay or Mary River. **With 8,000 heating degree days, fuel is a
  survival input on the order of food.** The forest is the reason a settlement here is possible at all.
- **Imported carbohydrate does not exist.** The companion file's finding holds: the northern problem is
  **calories and fat**, not protein.

**A defensible range.** [Reasoning, arithmetic mine, argue with it.] With hydroelectric power, greenhouses,
a managed lake fishery, firewood, moose and small game in the forest, waterfowl, berries, and trade up the
river — **a post-collapse Yellowknife plausibly holds somewhere in the range of 2,000 to 6,000 people,
depending almost entirely on whether the fishery is managed and whether the power stays on.** That is **10–30%
of its present population**, and it is **substantially better than any other settlement in the eastern
Republic can manage**, because the others have neither wood nor hydro. ⚠️ **This is an estimate with no
sourced fishery yield behind it. Treat the shape of the argument as sound and the number as a placeholder.**

**The blunt version, which the author asked for:** **a city of 20,000 on the Canadian Shield cannot feed
itself, and nothing in this research suggests otherwise.** What Yellowknife can do is something different and
arguably more interesting: **it can be the place that has power, workshops, a hospital, a library, a
legislature and a sawmill, fed by a hinterland of fishers and hunters and by a river that brings food north
and wood south — a small city living off a large territory, which is exactly what it has always been.** The
difference after a collapse is that the territory it lives off has to be the Mackenzie basin instead of
Alberta. [Reasoning.]

## Method note

Research was done with `curl` against primary sources, the Wikipedia action and REST APIs, `pdftotext` on
government PDFs, and Google News RSS as a search substitute. **No source passage is reproduced verbatim;
everything is restated.** Where two sources disagree the disagreement is recorded rather than resolved.
Arithmetic performed here — distances, heating degree days, carrying capacity, power-per-capita — is labeled
⚠️ and shown so it can be checked.
