# Midwestland — Real-World Material Base

**Compiled 2026-09-22.** This file is a *real-world factual baseline*, not fiction. It documents what the
territory assigned to the neo-country **the Midwest Republic ("Midwestland")** actually contains — soils,
yields, rivers, ores, populations — using free public data (USDA NASS, USGS, EIA, US Census, USACE). It exists
so the author can extrapolate a post-2083 reconstruction era from accurate numbers rather than from vibes.
Nothing here is invented. Where a figure is uncertain or sources disagree, it is flagged in the text and again
in the **Uncertainties and Unverified Claims** section near the end.

**Territory used for this file (per the author's map):** North Dakota, South Dakota, Minnesota, Nebraska,
Iowa, Kansas, Missouri — seven states. Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania are
*outside* and belong to other nations on the author's map; Chicago is therefore not in this territory.
Exact borders are the author's to set. All seven-state aggregates below were computed by me from the
underlying state figures and are labeled as such.

**Scale of the territory:** 507,607 square miles of land area (US Census 2024 Gazetteer county file, summed by
me), about 14% of US land; 21,977,413 people (Census 2024 estimates, summed by me), about 6.5% of US
population; roughly 43 people per square mile against a US figure near 96.

## 0. A Correction to the American Nations Cross-Reference

The existing file `American_Nations_to_New_North_America_Cross-Reference.md` records **"Midwestland = The
Midlands — strong match, including the name itself."** With the territory as scoped above, **that entry is no
longer accurate and should be revisited.**

Woodard's Midlands is centered on the lower Great Lakes and the Ohio Valley: southeastern Pennsylvania,
central Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Iowa, and northern Missouri, with lobes into Michigan. The re-scoped
Midwestland keeps only the western end of that (Iowa, eastern Nebraska, eastern Kansas, Missouri) and adds
large blocks that Woodard assigns elsewhere — **Minnesota and the eastern Dakotas are Woodard's Yankeedom**,
and **western Kansas, western Nebraska and the western Dakotas are his Far West.** The author's Midwestland is
therefore a *trans-Mississippi plains* nation, not a Midlands nation, and its cultural composition on
Woodard's terms is roughly Midlands + Yankeedom + Far West rather than Midlands alone.

The original observation about fragmentation still holds but now applies to a nation the author no longer
owns: Woodard's Midlands is unusually broken up, with separate lobes, and its Great Lakes wedge is what
divides the two halves of his Yankeedom. That wedge is now outside Midwestland entirely.

This is worth flagging because it changes the nation's character argument. A Midlands nation would be
pluralist, commercial, German-and-Quaker-descended, and industrial. A ND/SD/MN/NE/IA/KS/MO nation is
Scandinavian-and-German in the north, Upland Southern in Missouri, and Far Western on the high plains — and
it is overwhelmingly agricultural rather than industrial, because the manufacturing belt lies outside its
borders.

## 1. The Land Itself — Soil, Glaciation, Rainfall

### 1.1 Why this soil is exceptional

The dominant soil order across the eastern two-thirds of this territory is **Mollisol**. Mollisols are defined
by a *mollic epipedon* — a thick, dark, base-saturated surface horizon, typically 60–80 cm (24–31 inches)
deep, built up by the long-term decay of grass roots rather than by leaf litter from above. They have soft
granular structure, high cation exchange capacity, and high native fertility. They cover only about **7% of
the world's ice-free land area** and are described by soil science as the world's agriculturally most
productive soil order. ([USDA/Soil Taxonomy via Mollisol
overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollisol); [ScienceDirect Mollisol
topic](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/mollisol))

Three geological accidents stacked to produce them here:

1. **Glaciation.** The Laurentide Ice Sheet ground bedrock into fresh, unweathered mineral flour and spread it
   as till across Iowa, southern Minnesota, eastern Nebraska, and the eastern Dakotas, leveling the terrain in
   the process. Fresh mineral matter means the parent material had not been leached of its nutrients.
2. **Loess.** As the ice retreated, braided meltwater rivers left bare silt floodplains that dried each winter
   and blew downwind. The resulting loess blankets much of the territory and reaches **tens of meters** in
   places; the **Loess Hills along the Iowa–Nebraska border are the largest loess deposit in the United
   States.** Loess is fertile because of high cation exchange capacity and porosity, not because of organic
   matter. It is also highly erodible — even well-managed loess farmland can lose well over 2.5 kg/m²/year,
   and historic Iowa practices exceeded 10 kg/m²/year. ([Loess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loess))
3. **Tallgrass prairie.** Roughly ten thousand years of deep-rooted perennial grasses, periodic fire, and
   earthworm and ant mixing put organic carbon *down into* the profile rather than leaving it on the surface.
   Iowa's state soil, **Tama silt loam**, is loess over glacial till with organic matter in the 3–7% range.
   ([USDA NRCS, Iowa Soil
   Associations](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/HighwayGuideToIASoilAssociations.pdf))

The practical consequence for the author: **the soil is not an artifact of industry.** It was there before
anyone plowed it and it will be there after. What industry supplies is nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and
traction — not the soil itself. That distinction is the spine of the post-collapse assessment in §10.

### 1.2 The rainfall gradient and the 100th meridian — the defining internal fault line

The single most important internal division in this territory is not north–south, it is **east–west**, and it
is climatic rather than political. The roughly **100th meridian** marks the western limit of reliable moist
air from the Gulf of Mexico. John Wesley Powell identified this in the 1870s and argued that western
settlement law was written for a rainfall regime that did not exist past that line.
([100th meridian west](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100th_meridian_west))

The meridian runs north–south through the middle of **North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Kansas.** It
therefore runs through the middle of Midwestland, not along its edge.

Measured precipitation across the gradient:

| Place | Annual precipitation | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Southeast Kansas | ~47 in (1,200 mm) | [Climate of Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Kansas) |
| Southwest Kansas | ~16 in (410 mm) | same |
| Southeast Nebraska | ~31.5 in (800 mm) | [Nebraska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebraska) |
| Nebraska Panhandle | ~13.8 in (350 mm) | same |
| Nebraska Sandhills | 17–23 in | [Nebraska Sandhills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebraska_Sand_Hills) |

A 31-inch swing across a single state. East of the line, corn grows on rain alone and always has. West of the
line, corn grows only if it is irrigated, and everything else is cattle on grass.

**This is the fault line the author should treat as structural.** The wet east and the dry west of Midwestland
are different agricultural civilizations sharing a flag: one of dense rainfed cropland and river towns, the
other of thin ranching settlement with almost no people in it. Population density makes the point starkly

### 1.3 The Nebraska Sandhills

About a quarter of Nebraska — commonly given as **19,600–23,600 square miles** — is grass-stabilized sand
dunes up to 330 feet high. The sandy soil is too fragile to plow, so **most of it has never been broken**, and
as much as 85% of the ecoregion remains intact natural habitat. It is rangeland supporting beef cattle
(figures around 530,000 head are cited), and its shallow lakes and permeable sand make it one of the few
places where the Ogallala Aquifer meaningfully **recharges.**
([Nebraska Sandhills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebraska_Sand_Hills))

For the author: the Sandhills are a piece of terrain that behaves the same in 1850, 2026, and 2280. They are
grass, water, and cattle, and they require nothing from industry.

## 3. The Ogallala / High Plains Aquifer — The Clock Under the Dry Half

Primary source: **McGuire, V.L., and Strauch, K.R., 2024, *Water-Level and Recoverable Water in Storage
Changes, High Plains Aquifer, Predevelopment to 2019 and 2017 to 2019*, USGS Scientific Investigations Report
2023–5143** ([PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2023/5143/sir20235143.pdf)).

- The aquifer underlies **111.8 million acres (~175,000 sq mi)** across eight states, of which Nebraska,
  Kansas and South Dakota are inside Midwestland.
- Saturated thickness ranges from 0 to about **1,200 feet.**
- Groundwater irrigation began in earnest around **1950.** Irrigated acreage over the aquifer went from
  **2.1 million acres in 1949 to 13.7 million in 1980 to 14.8 million in 2017.** Withdrawals went from
  4 million acre-feet per year in 1949 to 19 million by 1974; 13.0 million acre-feet in 2015.
- **Recoverable water in storage in 2019: about 2.91 billion acre-feet**, a decline of **286.4 million
  acre-feet (about 10%)** since predevelopment.
- By 2019, **15% of the aquifer area had lost more than a quarter of its predevelopment saturated thickness,
  and 6% had lost more than half.**

State-by-state, and this is the part that matters most:

| State | Avg. water-level change, predevelopment→2019 | Change in recoverable storage |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Nebraska** | **−0.4 ft** | **−2.7 million acre-ft** |
| **South Dakota** | **+0.5 ft** | **+0.2 million acre-ft** |
| **Kansas** | **−27.3 ft** | **−72.0 million acre-ft** |
| Colorado | −14.0 ft | −18.3 |
| Oklahoma | −14.2 ft | −12.1 |
| New Mexico | −19.1 ft | −11.4 |
| Texas (outside territory) | −44.1 ft | −169.6 |
| **Whole aquifer** | **−16.5 ft** | **−286.4** |

**Nebraska's share of the aquifer is essentially intact after seventy-five years of the heaviest irrigation in
the country.** South Dakota's has *risen*. Kansas's has lost 27 feet and 72 million acre-feet. The reason is
the Sandhills and the Platte: Nebraska sits on the thickest saturated section of the aquifer, it is recharged
through permeable sand, and the Platte River recharges the alluvium alongside it. Western Kansas sits on a
thin southern lobe with recharge rates measured in fractions of an inch per year
([Ogallala Aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogallala_Aquifer) cites recharge from 0.024 in/yr in parts
of Texas and New Mexico up to 6 in/yr in south-central Kansas; full natural replenishment after depletion is
estimated at **over 6,000 years**).

**For the author:** the dry half of Midwestland is already splitting into two futures *in the real world,
today.* Nebraska can pump more or less indefinitely; western Kansas is running a closing account. In a
post-collapse setting where pumps stop entirely, this distinction mostly evaporates — but it matters enormously
in the *decline phase* before the war and in any partial-recovery phase after it, because Nebraska's
groundwater is shallow enough in places for simple lift, and western Kansas's is not.

## 4. THE CRITICAL QUESTION — What the Yields Actually Depend On

This is the section the brief flagged as most important. The short answer: **the soil and rainfall are
permanent; roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the current yield is not.**

### 4.1 Synthetic nitrogen

**Haber–Bosch** fixes atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia at **300–500 °C and 150–250 bar** over a promoted
iron catalyst. Global production is on the order of **230 million tonnes of ammonia a year**, consuming
**1–2% of global energy and 3–5% of the world's natural gas production.** Vaclav Smil's estimate is that
**nearly half the nitrogen in human tissue today originated in a Haber–Bosch reactor**, and that without it
crop production would need nearly four times the land.
([Haber–Bosch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber%E2%80%93Bosch_process))

The US-specific picture, from **USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025, Nitrogen (Fixed)—Ammonia**
([PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-nitrogen.pdf)):

- Ammonia was made by **18 companies at 37 plants in 17 states** in 2024.
- **About 55% of US ammonia capacity is in Louisiana, Oklahoma and Texas** — because that is where the natural
  gas is. **Only Oklahoma of those borders Midwestland, and none is inside it.**
- US production 2024: **14.0 million metric tons of nitrogen content.** About **88% goes to fertilizer.**
- Net import reliance is only 6%, but imports come from **Trinidad and Tobago (51%) and Canada (47%)** — both
  overseas or across a border.

How much is actually put on this land, from **USDA ERS, *Fertilizer Use and Price*, Tables 9–14**
([data page](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/fertilizer-use-and-price); crop-specific series end 2018):

**Nitrogen on corn, pounds per fertilized acre:**

| State | 1964 | 1980 | 2000 | 2018 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Missouri | 75 | 119 | 148 | **169** |
| Nebraska | 79 | 111 | 150 | **165** |
| Kansas | 60 | 169 | 147 | **147** |
| North Dakota | — | — | 98 | **146** |
| Iowa | 45 | 134 | 131 | **139** |
| Minnesota | 28 | 105 | 114 | **137** |
| South Dakota | 31 | 69 | 99 | **126** |

And the share of corn acreage receiving any nitrogen at all is now **96–100% in every one of these states**
(it was 25% in South Dakota in 1964 and 72% in Nebraska).

Phosphate and potash on corn, 2018 (lb/fertilized acre): P₂O₅ ranges from **52 (Nebraska) to 74 (Iowa,
Minnesota)**; K₂O from **32 (Nebraska) to 91 (Minnesota)**.

Nationally (ERS Table 2), **US corn alone consumed about 6.0 million short tons of nitrogen, 2.26 million
short tons of phosphate, and 2.26 million short tons of potash in 2018.** Total US plant nutrient consumption
was **22.0 million short tons** in 2015 (N 13.0, P₂O₅ 4.27, K₂O 4.75) — against 7.5 million short tons in
1960 (ERS Table 1). Applying this territory's 57.5% share of US corn, **Midwestland's corn crop alone requires
on the order of 3.4 million short tons of nitrogen a year** (my arithmetic).

### 4.2 Phosphate and potash — both mined somewhere else

This is stark and unambiguous.

**Phosphate rock** (USGS MCS 2025,
[PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-phosphate.pdf)): in 2024, US phosphate rock came from
**five companies at ten mines in four states — Florida, Idaho, North Carolina, and Utah.** Twenty million
metric tons marketable. **None of the seven Midwestland states produces any phosphate rock at all.** More than
95% of it is processed into wet-process phosphoric acid; net import reliance 13%, with imports 98% from Peru.

**Potash** (USGS MCS 2025, [PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-potash.pdf)): US production
in 2024 was **420,000 metric tons K₂O, from southeastern New Mexico and Utah only.** **None in the seven
states.** And the US is **93% net import reliant** — imports 79% from Canada, 11% from Russia, 4% from
Belarus. World potash consumption is around 38.8 million tonnes K₂O a year.

So: **Midwestland mines no phosphate and no potash.** Both are railed or barged in from Florida, Idaho, New
Mexico, and above all Saskatchewan. In a collapse, both simply stop, and unlike nitrogen there is no
biological substitute — phosphorus and potassium are elements, and you can only recycle what is already in the
system (manure, bone, wood ash, night soil) or mine more.

There is one partial mitigation worth knowing: mollisols under prairie accumulated large native reserves of
both, and the Corn Belt has been over-applied for decades, so soil test levels in many fields are well above
crop need. But this is a stock, not a flow, and the long-term trial data below shows how fast it draws down.

### 4.3 Hybrid and GMO seed — and the crucial asymmetry

**Corn is the vulnerable one.** Commercial field corn is an **F1 hybrid**: two inbred parent lines crossed to
exploit heterosis. **By 1950, 99% of Corn Belt corn was hybrid**
([Corn Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_Belt)). Seed saved from an F1 hybrid crop segregates in F2 —
the stand becomes visibly variable in height and maturity and yield drops sharply. Reproducing hybrid seed
requires maintaining inbred lines, isolation plots, detasseling or cytoplasmic male sterility, drying,
grading, treating, and cold storage — an industrial system with a multi-year pipeline. GMO traits (Bt,
glyphosate tolerance) sit on top of that and additionally require the herbicide or the insect pressure model
to be worth anything.

**Soybeans and wheat are self-pollinated and breed true.** A farmer can save soybean or wheat seed and plant
it the following spring at essentially full yield. This asymmetry is one of the most useful facts in this
entire file for worldbuilding purposes: **after a collapse, Midwestland keeps its wheat and soybeans as
varieties and loses its corn as a variety.** Corn does not disappear — open-pollinated dent corns adapted to
exactly this ground existed and were widely grown before the 1930s (Reid's Yellow Dent, Krug, Lancaster Sure
Crop) — but those genetics yield in the 30–50 bu/ac range under good management, not 211, and rebuilding
locally adapted open-pollinated landraces from surviving hybrid material is a decades-long selection project.

### 4.4 Diesel and mechanization

This is the constraint the author is most likely to underrate, because it limits **area**, not yield.

Average farm size in this territory is 308 acres (Missouri) to 1,537 acres (North Dakota). One operator with
a tractor, a planter, a sprayer and a combine farms all of it. With horses or mules, the historically
demonstrated figure is roughly **40–80 acres of row crop per family**, and on top of that **a fifth to a
quarter of the cropland has to be diverted to growing feed for the draft animals themselves** — oats, hay,
pasture. Before tractors, roughly a quarter of US cropland fed the horses and mules that worked the rest of
it.

So the collapse in output is multiplicative: fewer acres planted **and** lower yield per acre **and** a slice
of the remaining acres feeding the traction.

| 1936 | 18.6 | **2024** | **179.3** |

**From 1866 to 1936 — seventy years — US corn yield oscillated between 18 and 32 bushels per acre with no
trend whatsoever.** It was flat for the entire era of open-pollinated seed, animal traction, and manure. The
climb begins in the late 1930s with hybrids and accelerates after 1950 with cheap synthetic nitrogen. **Today's
179.3 bu/ac is roughly seven times the pre-industrial average.**

US all-wheat yield over the same period: **10.9 to 16.7 bu/ac, 1866–1918**, against **51.2 bu/ac in 2024** —
roughly a factor of four.

Two caveats the author should keep in mind. First, these are *national* averages including poor land; Iowa and
Illinois mollisols ran above the national figure, so **30–40 bu/ac corn in a good year on the best ground** is
the realistic historical upper end rather than 26. Second, 19th-century farmers were cropping virgin prairie
soil that had a huge native nitrogen stock; a modern field that has been in continuous corn for fifty years
does not start from the same place.

**(ii) The modern fertilizer-withdrawal trial — the single most useful data point in this file.**
A long-term strip at **Gothenburg, Nebraska**, in continuous corn, with **no fertilizer applied at all since
2010** ([Bayer Crop Science, *Long-Term Impact of Fertilizer Withdrawal on Corn Yield Potential and Soil
Properties*](https://www.cropscience.bayer.us/articles/bayer/long-term-impact-of-fertilizer-withdrawal-on-corn-yield-potential-and-soil-properties)):

| Year | Yield (bu/ac) | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| 2010 | 185 | first year, following a soybean crop (residual N) |
| 2011 | 113 | |
| 2014 | ~70 | stabilizing |
| 2022 | 64 | |
| 2023 | 62 | |

And the soil under it, over twelve years: **soil test phosphorus fell by half (26 ppm → 13 ppm at 0–8 in),
nitrate-N fell from ~32 lb/ac to ~12 lb/ac, and cation exchange capacity declined about 20%.**

This is the cleanest available answer to the brief's central question. **A modern Corn Belt field, with modern
hybrid genetics and modern equipment but zero fertilizer, converges on roughly 60–70 bushels per acre within
about four years, and is still slowly declining a decade later.** That is the *optimistic* floor, because it
assumes the hybrid seed keeps arriving and the tractor keeps running.

**(iii) Rotation as the real answer.**
The mitigation is not a trick, it is the system the Corn Belt actually used before 1950: a rotation of corn →
small grain → legume hay, with livestock consuming the hay and returning manure. A well-managed alfalfa or
red clover stand fixes on the order of **100–200 lb N/acre/year**, of which a large fraction is available to
the following crop — genuinely comparable to the 137–169 lb/ac of synthetic N applied today. The cost is that
**a third to a half of the rotation is not growing a cash grain.** This is precisely why 19th-century Midwest
farms were mixed crop-and-livestock operations and why the specialized corn-soybean farm is a post-1950
creature of the ammonia plant.

*(Figures for legume N fixation are widely cited agronomic ranges rather than a specific document I verified
in this session — see Uncertainties, §11.)*

### 4.6 Putting a number on it

My arithmetic, clearly labeled as extrapolation rather than data:

- **Today:** ~47.9 million harvested corn acres → **8.55 billion bushels.**
- **Low-input Midwestland** with open-pollinated seed, legume rotation, manure, animal traction:
  corn acreage falls to perhaps **15–25 million acres** (draft-animal labor ceiling, plus a third to a half of
  the rotation in legumes and small grains, plus feed for the animals), at **30–50 bu/ac.**
- **Output: roughly 0.5 to 1.2 billion bushels of corn — on the order of 6% to 14% of today's crop.**

That sounds catastrophic until it is set against consumption. Even at today's population of 22 million, direct
human grain consumption is on the order of **5–10 bushels per person per year**, and perhaps 15–25 bushels
including grain-fed meat and dairy at a modest level. **Twenty-two million people need something like 330–550
million bushels of grain equivalent.**

**The conclusion for the author is therefore the interesting one: even at a genuinely pre-industrial floor,
this territory feeds its own population several times over and still has a large exportable surplus.** The
surplus becomes hundreds of millions of bushels instead of billions — but a surplus measured in hundreds of
millions of bushels, in a continent where nobody else has one, is still the single most powerful
strategic asset on the map. The breadbasket leverage does not disappear. It shrinks by an order of magnitude
and becomes *harder to move*, which throws all the weight onto the rivers.

## 5. Water, Rivers, and the Great Lakes

### 5.1 The Missouri

- **2,341 miles long — the longest river in North America** — draining **529,350 square miles.**
  ([Missouri River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_River))
- **Six mainstem dams** built under the Pick–Sloan program, five of them inside Midwestland:

| Dam | State | Reservoir | Storage (acre-ft) | Hydro (MW) |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Fort Peck | Montana *(outside)* | Fort Peck Lake | 18,690,000 | 185 |
| Garrison | North Dakota | Lake Sakakawea | ~23,800,000 | 515–583 |
| Oahe | South Dakota | Lake Oahe | ~23,137,000–23,500,000 | 786 |
| Big Bend | South Dakota | Lake Sharpe | 1,910,000 | 493 |
| Fort Randall | South Dakota | Lake Francis Case | 5,700,000 | 320 |
| Gavins Point | Nebraska/South Dakota | Lewis and Clark Lake | 492,000 | 132 |
| **Total** | | | **~74.1 million** | **~2,431** |

([List of dams in the Missouri River watershed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dams_in_the_Missouri_River_watershed);
[Oahe Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oahe_Dam); [Garrison Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrison_Dam).
Garrison's capacity is given as 515 MW on the list page and 583.3 MW on the dam page — see Uncertainties.)

- **None of these dams has a navigation lock.** Commercial barge navigation on the Missouri therefore runs
  only from **Sioux City, Iowa, downstream to St. Louis — about 730 miles** — maintained as a self-scouring
  channel by bank revetment, dikes, and regulated releases from Gavins Point under the **Bank Stabilization
  and Navigation Project.** Everything above Sioux City is a chain of reservoirs, not a highway.
- This has a consequence the author should use: **Bismarck, North Dakota, sits on the Missouri and is
  nonetheless landlocked**, walled off by Garrison Dam upstream and Oahe Dam downstream. So is Pierre. The
  upper Missouri is a water *resource*, not a water *route*.
- Missouri River barge tonnage is small — a small fraction of Mississippi or Ohio traffic — because the
  channel is narrow, the season short, and the shippers few. I could not verify a current tonnage figure in
  this session (see §11).

### 5.2 The Mississippi

- **2,340 miles**, draining **1,151,000 square miles — nearly 40% of the landmass of the contiguous United
  States.** Discharge 200,000–700,000 cubic feet per second.
  ([Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River))
- **Upper Mississippi:** **29 locks and dams**, from Upper St. Anthony Falls in Minneapolis down to Chain of
  Rocks at Granite City, Illinois, maintaining a **9-foot navigation channel** over roughly 850 miles.
  **Authorized by Congress in the 1930s and mostly complete by 1940.** Total lift across the system is about
  **404 feet.** ([Upper Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Mississippi_River))
- **This is the structurally decisive fact about Midwestland's water transport.** Above St. Louis the
  Mississippi is not a river in the navigational sense — it is a **staircase of slackwater pools held up by
  concrete.** If the locks fail, the Upper Mississippi reverts to a shallow, shifting, rapids-punctuated
  stream that will not float a loaded barge. **Below the Missouri confluence at St. Louis there are no locks
  at all** — the Middle and Lower Mississippi are open river, needing only dredging and revetment.
- The head of navigation was Upper St. Anthony Falls in Minneapolis; that lock was closed in 2015 (to block
  invasive carp), so practical commercial navigation now begins downstream of the falls in the Twin Cities.
  *(The closure is widely reported but was not confirmed on the page I fetched — see §11.)*
- **At Cairo, Illinois, the Ohio carries more water than the Middle Mississippi does.** Cairo is where
  Midwestland's river and the Appalachian nations' river become one.

### 5.3 Barge economics — why this matters so much

From [Inland waterways of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_waterways_of_the_United_States):

- **More than 25,000 miles** of navigable inland waters; USACE maintains about 12,000 miles.
- **About 630 million tons a year**, roughly **17% of US intercity freight by volume.**
- **A 15-barge tow carries about 22,500 tons — the equivalent of about 225 rail cars or 870 tractor-trailers.**
  Mississippi tows can exceed 40 barges.
- **Fuel efficiency: one gallon moves a ton about 180–240 miles by truck, 450 miles by rail, and 514 miles by
  barge.**
- **191 commercially active lock sites with 237 chambers**; about 60% of chambers are 600–999 ft and only
  about 15% are 1,000–1,200 ft — so most of the system still locks a 15-barge tow through in two cuts.
- **Grain accounts for roughly 60% of US farm exports moving by barge.**

The gravity point the brief raised is real and should be stated precisely: **downstream is free, upstream is
not.** A loaded barge or raft drifts to the Gulf on the current. Getting it back requires a towboat and fuel.
Before steam, Mississippi commerce was one-way — flatboats went down, were broken up for lumber at New
Orleans, and the crews walked home. **A post-collapse Midwestland can export grain downriver with no engines
at all, and cannot import anything back up the same way.** That asymmetry should shape its entire trade
posture.

### 5.4 Lake Superior and the Great Lakes outlet

- **Lake Superior: 31,700 square miles of surface, about 2,900 cubic miles of water — the largest freshwater
  lake in the world by area.** ([Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior))
- **Duluth–Superior is the most inland point on the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence Seaway system.** It handled over
  **29 million short tons in 2022** and ranks about **20th among US ports by tonnage.**
  ([Duluth–Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duluth%E2%80%93Superior))
- The outlet from Superior is the **St. Marys River and the Soo Locks** at Sault Ste. Marie, dropping 25 feet
  to Lake Huron. **The Poe Lock (1,200 × 110 × 32 ft) is the only chamber that can pass the 1,000-foot lakers**
  that carry up to **72,000 short tons** of cargo; the MacArthur Lock is 800 × 80 × 29.5 ft. About 10,000 ship
  transits a year. A second Poe-sized lock is under construction, slated for around 2030.
  ([Soo Locks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soo_Locks))
- Commercial navigation on Superior **closes from roughly mid-January to late March** for ice.

**Strategic reading for the author:** Midwestland has **two water outlets to the ocean and controls the
downstream half of neither.** The Mississippi route passes Cairo and 1,000 miles of Lower Mississippi held by
other nations. The Great Lakes route passes the Soo (in Michigan), the Welland Canal and the St. Lawrence
locks (in Canada/Quebec territory on Woodard's map). **River access is a dependency, not a guarantee.** This
is the single best structural argument for why Midwestland would be a food-rich nation with chronically
anxious foreign policy, and why it would care intensely about St. Louis, Cairo, and Duluth.

## 6. Minerals, Metals, and Energy

### 6.1 Iron — the Mesabi Range, and the trap inside it

Minnesota's **Mesabi Range** runs about **110 miles across northeastern Minnesota**, from Babbitt to Grand
Rapids, in Itasca and St. Louis counties. Ore was found in 1890 and commercial mining began in 1892; it became
the chief iron ore district in the United States. ([Mesabi Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesabi_Range))

**USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025, Iron Ore**
([PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-iron-ore.pdf)):

- In 2024, **eight open-pit mines in Michigan, Minnesota and Utah**, each with an attached concentration and
  pelletizing plant, shipped 98% of domestic usable iron ore.
- **US production 2024 (est.): 48.0 million metric tons usable ore, containing 30.0 million tons of iron.**
  Value about $5.5 billion; about 5,000 people employed across mines and plants.
- US exports 10.0 Mt, imports 2.6 Mt. The US produced an estimated **1.8% of world iron ore output** (world
  total ~2,500 Mt, dominated by Australia 930 Mt and Brazil 440 Mt).
- **US reserves: 3,600 Mt crude ore / 2,300 Mt iron content. US resources: 110 billion tons of usable ore
  containing about 27 billion tons of iron.**
- And then the sentence that matters: **"U.S. resources are mainly low-grade taconite-type ores from the Lake
  Superior district that require beneficiation and agglomeration prior to commercial use."**

**Here is the trap.** The high-grade natural hematite that could be dug and shipped straight to a blast
furnace — the ore that built Pittsburgh — was substantially worked out by the mid-twentieth century. By 1965 a
third of Mesabi output was already taconite, and today it is **exclusively taconite.** Taconite is a hard,
banded, chert-hosted magnetite rock at roughly a quarter to a third iron. Turning it into something a furnace
can use requires drilling and blasting, multi-stage crushing, grinding to flour fineness, magnetic separation,
and rolling the concentrate into pellets with a bentonite binder fired at around 1,300 °C. **That is a heavy
industrial plant, not a smithy.**

Current operations on the range: **U.S. Steel's Minntac (Mountain Iron) and Keetac (Keewatin); Hibbing
Taconite; Cleveland-Cliffs' Northshore (Babbitt/Silver Bay) and United Taconite (Eveleth/Forbes); and Minorca
(Biwabik/Gilbert).** Ore moves by **Canadian National and BNSF** to docks at **Duluth and Two Harbors** and
then by lake freighter to mills in Indiana and Ohio — **all of which are outside Midwestland.** In May 2024,
one operator began producing DR-grade pellets at Hibbing at 4 million tons/year capacity and 67%+ iron.
*(Minorca's current ownership — historically ArcelorMittal, since consolidated — is uncertain in my sources;
see §11.)*

**Three real offsets the author can use:**

1. **Scrap is the actual iron source.** This territory is carpeted in steel — rail, bridges, grain bins,
   machinery, vehicles, structural steel in every town. Re-melting scrap is vastly easier than reducing ore
   and needs no ore at all. The USGS notes recycling of iron ore itself is "none," but that is because scrap
   is counted separately — and scrap is where most American steel already comes from.
2. **Bog iron and surface limonite.** Wetland bog iron and residual surface hematite can be smelted in a
   charcoal bloomery, which is Iron Age technology. Low volume, entirely real, and available across northern
   Minnesota.
3. **Tailings.** The USGS notes that "technological advancements have been made that allow hematite to be
   recovered from tailings basins and pelletized." A century of Mesabi tailings basins is a low-grade but
   pre-crushed resource.

### 6.2 Lead — the Viburnum Trend, and why it is the crown jewel

This is the most interesting mineral fact in the entire territory, and it should be treated as such.

**USGS MCS 2025, Lead** ([PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-lead.pdf)):

- **Lead was produced domestically by five lead mines in Missouri**, plus as a byproduct at two Alaskan zinc
  mines and two Idaho silver mines. That is the entire US lead mining industry.
- US mine production 2024 (est.): **300,000 metric tons lead in concentrates** (290,000 t recoverable),
  against world production of 4,300,000 t (China 1,900,000 t). US reserves 4,600,000 t.
- **Primary refinery production: zero, in every year 2020 through 2024.** The summary states plainly:
  **"Nearly all lead concentrate production has been exported since the last primary lead refinery closed in
  2013."**
- Secondary (recycled) refinery production: about **1.0 million metric tons**, equal to **70% of apparent US
  consumption**, almost all from spent lead-acid batteries.
- **Lead-acid batteries account for about 86% of US lead consumption.**

The geology, from
[Southeast Missouri Lead District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Missouri_Lead_District) and
[Doe Run Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doe_Run_Company):

- A **Mississippi Valley-type deposit** hosted in the dolomite of the Upper Cambrian **Bonneterre Formation**,
  mineralized by hydrothermal brines during the Permian. It contains **the world's highest concentration of
  galena.**
- **The Old Lead Belt** (Park Hills, Desloge, Bonne Terre) produced from **1864 to 1972** — 108 years — and
  yielded over **8.5 million tons of lead.** Lead had been worked in the region since the **1720s.**
- **The Viburnum Trend** was discovered in **1955** and opened in **1960**: a **64 km long mineralized shoot,
  average width 150 m, thickness 3–30 m, average depth 300 m**, with roughly **315 million tons of ore
  extracted since 1960.**
- It supplies about **70% of US primary lead.** Historically, roughly **90% of US primary lead supply** has
  come from this Missouri ore.
- Doe Run operates **Brushy Creek, Casteel, Fletcher/West Fork, Sweetwater, and Buick/Mine No. 29.** The
  **Herculaneum primary smelter closed in December 2013** under an EPA consent decree carrying a $65 million
  penalty. The **Buick Resource Recycling Division at Boss, Missouri, processes about 13.5 million lead-acid
  batteries a year.**
- Associated economic metals: **zinc (sphalerite), copper (chalcopyrite), and silver**, with sub-economic
  cadmium, nickel and cobalt.

**Why this is the crown jewel for a post-collapse nation.** Lead is the one strategically critical metal in
this territory that a low-technology society can genuinely produce. Galena is shallow, visually obvious, and
smelts at low temperature — it was worked here with hand tools and log furnaces from the 1720s, long before
any industrial infrastructure existed. And lead's uses map almost perfectly onto what a reconstruction-era
society needs: **lead-acid batteries** (the only rechargeable chemistry buildable without a modern chemical
industry, and already 86% of demand), **bullets and shot**, **pipe and sheet**, **solder**, **type metal for
printing**, **radiation shielding** (of obvious relevance in the author's setting), and glazes and paints.

Two further points the author can exploit. First, **the United States currently has no primary lead smelting
capacity at all** — so there is no plant to inherit; it must be rebuilt, and it *can* be rebuilt at
eighteenth-century technology. Second, in a continent where every neighbor also wants batteries and bullets,
**whoever holds Viburnum holds something nobody else can make.** That is a better lever than grain in some
ways, because grain can be grown elsewhere badly and lead cannot be mined elsewhere at all.

### 6.3 Salt

**USGS MCS 2025, Salt** ([PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-salt.pdf)): US production
2024 about **40 million metric tons**, from 26 companies at 64 plants in 16 states. **The top producing states
are Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, New York, Ohio, Texas and Utah** — together about 95% of US output. By type:
brine 42%, **rock salt 40%**, solar 9%, vacuum pan 9%. Uses: highway deicing 41%, chemical industry 39%,
distributors 9%, food processing 4%, agricultural 3%. Rock salt is cheap — about **$56 per metric ton.**

**Kansas's Permian salt beds (Hutchinson and Lyons) put Midwestland on the list of top US salt producers.**
For a society without refrigeration, this is not a minor commodity. Salt is meat preservation, hide tanning,
butter and cheese, livestock licks, and the feedstock for every chlorine and sodium chemical. Rock salt is
mined, not manufactured, and it does not require industry to be useful. **This is a genuinely strategic,
genuinely survivable asset inside the borders.**

### 6.4 Oil, gas, and coal

- **North Dakota / Bakken–Three Forks.** North Dakota reached about **1 million barrels per day by April
  2014** and peaked near **1.15 million bpd in mid-2015.** It was the **second-largest oil-producing state
  from April 2012 to February 2021** and is now third behind Texas and New Mexico. USGS estimated in 2013 that
  **7.4 billion barrels** are technically recoverable. This production depends entirely on **hydraulic
  fracturing and horizontal drilling**, with steep per-well decline — average per-well output fell from 145
  bbl/day (2010) to 115 (2015). Associated gas is substantially flared for want of pipeline.
  ([Bakken Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakken_Formation)) **This is the most purely industrial
  resource in the territory: without continuous drilling, fracturing, and pipe, it produces nothing.**
- **Kansas / Hugoton Gas Field.** Spanning Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas; discovered 1922, developed from 1927.
  **Cumulative production of about 18.98 trillion cubic feet through 2021**, but it is deep in decline —
  **from 110 Bcf in 2015 to 35 Bcf in 2021, a 68% fall in six years.** Its gas carries an unusually high
  **0.3–1.9% helium**, making the Hugoton fields the largest US helium reserve, piped to storage at Amarillo,
  Texas. ([Hugoton Gas Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugoton_Gas_Field))
- **North Dakota lignite.** The Fort Union lignite of the Williston Basin is shallow, low-rank, low-energy,
  crumbly, and burned essentially at the mine mouth. The EIA generation data below shows coal supplying 54.5%
  of North Dakota's electricity, which is consistent with mine-mouth lignite plants. *(I could not verify
  current lignite tonnage or individual mine output in this session — see §11.)* **Note that the Powder River
  Basin, the largest US coal source, is in Wyoming and Montana — outside this territory.** Missouri, Iowa and
  Kansas have historic shallow bituminous coal fields, largely abandoned, which were originally worked by pick
  and shovel — relevant for a low-technology revival.

For the author, this is a tidy irony: **the territory's present electrical system is the most collapse-fragile
of any major US region** — modern wind turbines need composite blades, gearboxes, power electronics and a
stiff grid; nuclear needs a fuel cycle nobody will have. Its **hydro, at roughly 2,400 MW on the Missouri
mainstem, is the most collapse-survivable generation technology in existence**, and it is all inside the
borders except Fort Peck.

And there is a historical rhyme available: the technology that actually settled the dry half of this territory
was the **mechanical water-pumping windmill** — a simple steel or wooden fan on a tower, driving a reciprocating
pump rod down a shallow well. That is rebuildable in any blacksmith shop. Electric generation is not.

### 6.6 What the territory does NOT have

This list is as important as the list of what it does have:

- **No phosphate rock.** (US supply: Florida, Idaho, North Carolina, Utah.)
- **No potash.** (US supply: New Mexico, Utah; world supply: Saskatchewan. US is 93% import-reliant.)
- **No primary lead smelter** (the last US one closed in 2013 — though the ore is here).
- **No bauxite or aluminum production.**
- **No copper in economic quantity.** *(Minnesota's Duluth Complex holds very large undeveloped copper-nickel
  resources at NorthMet and Twin Metals — real, but never mined; see §11.)*
- **No tin, chromium, manganese, nickel, or rare earths in production.**
- **No sulfur production of consequence** — and sulfuric acid is the workhorse of the chemical industry.
- **No high-grade direct-shipping iron ore** (only taconite; see §6.1).
- **No natural gas of consequence outside Kansas's declining Hugoton and North Dakota's associated gas** — and
  natural gas is the feedstock for synthetic nitrogen.
- **No seaport.** The nearest tidewater is 1,000 river miles past Cairo, or through three foreign lock systems
  from Duluth.

## 7. Industry, Processing, and Manufacturing

With the re-scope, **the manufacturing belt is outside Midwestland.** The steel of northwest Indiana and
Cleveland, the auto plants of Michigan and Ohio, the machine tools of the Ohio Valley — all now belong to
other nations on the author's map. What remains inside the borders is overwhelmingly **agriculture-derived
industry**, which is a meaningfully different economic character:

- **Meatpacking.** The great plants follow the feedlots: Dakota City (Nebraska), Garden City and Dodge City
  (Kansas), and a dense network across Iowa, Nebraska, Minnesota and South Dakota. The micropolitan
  populations of **Garden City (38,354) and Dodge City (34,072)** exist essentially because of beef.
- **Grain milling, soybean crushing, and corn wet milling / ethanol.** Fuel ethanol consumes a very large
  share of the US corn crop — USDA ERS puts fuel ethanol at **"nearly 45 percent of total corn use"**
  ([ERS, Feed Grains Sector at a Glance](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/corn-and-other-feed-grains/feed-grains-sector-at-a-glance))
  — and roughly a third of each bushel comes back as **distillers grains**, a livestock feed. Iowa, Nebraska,
  Minnesota and South Dakota are among the leading ethanol states.
- **Agricultural equipment.** John Deere's largest works are at **Waterloo and Des Moines, Iowa**, with
  Moline, Illinois (outside) as corporate headquarters. This matters: Midwestland can build tractors, for as
  long as it can make steel and diesel engines.
- **Refining.** Inside the territory: **El Dorado, McPherson and Coffeyville (Kansas); Mandan and Dickinson
  (North Dakota); Pine Bend/Rosemount and St. Paul Park (Minnesota).** *(Individual capacities were not
  verified in this session — see §11.)*
- **Nitrogen fertilizer plants.** A small number sit inside the territory, notably in Iowa (Wever and Port
  Neal) and at **Beulah, North Dakota**, where the **Great Plains Synfuels Plant is unusual in making ammonia
  and urea from lignite coal rather than natural gas.** That last one is worth the author's attention: it is a
  real, operating demonstration that nitrogen fixation can be run off coal instead of gas, and it sits on top
  of a lignite field. *(Capacities unverified — see §11.)*

Overall: **this is a processing economy, not a capital-goods economy.** It turns grain and cattle into flour,
meat, oil, alcohol and feed. It does not, on its own, build the machines that do so.

## 8. Transport

### 8.1 Rail

**Chicago is outside this territory**, which is a genuine strategic problem rather than a footnote: Chicago is
the point where the western railroads (BNSF, Union Pacific) historically interchange with the eastern ones,
and roughly a quarter of all US rail freight passes through it. Midwestland's rail network is built to *feed*
a hub it does not own.

Its internal hubs are:

- **Kansas City** — the largest rail center inside the territory by a wide margin, on the Missouri at the
  Kansas River confluence, and the junction where BNSF, Union Pacific, Norfolk Southern, CSX and Canadian
  Pacific Kansas City all meet. It is widely cited as the second-busiest US rail hub after Chicago.
  *(That ranking is commonly repeated but I could not confirm it against a primary source in this session —
  see §11.)*
- **St. Louis** — the second great interchange, at the Mississippi–Missouri confluence, and the place where
  the locked river ends and the open river begins.
- **Omaha / Council Bluffs** — Union Pacific's headquarters and the original eastern terminus of the first
  transcontinental railroad.
- **Minneapolis–St. Paul** — the northern hub, historically the milling capital, and the junction for
  Canadian National and Canadian Pacific Kansas City traffic.
- **Duluth / Two Harbors** — the ore railroads, moving taconite from the Mesabi to the lake docks.
- Secondary: **Fargo, Sioux City, Wichita, Des Moines.**

Rail moves a ton about **450 miles per gallon** (against 514 by barge and 180–240 by truck), and grain moves
in **110-car shuttle trains** either west to Pacific Northwest export or east and south to the river.

The collapse profile of rail is worth stating precisely: **the grade is permanent, the track is not.** Cuts,
fills, embankments and bridge piers are earthworks and masonry that last centuries with minimal care. Rail
steel, ties, welded joints, signals and locomotives are consumables. A reconstruction-era Midwestland would
plausibly retain the *alignments* — the routes would still be there, visible, gradable — while the operating
railroad shrank to whatever length of track it could actually maintain, potentially horse-drawn or
steam-hauled on the best lines.

### 8.2 The interaction that defines the territory

Rail runs east–west; the rivers run north–south into the Gulf. Every major settlement in the territory sits
where those two systems cross. **Which cities sit on navigable water is therefore the most consequential
geographic fact about the urban network**, and it splits cleanly:

**On navigable water:** Minneapolis–St. Paul (Upper Mississippi, head of navigation), Davenport/Quad Cities,
Dubuque, St. Louis (Mississippi–Missouri confluence), Kansas City (Missouri), Omaha/Council Bluffs (Missouri),
St. Joseph (Missouri), Sioux City (Missouri, head of navigation), Jefferson City (Missouri), **Duluth**
(Lake Superior).

**Not on navigable water:** Des Moines, Wichita, Lincoln, Sioux Falls, Fargo, Topeka, Springfield MO, Columbia
MO, Rochester MN, St. Cloud, Cedar Rapids, Rapid City, and — despite sitting directly on the Missouri —
**Bismarck and Pierre**, both walled off by the mainstem dams.

## 10. POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

The structural paradox of this territory: **it has the food, and therefore the leverage — but the modern
version of that food depends on inputs that a collapse severs at the source, and the surplus it can still grow
has to leave by rivers it does not control downstream.**

### (a) Survives regardless — soil, rainfall, grass, river geography

These are not infrastructure. They cannot be bombed out of existence or allowed to decay.

- **156.5 million acres of cropland, the majority of it deep mollisol** over loess and glacial till, with a
  mollic horizon 24–31 inches thick and 3–7% organic matter. Ten thousand years of prairie made it; nothing in
  a 150-year collapse unmakes it.
- **Rainfall east of the 100th meridian** — roughly 30 to 47 inches a year across Missouri, Iowa, southern
  Minnesota, eastern Nebraska and eastern Kansas. This is *demonstrably* enough for corn, wheat, oats, hay,
  and pasture with no irrigation whatsoever: it was how the entire Corn Belt worked before 1950, and it is how
  Iowa's 2.63 billion bushels are grown *today* (Iowa irrigates 165,000 acres out of 26 million).
- **78.5 million acres of pastureland in farms**, plus the Sandhills (~20,000 sq mi that has never been
  plowed) and the western shortgrass plains. Cattle on grass need no grain, no fertilizer, and no fuel.
  **Missouri's 1.86 million beef cows — second in the nation — are a grass-and-hills operation that barely
  changes.**
- **Downstream river transport.** The Missouri and Mississippi flow to the Gulf under gravity. Below St. Louis
  there is not a single lock. A raft or barge of grain reaches New Orleans without an engine, exactly as it did
  from the 1790s to the 1820s.
- **Lake Superior** as a body of water, and the Duluth harbor as a harbor.
- **Shallow, rich lead ore** in southeast Missouri, worked by hand from the 1720s.
- **Rock salt** in the Kansas Permian beds.
- **Limestone** for lime, mortar, and soil pH, quarried across Missouri, Iowa and Kansas.
- **Timber** along the river bottoms and across northern Minnesota; 4.19 million acres of woodland in
  Missouri farms alone (Ozark mast country — good hog country).

### (b) Depends on infrastructure, plausibly repairable

Things that need maintenance and industry, but where the hard part is already built and the engineering is
within reach of a recovering society.

- **The Upper Mississippi lock system.** Twenty-nine locks and dams of 1930s mass concrete, lifting a total of
  404 feet over 850 miles — about 14 feet per lock, which is modest. The concrete monoliths will outlast the
  gates, valves, and machinery by a very long way. **This is the single highest-value repair target in the
  territory**, because it is what reconnects Minneapolis, the Quad Cities and Dubuque to St. Louis and the
  open river. Expect it to fail first and be restored in stages from the bottom up.
- **Missouri River navigation to Sioux City.** Much harder than the Mississippi. The 730-mile channel is not
  held by locks but by continuous dredging, revetment, dikes, and regulated releases from Gavins Point. Without
  that maintenance the Missouri re-braids into shifting sandbars within years. Realistically this degrades to
  seasonal, shallow-draft, partial navigation — which is still worth a great deal, since it is how Kansas
  City and Omaha reach water.
- **Missouri mainstem hydropower — about 2,400 MW across six dams, five inside the territory.** Hydro turbines
  are the most repairable large-scale generation technology that exists: low-speed machinery, no fuel cycle, no
  exotic materials. The earth-fill embankments need spillway care, and Gavins Point and Big Bend are simple
  run-of-river-ish structures. **This is the realistic backbone of any restored electrical grid** — and note
  that South Dakota already draws 22% of its power from it.
- **Rail alignments.** Grades, cuts, fills and bridge piers persist; rail, ties and motive power do not.
  Expect a much shorter network on the best routes.
- **Grain elevators, flour mills, oilseed presses, slaughterhouses, tanneries.** Mechanically simple, locally
  repairable, and the economic core of every river town.
- **Shallow coal.** Missouri, Iowa and Kansas bituminous fields were originally worked by pick and shovel;
  North Dakota lignite is shallow and can be dug with minimal equipment (it is just poor fuel, and does not
  travel).
- **Mechanical water-pumping windmills** on the plains — steel fan, tower, pump rod, shallow well. Blacksmith
  technology, and historically the thing that made the dry half habitable at all.
- **Limestone burning** for agricultural lime and lime mortar — a genuinely pre-industrial process, and
  important because sustained cropping acidifies these soils.
- **Charcoal bloomery iron** from bog ore, and above all **scrap re-melting**, which is the real iron supply.

### (c) Purely artifacts of industry and global trade — these simply vanish

- **Synthetic nitrogen.** Haber–Bosch needs 300–500 °C, 150–250 bar, promoted iron catalyst, and 3–5% of the
  world's natural gas. 55% of US ammonia capacity is in Louisiana, Oklahoma and Texas — outside the territory.
  Midwestland's corn crop alone currently needs on the order of **3.4 million short tons of nitrogen a year.**
  That number goes to approximately zero.
- **Phosphate and potash.** **Zero domestic production in all seven states.** Phosphate comes from Florida,
  Idaho, North Carolina and Utah; potash from New Mexico, Utah and above all Saskatchewan, with the US 93%
  import-reliant. Unlike nitrogen there is no biological substitute — only recycling manure, bone, ash and
  night soil, which returns what is already in the system and no more.
- **Hybrid corn seed.** 99% of Corn Belt corn has been hybrid since 1950 and hybrids do not breed true. The
  seed industry — inbred line maintenance, isolation, detasseling, drying, treating, cold storage — is an
  industrial pipeline. **But note the asymmetry: soybeans and wheat are self-pollinated and DO breed true, and
  can be saved and replanted at full yield.**
- **GMO traits** (Bt, herbicide tolerance) and the herbicides that make them useful.
- **Diesel mechanization.** This collapses *planted area*, not just yield: from 300–1,500 acres per operator to
  roughly 40–80 acres per family with draft animals, with a fifth to a quarter of the cropland diverted to
  feeding the animals.
- **Ogallala irrigation.** Nebraska's 9.58 million irrigated acres and Kansas's 3.12 million are pumped —
  **6,090 and 2,680 million gallons a day respectively, roughly 89% and 96% of it groundwater**, lifted 100 to
  300 feet by engine-driven pumps onto center pivots. Windmills lift stock water from shallow wells; they do
  not run a quarter-section pivot. **Everything west of the 100th meridian that is currently row crop reverts
  to grass.**
- **The 45%-of-the-corn-crop ethanol industry** and the distillers grains that feed back into the feedlots.
- **Confinement hog production at 45.5 million head** — ventilation, antibiotics, closed genetics, and
  slaughter lines. (Pasture and woodlot hogs persist at a much smaller scale; Missouri's Ozark mast country is
  natural hog range.)
- **The feedlot system at ~7.2 million head on feed** in this territory, which exists only because corn is
  free-flowing and cheap.
- **Bakken oil.** Fracked horizontal wells declining 20%+ a year need continuous re-drilling. Stop drilling
  and it stops, fast.
- **Taconite iron.** Drilling, blasting, fine grinding, magnetic separation, pelletizing at 1,300 °C. The
  Mesabi becomes a mountain of rock nobody can use.
- **Modern wind generation** — composite blades, gearboxes, power electronics, and a stiff synchronous grid.
  **This is the territory's largest present power source (37.9%) and one of its least recoverable.**
- **Nuclear generation** in Minnesota, Nebraska, Kansas and Missouri.
- **Long-distance refrigeration, propane grain drying, and the export logistics** that currently move 60% of
  US farm exports by barge.

### (d) The synthesis — what Midwestland actually is after the collapse

**It is still the breadbasket, at roughly a tenth of the volume, and with far more strategic leverage than
volume alone suggests.**

Concretely, the picture the author can build on:

1. **The wet half is a functioning agricultural civilization from day one.** Missouri, Iowa, southern
   Minnesota, eastern Nebraska and eastern Kansas grow corn, wheat, oats, and hay on rain and mollisol with
   rotation and manure, at something like **30–50 bu/ac corn and 18–25 bu/ac wheat.** That is exactly what
   this land did for the seventy years from 1866 to 1936, and the record shows it was stable.
2. **The dry half reverts to what it was before 1950: cattle country.** Western Kansas, western Nebraska, and
   the western Dakotas go back to grass. This is not a disaster — it is a large, durable protein economy that
   needs almost no inputs — but it supports a fraction of the population and it produces hides, tallow, dried
   and salted beef, and horses rather than grain.
3. **The internal fault line becomes a political fact.** A wet, dense, river-oriented east and a dry, empty,
   horse-and-cattle west, in one nation, with the 100th meridian between them and no natural reason for the
   west to defer to the east except that the east has the food, the towns, and the rivers. **This is the
   country's central internal tension and it is written into the rainfall map.**
4. **The surplus is real but has to leave by water.** Rafting grain down the Missouri and Mississippi requires
   no engines. Getting anything back upriver does. **Midwestland therefore trades downhill into the hands of
   whoever holds Cairo and the Lower Mississippi**, and its alternative outlet at Duluth passes through three
   foreign lock systems. A nation that can feed a continent and cannot guarantee its own export route is a
   nation that will either build a navy it cannot afford, cut a deal it resents, or fight over St. Louis and
   Cairo. All three are good story.
5. **Lead is the wildcard.** Midwestland is the only place on the continent that can produce lead at
   pre-industrial technology from a world-class deposit. Batteries, bullets, pipe, solder, type, and radiation
   shielding. **Food plus lead is a much stronger hand than food alone**, and it gives the author a second,
   non-agricultural reason for other nations to need this one.
6. **Salt is the quiet one.** Kansas rock salt means Midwestland can preserve the meat that the western half
   produces and the hogs that the eastern half raises. Without salt, a cattle surplus 800 miles from a market
   is not a surplus at all. With it, dried and salted beef and pork become the territory's second export after
   grain.
7. **Iron is the weakness.** Standing on the largest iron resource in North America and being unable to use
   it is a genuinely poignant detail, and the workaround — scrap, bog iron, and tailings — means Midwestland's
   metal supply is literally the recycled bones of the old world, and is finite.
