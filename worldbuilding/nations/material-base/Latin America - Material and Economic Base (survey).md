# Latin America — Material and Economic Base (survey)

**What this file is:** a factual, real-world research baseline covering **South America, Central America, Mexico south
of the Sonora file's scope, and the Caribbean**, compiled 2026-09-23. It is *not* fiction and contains no invented
content. It documents what the ground actually holds — geology, soil, climate, fisheries, water, industry — so the
author can design post-collapse successor nations from an accurate material floor rather than from guesswork — but
this file does not design any of them. It supplies the ground; the nations are derived separately.
The closing **Post-Collapse Assessment** is the only interpretive section, and it reasons *from* the facts above it.

**This is a survey, not a country-by-country buildout.** The aim is to make the *shape* of the continent's material
position legible. Where a country or a deposit is named, it is named because it carries weight in that shape.

**Scope, stated precisely.**

**In scope — everything south of the Sonora file's line:**

- **Mexico**, all of it south of the five northern-tier states — the Bajío, Querétaro, Jalisco, Michoacán,
  Guerrero, Zacatecas, Durango, San Luis Potosí, Veracruz, Oaxaca, Chiapas, the Isthmus, Yucatán, and the Campeche
  offshore.
- **All of Central America** — Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama.
- **The Caribbean islands** — Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, Trinidad and Tobago, and the Lesser Antilles.
- **All of South America, including the Guianas** — Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana are in scope, and Guyana in
  particular is now materially significant (§1.6, §2.7).

**Explicitly out of scope:**

- **Sonora and Chihuahua**, plus **Arizona and New Mexico** — the four territories of La República de Sonora,
  covered in `Sonora - Real-World Material Base.md`. That material is not repeated here; where the two files
  touch, this one says so.
  ⚠️ **Corrected by the author 2026-09-23.** An earlier version of this line also excluded **Coahuila, Nuevo
  León and Tamaulipas**. Those three are **unpartitioned — in scope, claimed by nobody.** They are already
  researched in detail in the Sonora file (Monclova and AHMSA, the Sabinas coal basin, the Burgos gas basin,
  Monterrey, the Comarca Lagunera, Saltillo/Ramos Arizpe, Met-Mex Peñoles at Torreón); read that material as a
  dossier on unclaimed ground rather than as another nation's assets.
- **Both Bajas — Baja California and Baja California Sur**, which are Cascadia's. **The Gulf of California is
  therefore an international frontier, not an internal sea**, and its western shore is outside this survey entirely.
  This matches the Sonora file's treatment; see its §0.

**Two constraints on what this file is allowed to do, and they are absolute:**

1. **This is a survey of material geography only. It proposes no nations, no borders and no map.** What is where, and
   what survives a collapse — nothing else. Deriving polities from this ground is the author's work, separately.
2. **No in-world Latin American polity exists yet.** None is named in the setting, and none sits in the Assembly of
   Nations. Everything below therefore describes **real-world territory** with no fictional owner attached. Where this
   file says "the continent" or names a modern country, that is a real-world geographic referent, not a successor
   state.

**Standing constraint observed:** the author's **"No National Stereotypes"** rule. Every territory here is treated in
the same register — ore grades, hectares, tonnages, megawatts, ECI values. No cultural characterization, no governance
or political-temperament assumptions, no security framing. Where a human-conflict or institutional fact appears, it
appears because it is a *material* fact about who physically controls an ore body, a river, a canal, or a smelter, and
it is stated neutrally.

## 0. Orientation — the shape of the thing

Four sub-regions, materially very different from one another:

1. **The Andean spine and the Southern Cone** — the world's greatest copper province, the lithium triangle, the silver
   that financed an empire, the Humboldt Current, the potato's homeland, and the Pampas. Mineral wealth of the first
   rank; industrial depth of the third.
2. **Brazil** — a subcontinent on its own, and the only territory in the hemisphere holding **iron ore, its own steel
   alloying elements, bauxite, and continent-scale hydropower at the same time**. Also the one economy on this list
   whose productive sophistication has measurably *fallen* over a generation.
3. **Mexico south of Sonora, and the Central American isthmus** — Mexico's domestic industrial core and its most
   productive farmland, plus a narrow land bridge whose single most valuable physical object is a canal.
4. **The Caribbean** — small, import-dependent, with two material exceptions worth naming (Jamaican bauxite,
   Trinidadian gas), and the region most exposed to any interruption of shipping.

**The one-sentence headline, argued in §6:** Latin America is poor in *productive know-how* and extraordinarily rich
in *material endowment*, and a collapse inverts the value of those two things — which would make the southern half of
the hemisphere, not the northern, the part that can still make steel.

## 1. Economic Complexity — the Quantified Backbone

### 1.1 What ECI is, and why it is worth putting first

The **Economic Complexity Index** ranks economies by the diversity and ubiquity of what they successfully export — in
effect, a measure of how much productive *know-how* a society has accumulated and can combine. A country that exports
many things which few other countries can make scores high. A country that exports one thing which many countries
could make scores low. It is not a measure of wealth, and it is emphatically **not a measure of what is in the
ground.** That distinction is the hinge of this entire file.

All values below are from the **Observatory of Economic Complexity**, HS92 four-digit cube, retrieved directly from
the OEC API on 2026-09-23
([OEC Economic Complexity data](https://oec.world/en/resources/bulk-download/international?category=ECI&topic=Complexity)).
The 2022 column matches the author's existing figures exactly, so the series is a clean extension of what is already
on file rather than a separate estimate.

### 1.3 Mexico — the outlier, and a score that is partly borrowed

**Mexico is the only Latin American economy in the world's upper tier**, and by a wide margin: +0.914 in 2024 against
a regional runner-up of +0.424. It is also the only one whose rise looks like a genuine industrial transformation —
from +0.305 in 1995 to a **peak of +1.087 in 2015**, then a slow drift down over the following decade.

**But say plainly what that score is made of.** Mexico's 2023 merchandise exports totaled about **US$621 billion**,
and the top items were
([OEC, BACI HS92, 2023](https://oec.world/en/profile/country/mex)):

| Product | Value | Share of exports |
|---|---|---|
| Cars | $63.1B | 10.2% |
| Motor vehicle parts | $41.4B | 6.7% |
| Delivery trucks | $38.2B | 6.2% |
| Crude petroleum | $34.6B | 5.6% |
| Computers | $29.3B | 4.7% |
| Medical instruments | $18.1B | 2.9% |
| Insulated wire | $17.9B | 2.9% |
| Tractors | $14.2B | 2.3% |

Mexico's **top five products are only 33.3% of its exports** — by far the most diversified export basket in Latin
America, and the direct arithmetic cause of its ECI score. But cars, vehicle parts, delivery trucks, computers and
insulated wire are the output of **cross-border assembly integration**, not of a self-contained domestic industrial
system. The complexity is real, and it is measured at the border; what it measures is Mexico's position inside a
North American production network, not the depth of an independent Mexican one.

**The consequence for the author is direct and severe: a collapse that severs the northern border does not reduce
Mexico's ECI, it deletes the thing the ECI was measuring.** Mexico's post-collapse worth has to be assessed from its
farmland, its silver, its limestone and its rivers — not from this number. This is the single most important caveat
in the section.

### 1.4 Brazil's slide — the most important trend line on the continent

**+0.610 in 2000 → +0.003 in 2023 → −0.054 in 2024.** Brazil has fallen out of positive complexity territory
entirely, after a quarter-century of continuous decline. A 2022 snapshot (+0.042) shows a middling score; the series
shows a **direction**, and the direction is the fact.

The trade data says exactly what happened. Brazil's 2023 exports of about **US$358.9 billion**
([OEC, BACI HS92, 2023](https://oec.world/en/profile/country/bra)):

| Product | Value | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Soybeans | $53.2B | 14.8% |
| Crude petroleum | $46.0B | 12.8% |
| Iron ore | $35.2B | 9.8% |
| Raw sugar | $16.4B | 4.6% |
| Corn | $14.7B | 4.1% |
| Soybean meal | $10.9B | 3.0% |
| Refined petroleum | $9.5B | 2.6% |
| Wood pulp | $9.2B | 2.6% |

**Every one of Brazil's top eight exports is a primary commodity.** The top five are 46.1% of all exports. This is
re-primarization: an economy that in 2000 was exporting a meaningful volume of manufactured goods now earns its
foreign exchange from soybeans, crude, iron ore and sugar. The manufacturing has not vanished — Embraer still builds
airliners, §5.1 — but it has shrunk relative to the commodity trade, and ECI measures exactly that ratio.

**Why this matters for a post-collapse setting, and it is not the obvious reading:** the things Brazil has shifted
*toward* — iron ore, grain, sugar — are the things that still have value when global trade stops. The ECI decline
measures a loss of sophistication that is real, but it is a loss of *export sophistication*, and the physical assets
underneath it are the most collapse-durable on the continent. Brazil scores worse each year on a metric that is
measuring the wrong thing for the author's purposes.

### 1.5 The commodity floor — and the central irony

**Chile is the world's largest copper producer and scores −0.227. Peru is second in copper and holds the world's
largest silver reserves, and scores −0.715. Bolivia sits on one of the two largest lithium resources on Earth, plus
tin and silver, and scores −0.940 — last in the region.**

The export data shows the mechanism with no ambiguity
([OEC, BACI HS92, 2023](https://oec.world/en/profile/country/chl)):

- **Chile** ($100.1B): copper ore 24.1% + refined copper 17.1% = **41.2% of everything Chile sells is copper.** Add
  carbonates (lithium carbonate) 6.8%, fish fillets 4.3%, molybdenum ore 2.4%. Top five = 55.5%.
- **Peru** ($67.1B): copper ore 29.6%, gold 13.3%, refined copper 4.5%. Top five = 53.7%.
- **Bolivia** ($10.8B): gold 23.1%, petroleum gas 20.0%, zinc ore 8.5%, precious metal ore 7.7%, soybean meal 7.6%,
  raw tin 4.1%. Top five = 66.9%.
- **Ecuador** ($37.6B): crude petroleum 34.6%, crustaceans 18.5%, bananas 12.5%. Top five = 73.2%.
- **Venezuela** ($7.7B — note the total): crude petroleum 52.7%, petroleum coke 7.2%, scrap iron 5.5%. Top five =
  74.5%.

**Venezuela deserves its own line.** Total merchandise exports of **US$7.7 billion in 2023** — less than Bolivia, less
than Paraguay, roughly a third of Costa Rica's — from a country sitting on what may be the largest petroleum reserve
on Earth (§4.1). Its ECI bottomed at **−1.026 in 2019** before partially recovering to −0.559. That the third-largest
export item is **scrap iron** is the whole story in one line: an economy dismantling its own installed capital and
selling it. This is the closest thing in the real-world dataset to the author's scenario, and it is worth studying
directly.

**ECI is measuring economic structure, not endowment.** Chile's low score says that Chile has organized itself around
doing one thing superbly. It says nothing whatever about whether Chilean copper exists, and copper does not stop
existing when the market for it does.

### 1.6 The small risers, and two special cases

- **Costa Rica** (−0.497 in 1995 → **+0.424** in 2024) is the region's clearest genuine complexity gain, and unlike
  Mexico's it is not a border artifact. Its 2023 exports ($24.0B): **medical instruments 25.6%, integrated circuits
  11.8%, orthopedic appliances 9.3%**, then bananas 7.2% and tropical fruits 6.9%. A real precision-manufacturing
  cluster on a small agricultural base.
- **Panama** rose to **+0.629 in 2015** and has since fallen back to −0.008. Its merchandise exports are small
  ($6.8B) and now **43.9% copper ore** — the Cobre Panamá mine. Panama's real economy is the canal, which is a
  *service* and therefore does not appear in merchandise trade at all. **Panama's ECI is close to meaningless as a
  description of Panama.** ⚠️ Flagged explicitly.
- **Paraguay** ($12.3B): soybeans 26.8%, then **electricity at 12.3%** — the second-largest export of the country is
  power from Itaipu (§4.4). There is no other economy in the hemisphere structured this way.
- **Guyana** ($16.0B): **crude petroleum 83.5%.** Absent from the ECI dataset, but its merchandise exports now exceed
  Uruguay's, Paraguay's and Bolivia's, entirely on offshore oil discovered in the last decade. The most extreme
  single-commodity dependency in the region, and the most recently created.
- **Trinidad and Tobago** ($12.3B): petroleum gas 30.2%, **ammonia 15.4%**, methanol 14.5%, crude 12.4%, and **direct
  reduced iron 5.0%**. A gas-to-chemicals economy. The ammonia matters — see §4 and §6.

### 1.7 What ECI does and does not predict about rebuilding

ECI is a genuinely good predictor of who can rebuild, but it predicts **one specific thing**: the breadth of
technical and organizational know-how resident in a population. A society that knew how to make many different things
retains more transferable skill than one that knew how to dig one thing very efficiently. On that reading Mexico,
Costa Rica, Brazil and Uruguay start with a real advantage, and Bolivia, Ecuador and Venezuela with a real deficit.

**But the ranking partially inverts over a long enough horizon, for three reasons:**

1. **Know-how decays across generations; geology does not.** A century and a half out, the accumulated know-how
   advantage has largely evaporated. What is left is ore bodies, soil, rainfall and river gradient.
2. **The high scorers are high because they are integrated into a system that is gone.** Mexico's assembly
   complexity and Costa Rica's medical-device cluster are both *externally* coupled. Chile's copper is not coupled to
   anything; it is a mountain.
3. **Some low scorers are low precisely because they hold the things that matter most after a collapse.** Bolivia
   ranks last in the region while holding tin, silver, and the Uyuni brines. Peru ranks third-from-last while holding
   the largest silver reserves on Earth and a share of the world's most productive fishery.

**The right way to use this table is as a measure of how far each society has to fall, not of how high it can climb
back.** The spread the author noticed — Mexico +0.959 against Bolivia −0.902 — is a vast gap in *2026 economic
organization* and a much smaller gap in *2283 material prospects*.

## 2. Minerals

Latin America is one of the two or three great mineral provinces on Earth, and unlike most such provinces it is rich
across a genuinely broad set rather than in one or two commodities. All production and reserve figures below are from
the **USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025**, retrieved and read directly.

**Chile and Peru together produced 7,900 kt in 2024 — about 34% of world mine output — and hold 290,000 kt of
reserves, roughly 30% of the world total.** Add Mexico and Latin America holds about 35% of known world copper.
Chile also refined **1,900 kt** domestically in 2024, so the smelting capability is on the continent, not only the
ore.

**Named operations, with the detail that matters:**

| Mine | Country | Scale / grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Escondida** | Antofagasta, Chile | Largest copper mine in the world by output. 2019 known resource **21.7 billion tonnes at 0.54% Cu**; proven and probable reserves 34.7 Mt of contained copper ([Escondida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escondida)) |
| **Chuquicamata** | Antofagasta, Chile | Worked since 1915; **the Guggenheims' original 1910s estimate was 690 Mt at 2.58% Cu** — see below, this number is load-bearing ([Chuquicamata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuquicamata)) |
| **Collahuasi** | Tarapacá, Chile | **3.93 billion tonnes at 0.66% Cu**; 560 kt produced in 2018. Concentrate ~41% chalcopyrite, 18% chalcocite ([Collahuasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collahuasi)) |
| **El Teniente** | O'Higgins, Chile | The great underground mine; nationalized 1971; New Mine Level project extends it deeper ([El Teniente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Teniente)) |
| **Cerro Verde** | Arequipa, Peru | **4.63 billion tonnes at 0.4% Cu plus 113.2 million oz silver.** Spaniards mined high-grade oxide copper here in the 1800s ([Cerro Verde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Verde_mine)) |
| **Antamina** | Áncash, Peru | Copper–zinc–molybdenum at 4,300 m elevation. **410 kt in 2024, about 15% of Peru's total** ([Antamina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antamina)) |
| **Cobre Panamá** | Panama | 43.9% of Panama's merchandise exports in 2023 |

**The grade trap — and it is the same trap the Sonora file identified, in sharper form.** Modern Andean copper is
mined at **0.4% to 0.66%**, and Escondida at 0.54%. That is economic only because of diesel, grid electricity,
sulfuric acid, flotation reagents and 400-tonne haul trucks. **Chuquicamata's original assessed grade was 2.58% —
roughly five times the grade of what is mined today.** The high-grade near-surface material was worked first and is
largely gone; what remains in the statistics is enormous tonnage at grades no pre-industrial operation can touch.

What a reconstruction society actually works, therefore, is not "the world's largest copper reserves." It is
(a) near-surface oxide and carbonate ore — the chalcocite and oxide minerals named at Collahuasi and the ore the
Spanish took from Cerro Verde by hand — (b) the historic underground high-grade workings, and (c) **a century of
tailings, waste dumps and spent leach piles**, which are rock already drilled, blasted, hauled and crushed. Those are
the energy-intensive steps, and they are already paid for. This is developed further in §6.

### 2.2 The Lithium Triangle — and a hard caution

| Country | 2024 production (t) | Reserves (t) | Resources (t) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chile** | **49,000** | **9,300,000** | 11,000,000 |
| **Argentina** | **18,000** | **4,000,000** | 23,000,000 |
| Brazil | 10,000 | 390,000 | 1,300,000 |
| **Bolivia** | **not listed** | **not listed** | **23,000,000** |
| Australia | 88,000 | 7,000,000 | 8,900,000 |
| **World** | **240,000** | **30,000,000** | ~115,000,000 |

([USGS MCS 2025, Lithium](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-lithium.pdf))

Chile, Argentina and Brazil produced **77,000 t in 2024, about 32% of world output**, and hold **13.7 million tons of
reserves, roughly 46% of the world total.** Argentine production doubled between 2023 (8,630 t) and 2024 (18,000 t).

**The Bolivian caution, and it is exactly the Sonora file's Bacadéhuachi problem in a different key.** Bolivia appears
in the USGS **resources** line at 23 million tons, tied with Argentina for the largest in the world. **It does not
appear in the production table and it does not appear in the reserves table.** Resources are not reserves: reserves
are the portion demonstrated to be economically extractable with current technology, and Bolivia's are not
established. Three physical reasons, all durable:

1. **The Salar de Uyuni brine is a saturated solution of sodium, lithium and magnesium chlorides**
   ([Salar de Uyuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Uyuni)). A high magnesium-to-lithium ratio is the known
   hard problem in brine processing — Mg must be separated from Li, and it is chemically similar enough to make that
   expensive.
2. **Uyuni gets rain.** Up to about 80 mm in January, and the salar floods into a thin sheet of water. Solar
   evaporation ponds — the entire basis of Atacama-style production — work badly where it rains.
3. **Salar de Atacama, by contrast, sits in the driest non-polar desert on Earth** (§4.5), which is precisely why
   Chile produces and Bolivia does not. The lithium at Atacama was found accidentally in 1962 by Anaconda, drilling
   for fresh water for its copper operations ([Salar de Atacama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Atacama)).

**Recommendation to the author, mirroring the Sonora file: lithium is the continent's most over-discussed mineral
asset and one of its least useful post-collapse.** Lithium chemistry requires an industrial chemical supply chain;
lead-acid batteries do not. The copper–iron–tin–silver–niobium set below is where the genuine, verifiable, usable
wealth is.

### 2.3 Brazilian iron ore — Carajás and the Iron Quadrangle

**This is the most consequential section in the file for the author's existing North American nations, because those
nations have almost no iron at all.**

| Country | 2024 usable ore (kt) | 2024 iron content (kt) | Reserves, crude ore (Mt) | Reserves, iron content (Mt) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 930,000 | 580,000 | 58,000 | 27,000 |
| **Brazil** | **440,000** | **280,000** | **34,000** | **15,000** |
| China | 270,000 | 170,000 | 20,000 | 6,900 |
| India | 270,000 | 170,000 | 5,500 | 3,400 |
| **Peru** | 21,000 | 14,000 | 2,600 | 1,500 |
| **Chile** | 18,000 | 11,000 | NA | NA |
| **Mexico** | 8,000 | 5,000 | NA | NA |
| **World** | **2,500,000** | **1,600,000** | **200,000** | **88,000** |

([USGS MCS 2025, Iron Ore](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-iron-ore.pdf))

**Brazil holds about 17% of world iron reserves by contained metal — 15 billion tonnes of iron — and is second only
to Australia.** Two districts hold nearly all of it:

- **Carajás, Parauapebas, Pará** — **the largest iron ore mine in the world.** Estimated at roughly 7.2 billion
  tonnes of ore at the mine itself, within a concession area holding **known reserves of 18 billion tons at an
  average grade of 65.4% Fe**. Hosted in banded iron formation in the southeastern Amazon Craton, Archean to
  Paleoproterozoic. Also carries gold, manganese, bauxite, copper and nickel in the same district. **The mine is
  largely powered by hydroelectricity from the Tucuruí Dam**, and the ore moves to tidewater on the **Carajás
  Railway** to São Marcos Bay in Maranhão ([Carajás Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caraj%C3%A1s_Mine)).
- **The Iron Quadrangle (Quadrilátero Ferrífero), Minas Gerais** — about **7,000 km²** in south-central Minas Gerais,
  home to more than 4 million people, with **Belo Horizonte** as its hub. The ore is **itabirite**, a metamorphosed
  banded iron formation hosted in the Cauê Formation of the Minas Supergroup, deposited about 2.5 billion years ago
  on the edge of the São Francisco craton. The district also produced roughly 40% of all Brazilian gold between 1500
  and 2000, and holds diamonds and gemstone pegmatites. Two large tailings dam failures occurred here in the 2010s
  ([Iron Quadrangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Quadrangle)).

**Grade is the point.** A 65.4% Fe average at Carajás is near the theoretical maximum for hematite ore (pure
Fe₂O₃ is 69.9% Fe). This is ore that can be reduced in a simple furnace with minimal beneficiation — no flotation
circuit, no pelletizing plant, no magnetic separation. **It is the rare kind of ore body that a low-technology
society can actually use**, which cannot be said of the low-grade taconite that dominates most surviving iron
districts elsewhere.

⚠️ Brazilian domestic figures differ slightly from the USGS: Brazilian sources give **410 million tonnes of iron ore
produced in 2019 and at least 29 billion tonnes of reserves**, concentrated in Minas Gerais and Pará
([Siderurgia, pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siderurgia)). The discrepancy is a reserve-definition
difference, not a factual conflict; the USGS figures are used above.

### 2.4 Niobium — Brazil's near-monopoly, and why it belongs beside the iron

| Country | 2024 production (t) | Reserves (t) |
|---|---|---|
| **Brazil** | **100,000** | **16,000,000** |
| Canada | 7,100 | 1,600,000 |
| Congo (Kinshasa) | 700 | NA |
| United States | 0 | 210,000 |
| **World** | **110,000** | **>17,000,000** |

([USGS MCS 2025, Niobium](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-niobium.pdf))

**Brazil produces roughly 91% of the world's niobium and holds about 94% of world reserves.** This is the most
concentrated supply position of any significant industrial metal on Earth, and it is not widely understood outside
the metals trade. The niobium occurs as pyrochlore in carbonatite intrusions.

**Why this matters, stated plainly:** niobium is the principal micro-alloying element for **high-strength low-alloy
(HSLA) steel**. Adding fractions of a percent produces steel that is substantially stronger and tougher than plain
carbon steel — the difference between a usable structural steel and a good one. USGS notes that molybdenum,
tantalum, titanium and vanadium can substitute, but with a performance or cost penalty.

**The implication the author should take from this section: Brazil holds world-class iron ore and the world's supply
of its most important alloying element inside the same national territory, about 1,400 km apart.** Add Brazilian
manganese (§2.9), which is both a deoxidizer and an alloying element and is essential to making any steel at all, and
Chilean and Peruvian molybdenum (§2.9), and the alloy package is complete.

**This is a hemisphere-scale asymmetry.** The author's North American territories have almost no iron — Alaska's
resource inventory does not list it, and "can this territory make iron?" was independently raised as the leading open
question by four separate North American research passes. Brazil answers that question for the southern half of the
hemisphere and answers it *better than the pre-collapse world did*, because the ore is high-grade enough to work
simply. **Iron in the north is the constraint; iron in the south is the endowment.**

|---|---|---|

| China | 3,300 | 70,000 |

([USGS MCS 2025, Silver](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-silver.pdf))

**Latin America produced about 12,700 t of silver in 2024 — more than half of world output — and holds about 232,000 t
of reserves, roughly 36% of the world total. Peru alone holds the largest reserves of any country at 140,000 t.**

USGS notes that silver is primarily recovered as a **byproduct** of lead–zinc, copper and gold mining, and that
polymetallic deposits account for more than two-thirds of world silver resources. That is important for §6: silver
comes out with the base metals, so a society mining copper and lead is also producing silver whether it intends to or
not.

**Cerro Rico, Potosí, Bolivia** is the historical anchor and remains materially relevant. Roughly **85% of the silver
produced in the central Andes** during the colonial period came from this one mountain, and it financed the Spanish
Empire ([Cerro Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Rico)). It is worth the author's attention as a
demonstration that this ground was mined at enormous scale **with pre-industrial technology** — hand tools,
water-powered stamp mills, mercury amalgamation, and human and animal muscle. Whatever else is true of Andean metal,
it has been extracted before without a single machine that required petroleum.

### 2.6 Tin — the metal the North American territories do not have

| Country | 2024 production (t) | Reserves (t) |
|---|---|---|
| China | 69,000 | 1,000,000 |
| Indonesia | 50,000 | NA |
| Burma | 34,000 | 700,000 |
| **Peru** | **31,000** | **130,000** |
| **Brazil** | **29,000** | **420,000** |
| Congo (Kinshasa) | 25,000 | 120,000 |
| **Bolivia** | **21,000** | **400,000** |
| United States | **0** | **0** |
| **World** | **300,000** | **>4,200,000** |

([USGS MCS 2025, Tin](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-tin.pdf))

**Peru, Brazil and Bolivia together produced 81,000 t in 2024 — about 27% of world output — and hold roughly 950,000 t
of reserves.** The United States produces none and holds none.

**Tin deserves prominence out of proportion to its tonnage, for three reasons:**

1. **Bronze.** Copper plus tin. It casts better than copper, is harder, and does not require the temperatures or the
   alloying control that steel does. A society with copper but no tin is stuck with brass (needing zinc, which the
   Andes also has — §2.9) or with pure copper. A society with both makes bearings, bushings, valves, bells, marine
   fittings and gun metal.
2. **Solder.** Tin–lead solder is the basis of all repairable electrical and plumbing work, and it is makeable in a
   workshop. This is the quiet enabler of any electrical reconstruction.
3. **Tinplate.** Tin-coated steel is how food was preserved for a century and a half before refrigeration chains.

**Bolivian tin in particular is a deep historical resource** — the Bolivian tin industry ran on the same Andean
polymetallic belt as the Potosí silver, and tin remains 4.1% of Bolivian exports even now. Latin America's tin is
the direct complement to Sonora's copper, and it is the specific gap the Sonora file identified in its
"what the territory does not have" section.

### 2.7 Bauxite, alumina and the Guiana Shield

| Country | 2024 bauxite (kt) | 2024 alumina (kt) | Bauxite reserves (kt) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guinea | 130,000 | 300 | 7,400,000 |
| Australia | 100,000 | 18,000 | 3,500,000 |
| China | 93,000 | 84,000 | 680,000 |
| **Brazil** | **33,000** | **11,000** | **2,700,000** |
| Indonesia | 32,000 | 1,300 | 2,800,000 |

([USGS MCS 2025, Bauxite and Alumina](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-bauxite-alumina.pdf))

**Brazil is the world's third-largest alumina refiner at 11,000 kt/yr**, and supplied **68% of US alumina imports**
in 2020–23. **Jamaica supplied 67% of US bauxite imports** in the same period, with Guyana at 8%.

The **Guiana Shield** — the Precambrian craton underlying the Guianas, southern Venezuela and northern Brazil — is
the geological basis of this. Lateritic weathering over a stable ancient shield in a wet tropical climate is what
makes bauxite, and the shield produces it across Guyana, Suriname, Venezuela and northern Brazil. Venezuela retains
**raw aluminum at 3.0% of its (much diminished) exports**, a remnant of the Guayana industrial complex built around
Guri hydropower (§4.4).

⚠️ **I could not verify current Venezuelan bauxite production or the operating status of the Ciudad Guayana alumina
and aluminum smelters this session.** Venezuelan industrial statistics are poor and the sector has contracted
sharply. Treat Venezuelan aluminum capacity as existing on paper and unverified in practice.

**Blunt caveat for §6: aluminum smelting takes roughly 13–15 MWh of electricity per tonne of metal.** Holding
bauxite is not holding aluminum. This is the one place where South America's hydropower endowment and its mineral
endowment genuinely intersect — and the reason the smelters were built at Ciudad Guayana next to Guri, and in Pará
next to Tucuruí, rather than anywhere else.

### 2.8 Colombia — coal and emeralds

- **Cerrejón, La Guajira** — an open-pit thermal coal complex in three zones (North, Central, South), **the largest
  in Latin America and among the ten largest in the world**. Proven reserves about **503 million tonnes**; production
  of **32.7 million tonnes in 2016**. The coal is **low-ash and low-sulfur**. Mining began in 1985 under Carbocol and
  Intercor. There has been sustained litigation on behalf of the local Wayuu community regarding the operation
  ([Cerrejón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrej%C3%B3n)).
- Coal is **19.0% of Colombian merchandise exports** ($10.5B in 2023), second only to crude petroleum at 23.7%.
  Colombia also exports **coke** at 2.4% — i.e. it has metallurgical coal and coking capacity, not just thermal coal.
  That is rarer and more valuable than the tonnage suggests.
- **Emeralds** — the Muzo, Coscuez and Chivor districts of Boyacá are the source of the world's finest emeralds,
  formed in hydrothermal veins in black shale rather than in the pegmatites that produce emeralds elsewhere.
  ⚠️ **I could not retrieve verified production or reserve figures for Colombian emeralds this session.** The
  district's pre-eminence is not in dispute; the numbers are not verified here.

### 2.9 Gold, zinc, nickel, manganese, molybdenum, phosphate, potash

**Gold** (2024 production / reserves, tonnes)
([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-gold.pdf)): Mexico 130 / 1,400; **Peru 100 / 2,500**;
Brazil 70 / 2,400; Colombia 60 / 700. World 3,300 / 64,000. Gold is 13.3% of Peru's exports, 23.1% of Bolivia's and
5.9% of Colombia's. Note that gold is the one metal whose post-collapse value is almost entirely *monetary* rather
than industrial.

**Zinc** (kt) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-zinc.pdf)): **Peru 1,300 / 20,000**;
Mexico 700 / 14,000; **Bolivia 510 / NA**. World 12,000 / 230,000. Peru holds the second-largest zinc reserves in the
world after Australia. Zinc means brass, and it means galvanizing — corrosion protection for iron, which is how you
make ironwork last outdoors without stainless steel.

**Nickel** (t) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-nickel.pdf)): **Brazil 77,000 / 16,000,000
reserves** — the **second-largest nickel reserves in the world** after Australia. This is a significant and
under-noted fact: nickel plus chromium is stainless steel, and nickel alone substantially improves steel toughness.

**Manganese** (kt, Mn content) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-manganese.pdf)):
**Brazil 590 / 270,000 reserves** — third-largest reserves after South Africa (560,000) and Australia (500,000).
Also present in the Carajás district. **Manganese is not optional in steelmaking** — it deoxidizes and desulfurizes
the melt, and there is no steel industry without it.

**Molybdenum** (t) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-molybdenum.pdf)): **Peru 41,000 /
1,900,000 reserves**; **Chile 38,000 / 1,400,000**; Mexico 17,000 / 130,000. Against US production of 33,000 t.
Chile and Peru together out-produce the United States roughly 2.4 to 1, entirely as a copper byproduct. Molybdenum is
the other major alloying element for tool steel and high-temperature service.

**Phosphate rock** (kt) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-phosphate.pdf)):
**Brazil 5,300 / 1,600,000 reserves**; **Peru 5,000 / 210,000**; Mexico 360 / 30,000.

**Potash** (kt) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-potash.pdf)): **Chile 750 production /
100,000 K₂O reserves** — from the Atacama salars, the same brines as the lithium; **Brazil 360 / 2,300**.

**The fertilizer point, which mirrors the Sonora file's treatment of Carlsbad:** nitrogen can be fixed biologically
or industrially, but **phosphorus and potassium must be mined.** Brazil holds large phosphate reserves; Chile holds
substantial potash in the salars; Peru holds phosphate at Bayóvar. The continent has both of the unfixable
macronutrients inside its own borders. Trinidad's ammonia industry (§1.6) is the nitrogen leg, and it is the one that
vanishes — see §6.4.

### 2.10 What the continent does *not* have

Being honest about gaps matters as much as cataloging strengths.

- **Chromium.** Essentially absent at world scale. **Without chromium there is no stainless steel**, regardless of how
  much nickel Brazil holds. This is the single most important mineral gap in the survey.
- **Metallurgical coal is thin and badly placed.** Colombia has coking coal and coke capacity at Cerrejón and in
  Boyacá/Cundinamarca; Brazil's own coal is low-grade and high-ash and the Brazilian steel industry has historically
  imported coking coal. **This gap is the reason §6.5's charcoal argument matters so much.**
- **Tungsten** is minor. Bolivia and Peru have some, but not at a scale that supports a tool-steel industry.
- **Rare earth separation capacity** does not exist on the continent, though Brazil holds significant deposits.
- **Potash outside Chile is weak** — Brazil imports the large majority of its potassium fertilizer.
- **The Caribbean islands and the Central American isthmus are mineral-poor** with the named exceptions of Jamaican
  bauxite, Cuban nickel and the Cobre Panamá copper deposit.

## 3. Agriculture

### 3.1 The Pampas — the continent's single best farmland

The **Pampas** cover more than **1,200,000 km²** across the Argentine provinces of Buenos Aires, La Pampa, Santa Fe,
Entre Ríos and Córdoba, all of Uruguay, and the southernmost Brazilian state of Rio Grande do Sul. The climate is
temperate, with **600–1,200 mm of precipitation distributed more or less evenly through the year**
([Pampas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampas)).

**That last clause is the whole argument.** Evenly distributed rainfall in that range over deep, fertile temperate
grassland soil means **the Pampas farm without irrigation.** No dams, no canals, no pumps, no aquifer. It is the same
agronomic category as the North American prairie, and it is the largest such region in the Southern Hemisphere.

Argentina's output: **fourth-largest beef producer in the world at about 3 million tonnes (2018)**, top-five globally
in soybeans, maize and sunflower seed, top-ten in barley and grapes, top-fifteen in wheat and sorghum
([Agriculture in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Argentina)). Its 2023 exports were led by
**soybean meal 12.1%, corn 9.8%, soybean oil 6.4%** and frozen bovine meat 2.9%.

Uruguay is the Pampas in miniature and unusually well-balanced: wood pulp 20.2%, **frozen bovine meat 14.2%**, rice
5.5%, concentrated milk 5.3%, more beef 3.8%. Uruguay is also the **only country in this survey whose ECI is higher
in 2024 (+0.320) than in 2005**, having recovered to near its 1995 level.

### 3.2 The Cerrado and Brazilian soy — with the caveat that governs everything

Brazil's 2018 production ([Agriculture in Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Brazil)):

| Crop | Output | World rank |
|---|---|---|
| **Sugarcane** | **746.8 Mt** | **1st** (India, 2nd, produces about half) |
| **Soybeans** | 117.8 Mt | 2nd in 2018; **passed the US in 2020** |
| **Maize** | 82.2 Mt | 3rd |
| **Cassava** | 17.6 Mt | 5th |
| **Oranges** | 16.7 Mt | **1st** |
| Rice | 11.7 Mt | 9th |
| Bananas | 6.7 Mt | 3rd |
| Cotton | 4.9 Mt | 4th |
| **Coffee** | 3.5 Mt | **1st** |
| Beans | 2.9 Mt | 3rd |

Brazil is also the world's largest exporter of coffee, soybeans, beef, poultry, cane sugar, soybean meal, orange
juice and tobacco.

**Now the caveat, and it is the most important agricultural fact in this file.** The **Cerrado** — the vast tropical
savanna across Goiás, Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul, Tocantins, Maranhão, Piauí, Bahia and Minas Gerais — carries
much of this production, and **it was not farmable until it was chemically rebuilt.** Cerrado soils are
**oxisols: well-drained, low pH, low in calcium and magnesium**. Embrapa, the Brazilian agricultural research agency,
established that they could be made fit for industrial crops **by adding phosphorus and lime**. By the late 1990s
Brazil was applying **14–16 million tonnes of lime per year**, rising to **25 million tonnes in 2003 and 2004 — about
five tonnes of lime per hectare** ([Cerrado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrado)). Embrapa separately bred
tropical soybean varieties, soy having been a temperate crop until then.

**The Cerrado is an industrial artifact.** Its fertility is applied, annually, from outside, in quantities that
require quarries, kilns, crushers, rail and trucks. The Brazilian sources are explicit that central Brazilian
grasslands "are far less fertile than those of North America, and are generally suited only for grazing." This is
category (c) in §6 and it is by far the largest single item in that category anywhere in the survey.

### 3.3 The Andean crop complex — and the fact that matters most

**The potato was domesticated in southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, around Lake Titicaca, roughly 7,000–10,000
years ago**, from a species in the *Solanum brevicaule* complex. Genetic studies indicate a **single origin** for the
cultivated potato, in that area ([Potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato)).

**There are about 5,000 potato varieties worldwide, and roughly 3,000 of them are in the Andes** — principally in
Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Chile and Colombia. **Over 100 cultivars may be found in a single valley, and a dozen or more
may be maintained by a single farming household.**

The counter-example is in the same article and is the point: the potato introduced to Europe came from a very limited
number of varieties, and that **lack of genetic diversity left the crop defenseless when *Phytophthora infestans*
arrived in 1845.** Conservative estimates attribute a quarter of Old World population growth and urbanization between
1700 and 1900 to the potato — a crop that then failed catastrophically because it had been reduced to a monoculture.

The **International Potato Center (CIP)**, established by Peruvian government decree in **1971** and a CGIAR center
since 1972, is in **Lima**. It holds and researches potato, sweet potato and other Andean root and tuber germplasm,
and works on natural resource management in the Andes specifically
([CIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Potato_Center)).

**This is the direct structural equivalent of the Sonora file's CIMMYT fact, and it is arguably larger.** The world's
principal collection and living field diversity of the crop that feeds more people in marginal highland conditions
than any other is physically located in the Andes — both in a research institution in Lima and, far more durably, in
the hands of thousands of farming households maintaining landraces in cultivation. **The institutional collection is
fragile; the in-field diversity is not.** That distinction is developed in §6.2.

The rest of the Andean package is equally pre-industrial and equally durable: **quinoa** (*Chenopodium quinoa*,
domesticated around Lake Titicaca, exceptional protein profile, tolerant of salinity, drought, frost and poor soil),
**maize** in enormous landrace diversity, **oca, ulluco, mashua, arracacha**, and the **camelids** — llama and alpaca
for fiber and transport, and a fully domesticated protein source in the guinea pig.

Two engineering inheritances matter as much as the crops: the **Andean vertical archipelago**, in which a single
community farms a ladder of ecological zones at different elevations to hedge against failure in any one of them;
and the terraces (**andenes**) and canal systems that made those slopes farmable and which are masonry earthworks,
not machines.

### 3.4 Tropical staples and the lowland package

**Cassava** (*Manihot esculenta*) was domesticated in Brazil and is the continent's great lowland calorie crop —
tolerant of poor acid soils and drought, storable in the ground rather than in a granary, and productive on land that
will not support cereals. Brazil produced 17.6 Mt in 2018, fifth in the world, and it is grown throughout tropical
Latin America. **Maize, beans, squash, sweet potato, peanut, chili, cacao and plantain** complete the package.

⚠️ **The Amazon Basin should not be read as agricultural land.** Its soils are largely nutrient-poor and the biomass
holds the nutrients, not the ground. The exception is **terra preta** — anthropogenic dark earths created by
pre-Columbian populations through charcoal, pottery and organic waste incorporation, which are genuinely fertile and
genuinely persistent. Their total extent is disputed and I did not verify it this session. Treat Amazonia as
supporting a modest population by riverine fishing, cassava and agroforestry, not as a breadbasket.

### 3.5 Coffee, sugar, cacao

**Coffee:** Brazil is the world's largest producer at 3.5 Mt (2018); Colombia's coffee is 5.8% of its exports.
**Sugar:** Brazilian cane at 746.8 Mt dwarfs all other production, and much of it goes to ethanol rather than sugar —
a significant and underappreciated fact, since **sugarcane ethanol is a liquid fuel makeable with fermentation and
distillation**, both pre-industrial technologies (§6.3). **Cacao** is native to the upper Amazon and remains
significant in Ecuador (3.1% of exports) and Brazil.

### 3.6 Fisheries — the Humboldt Current

**The Humboldt Current is the most productive eastern boundary current system on Earth and accounts for roughly
18–20% of the total worldwide marine fish catch**
([Humboldt Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_Current)). Coastal upwelling brings deep nutrients to the
surface along the Peruvian and Chilean coast, driving phytoplankton production and an enormous pelagic biomass —
anchovies, sardines and jack mackerel, supporting seals, cetaceans and seabirds above them. Surface temperatures off
Peru near 5°S fall as low as 16°C, wildly anomalous for tropical latitudes.

The **Peruvian anchoveta** (*Engraulis ringens*) has been characterized as the most heavily exploited fish in world
history. Annual harvests ran **between 3.14 and 8.32 million tonnes from 2010 to 2021**, with a historic peak of
**13.1 million tonnes in 1971** ([Peruvian anchoveta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_anchoveta)). Schools
concentrate within 80 km of the coast.

**Two crucial qualifications.** First, this fishery is **El Niño-dependent**: the 1972, 1982–83 and 1997–98 events
each collapsed the population by shutting down the upwelling and starving the food chain. It is a spectacularly
productive resource that fails hard, unpredictably, on a multi-year cycle. Second, the modern fishery lands the
anchoveta almost entirely for **fishmeal**, not human food — which is a market artifact, not a biological one. A
subsistence fishery eats them.

Chile's fish fillets are 4.3% of exports and Ecuador's crustaceans are 18.5% — the second-largest item in the
Ecuadorian economy.

### 3.7 Cattle

Brazil and Argentina hold two of the largest cattle herds in the world. Argentina is the fourth-largest beef producer
at about 3 Mt; Brazil is the largest beef exporter. Uruguay's frozen and chilled beef together are about 18% of its
exports; Paraguay's are about 12.5%. **The Pampas cattle economy is grass-fed and rainfed**, which is the material
distinction from feedlot systems dependent on grain, and it is why it belongs in category (a) in §6.

## 4. Energy

### 4.1 Venezuelan heavy oil — the Orinoco Belt

The **Orinoco Belt** covers about **55,314 km²** along the northern edge of the Orinoco River basin and consists of
large deposits of **extra-heavy crude oil**. Oil in place is estimated at roughly **1,200 billion barrels**, which is
approximately equal to the world's entire reserves of lighter oil. PDVSA has estimated **producible reserves of up to
235 billion barrels**, which would make it the largest petroleum reserve in the world, slightly ahead of the Canadian
Athabasca oil sands and above Saudi Arabia. **In 2009 the USGS raised the technically recoverable estimate to 513
billion barrels**, while making no estimate of what is economically recoverable
([Orinoco Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orinoco_Belt)).

**The physical caveat is decisive, and the author should not let the headline number stand alone.** Extra-heavy crude
at roughly 8–10° API is closer to bitumen than to oil: it will not flow at ambient temperature, cannot be moved
through a pipeline without heating or blending with a lighter diluent, and cannot be run in an ordinary refinery
without upgrading. The Orinoco industry was built around **upgraders** — capital-intensive plants that add hydrogen
or reject carbon to convert the extra-heavy crude into synthetic crude. **The reserve is real; the usability is
entirely a function of installed industrial plant.** The first well in the belt, La Canoa-1 in 1938, produced about
1,000 barrels per day for 44 days and was then abandoned precisely because the hydrocarbons could not be extracted
practically.

Venezuela's collapse to **US$7.7 billion in total merchandise exports** (§1.5) while sitting on this reserve is the
most instructive single data point in the survey.

### 4.2 Brazilian pre-salt

The **pre-salt province** lies beneath a salt layer up to about 2,000 m thick, extending roughly **800 km along the
Brazilian continental shelf** from Santa Catarina to Espírito Santo. Petrobras and partners have implicated
**reserves of over 50 billion barrels**, against Brazilian national reserves of roughly 14 billion barrels before the
discoveries. At the **Tupi (Lula) and Iara** prospects in block BM-S-11 alone, Petrobras estimates **8 to 12 billion
barrels recoverable** ([Pre-salt layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-salt_layer)).

Crude petroleum is now **12.8% of Brazilian merchandise exports**, second only to soybeans.

**This is the most infrastructure-dependent hydrocarbon resource in the hemisphere.** It sits under deep water, then
under thousands of meters of rock, then under a salt layer that is difficult to image seismically and mechanically
awkward to drill through. It is produced from floating production units by dynamically positioned drillships. There
is no low-technology version of any part of it.

### 4.3 Vaca Muerta, and the rest of the oil and gas

**Vaca Muerta**, in the Neuquén Basin of Argentina, is a shale oil and shale gas formation. The major discovery was
made in 2010 by Repsol-YPF. Proven reserves are around **927 million barrels**; Repsol YPF raised its estimate of
oil resources to **22.5 billion barrels** in February 2012; the **US EIA estimates total technically recoverable
hydrocarbons at 16.2 billion barrels of oil and 308 trillion cubic feet of gas**
([Vaca Muerta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaca_Muerta)).

**Same caveat as the New Mexico Permian in the Sonora file, and it applies with equal force.** This is horizontal
drilling with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing, consuming water, proppant sand, specialty steel casing and diesel at
industrial scale. Argentina has the *resource*; it does not have a resource a reconstruction society can tap at
anything like these rates.

Other systems: **Mexican offshore Campeche** (Cantarell, Ku-Maloob-Zaap, Zama) — in scope for this file, unlike the
Sonora file, and Mexico's crude is 5.6% of its exports. **Colombian** crude is 23.7% of exports, from the Llanos and
Magdalena basins. **Ecuadorian** crude is 34.6% of exports, from the Oriente. **Guyana's Stabroek block** is the
newest and most concentrated: 83.5% of all Guyanese exports. **Bolivian and Trinidadian gas** are the two significant
gas positions — Bolivia's petroleum gas is 20.0% of exports, Trinidad's 30.2%.

### 4.4 Hydropower — the decisive asset

South America is the most hydro-dependent continent on Earth, and this is the single most important energy fact in
the file.

**Brazil: 109.4 GW of installed hydroelectric capacity at the end of 2021, second in the world**, with **hydro at 77%
of total installed generating capacity and meeting over 60% of electricity demand**. The national grid runs at 60 Hz
and is **83% renewable-powered**. There are **24 hydro plants above 1,000 MW**. Brazil also holds the largest water
storage capacity in the world ([Electricity sector in Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_sector_in_Brazil)).

**Named plants:**

| Plant | River / location | Capacity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Itaipu** | Paraná, Brazil–Paraguay | **14 GW** | 20 units × 700 MW, hydraulic head 118 m. Averaged **89.22 TWh/yr 2012–2021**, second-highest in the world; record **103.1 TWh in 2016**. Flooded only 1,350 km² — the highest ratio of output to flooded area of any Brazilian plant. **Ten units generate at 50 Hz for Paraguay and ten at 60 Hz for Brazil** ([Itaipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itaipu_Dam)) |
| **Belo Monte** | Xingu, Pará | **11,233 MW** | Completed Nov 2019; fifth-largest in the world by installed capacity. Main powerhouse 11,000 MW (18 Francis turbines at 611.11 MW) plus Pimental at 233.1 MW. ⚠️ **Guaranteed minimum generation is far below nameplate** because the Xingu is strongly seasonal ([Belo Monte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belo_Monte_Dam)) |
| **Tucuruí** | Tocantins, Pará | **8,000 MW** | Flooded 2,430 km². **Powers the Carajás iron ore operation** ([Tucuruí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucuru%C3%AD_Dam)) |
| **Guri (Simón Bolívar)** | Caroní, Bolívar State, Venezuela | ~10 GW | Concrete gravity and embankment, 7,426 m long, 162 m high, built 1963–69, reservoir **4,250 km²** — one of the largest on Earth. Was the world's largest by installed capacity before Itaipu ([Guri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guri_Dam)) |
| **Yacyretá** | Paraná, Argentina–Paraguay | ~3.1 GW | ⚠️ capacity figure not verified this session |

**Paraguay exports electricity as its second-largest export good (12.3%, $1.52B in 2023)** — a position no other
country in the hemisphere occupies. Its share of Itaipu and Yacyretá vastly exceeds domestic demand.

**Why hydro is the hinge of §6:** a hydroelectric dam is a **gravity machine**. The expensive, irreplaceable part —
the excavation, the diversion, the dam structure, the penstock alignment, the head — is civil engineering that
persists. Turbines and generators wear out and need rewinding, bearings and machining, but water falling through a
head of 118 meters will turn something for as long as the river runs and the structure stands.

### 4.5 Solar — the Atacama

The **Atacama Desert** covers about **105,000 km²** (128,000 km² including the barren lower Andean slopes) along the
Pacific coast of Chile, between the Andes and the Chilean Coast Range. **It is the driest non-polar desert in the
world and the largest fog desert on Earth** ([Atacama Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert)). Extreme
aridity, high elevation, and low humidity combine to give it what is widely regarded as the best solar resource
measured anywhere. Since 2024 the **"Oasis de Atacama"** project has been building roughly **2 GW of PV with 11 GWh
of storage**.

⚠️ **I could not retrieve precise DNI/GHI values in kWh/m²/day for the Atacama this session.** The directional claim
— best solar resource on Earth — is well supported and uncontroversial; the exact figures are not verified here. The
author should pull them from the Global Solar Atlas (<https://globalsolaratlas.info/>) if precision is wanted.

Note the geographic coincidence worth building on: **the Atacama holds the world's best solar resource, the world's
largest copper mines, and the world's largest lithium reserves, in the same desert**, and it has essentially no fresh
water. That last constraint is as hard as the Sonora file's, and for the same reason.

### 4.6 Wind, nuclear, and grids

- **Wind:** Brazil had **21.1 GW installed at end-2021, seventh in the world**, concentrated in the Northeast
  (Ceará, Rio Grande do Norte, Bahia) where the trade winds are unusually steady. Patagonia has an exceptional and
  largely undeveloped wind resource.
- **Nuclear:** Brazil's **Angra 1 and 2** (Angra dos Reis, Rio de Janeiro), Argentina's **Atucha I and II** and
  **Embalse**, and Mexico's **Laguna Verde** (Veracruz). Nuclear is 2.5% of Brazilian generation. ⚠️ Individual
  reactor capacities and operating status not verified this session.
- **Grid topology.** Brazil runs a single large interconnected system at **60 Hz**. Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and
  Chile run at **50 Hz**. **Itaipu physically straddles the boundary, generating at both frequencies with separate
  machine sets** — half its units at 50 Hz for Paraguay, half at 60 Hz for Brazil, with HVDC links carrying the
  Paraguayan half into the Brazilian system. That is one of the more remarkable pieces of electrical infrastructure
  in the world and it is worth the author knowing it exists.

## 5. Industry, Ports and Connectivity

### 5.1 Brazilian manufacturing

- **Embraer** is **the third-largest producer of civil aircraft in the world after Boeing and Airbus**, and ranks
  among the top 100 defense contractors globally. It is best known for the **ERJ and E-Jet** families of narrow-body
  regional airliners and for the market-leading **Phenom 300** business jet, and it maintains an aerospace technology
  incubator ([Embraer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embraer)). Headquartered at São José dos Campos, São Paulo.
- **Steel.** Brazil produced **35.4 million tonnes of crude steel**, ninth in the world
  ([Siderurgia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siderurgia)). Major producers include **Gerdau, CSN (Companhia
  Siderúrgica Nacional), Usiminas and ArcelorMittal Brasil**; CSN alone produces about 4.5 million tonnes annually.
  **Brazil is the world's largest exporter of pig iron** and the sixth-largest producer.
- **Autos** — São Paulo and Minas Gerais host assembly for most global manufacturers.
- **São Paulo** is the industrial core, and the **Port of Santos** is its outlet and the largest port in Latin
  America. ⚠️ Santos throughput figures not verified this session.

### 5.2 Mexico south of the Sonora line

The **Bajío** — Guanajuato, Querétaro, Aguascalientes, San Luis Potosí — is Mexico's fastest-growing industrial
region and its automotive heartland outside the border states. **Querétaro is Mexico's largest aerospace cluster**, a
fact the Sonora file noted as falling outside its own scope; it falls inside this one. **Michoacán** holds Mexican
iron ore and the Pacific port of **Lázaro Cárdenas**, which is also a steelmaking site. **Zacatecas** holds the
Fresnillo/Proaño, Saucito and Juanicipio silver complex — the largest silver operations in the world, and again
explicitly out of the Sonora file's scope and inside this one. ⚠️ Plant-level and production detail for all of these
is unverified this session.

### 5.3 The Panama Canal

The canal cuts the Isthmus of Panama, lifting ships by locks at each end into **Gatun Lake, an artificial fresh water
lake 26 meters (85 ft) above sea level**, formed by damming the Chagres River, with **Lake Alajuela** (created by the
Madden Dam, completed 1935) as additional storage. The expanded Neopanamax locks opened commercially on **26 June
2016** ([Panama Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal)).

**Three facts the author should hold onto, because they decide the canal's fate in §6.6:**

1. **The locks are gravity-fed.** The original design principle was explicit: water to fill the locks is taken from
   Gatun Lake by opening gates and valves and **letting gravity move it**. There is no lift pumping in the basic
   operation of the canal. This is a far more favorable position than, say, the Central Arizona Project.
2. **But each transit consumes fresh water and dumps it to sea.** An average transit uses about **200 million liters
   (52 million US gallons)** of fresh water. The Neopanamax locks are accompanied by **nine water-reuse basins
   (three per chamber), each about 70 m wide, 430 m long and 5.5 m deep**, which recover **60% of the water used per
   transit** and cut per-lane water use by about 7%. These basins are also gravity-fed.
3. **Water, not machinery, is already the binding constraint.** Drought has repeatedly forced transit restrictions:
   **24 ships per day in January 2024**, and in 2026 the Panama Canal Authority again reduced daily transits because
   of low rainfall during an intensifying El Niño, allowing **34 ships per day from 3 September 2026**. In 2025 the
   authority began planning a new dam to feed Gatun Lake. Gatun is also **Panama City's primary source of potable
   water**, so the canal and the capital compete for the same reservoir.

⚠️ **I could not verify how the lock gates and valves are actuated** — whether by electric motors, hydraulics, or
some combination — or the original lock chamber dimensions, this session. That is the decisive unknown for §6.6 and
the author should check it. The water system is gravity; the *gates* may not be.

### 5.4 Ports

The major outlets: **Santos** (Brazil, the largest in Latin America), **Ponta da Madeira / São Luís** (Maranhão — the
Carajás iron ore terminal), **Tubarão** (Vitória — the Iron Quadrangle's ore terminal), **Itaqui**, **Paranaguá**
(grain), **Rio de Janeiro**; **Buenos Aires** and **Rosario** (the Paraná grain corridor); **Montevideo**;
**Valparaíso** and **San Antonio** (Chile); **Callao** (Peru); **Cartagena**, **Buenaventura** and **Barranquilla**
(Colombia); **Guayaquil** (Ecuador); **Colón** and **Balboa** (Panama, at the canal's two ends); **Veracruz**,
**Lázaro Cárdenas** and **Manzanillo** (Mexico). ⚠️ Throughput tonnages for all of these are unverified this session.

**The Paraná–Paraguay waterway** deserves separate mention: it is a navigable river corridor running from the
Pampas and the Brazilian interior to the Río de la Plata, and it moves an enormous share of the region's grain. It
is a **river**, which is to say it is category (a) and (b) in §6, not category (c).

### 5.5 The connectivity problem

**This is the continent's defining infrastructural weakness and it is geological, not political.**

- **The Andes** run the entire length of the continent, 7,000 km, with peaks over 6,000 m and few low passes. They
  separate the Pacific slope from everything east of them almost completely. There is no equivalent of the
  Mississippi–Great Lakes corridor connecting the interior to a coast on both sides.
- **Amazonia** is roughly 6 million km² of rainforest on poor soil with a river system that is superb for boats and
  nearly impossible for roads. The Trans-Amazonian Highway is a partial and poorly maintained exception.
- **There is no continental rail network.** What rail exists is overwhelmingly **export-oriented spurs running from a
  mine or a grain region to the nearest port** — the Carajás Railway to São Marcos Bay is the archetype, 892 km from
  pit to tidewater. Rail gauges differ between and within countries. There is no through route from Buenos Aires to
  Caracas, or from Lima to São Paulo, comparable to what exists in North America or Europe.
- **The practical consequence: Latin American internal trade has historically moved by sea around the continent, or
  not at all.** Each major population center is better connected to the ocean than to its neighbors.

**For the author this is the most important structural fact in §5.** Overland movement across this continent is
expensive in a way that has no North American equivalent, and it is expensive for permanent geological reasons. Any
region here is materially better connected to the open ocean than to the region on the other side of the cordillera
or the basin. That is a fact about terrain and transport cost; what the author builds on it is the author's own.

## 6. POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

*Everything above is sourced fact. This section is reasoning from those facts, and is the author's to accept, modify
or discard. Assumption, matching the Sonora file: a civilization-ending nuclear exchange in 2083, followed by a
reconstruction era 150–250 years later, with no functioning global trade and no external supply chains.*

**Structure note, for cross-file comparison.** This section follows the same six-part spine as the nine North
American material-base files, so the regions can be read against each other directly: **headline (6.1) → (a) survives
regardless (6.2) → (b) repairable (6.3) → (c) vanishes (6.4) → carrying capacity (6.7) → summary judgment (6.8)**.
Two extra subsections sit inside that spine because the brief asked for them specifically and because they are too
large to fold into the lists: **6.5, the steel-and-electricity question**, and **6.6, the Panama Canal**. Both are
elaborations of category (b).

### 6.1 The headline

**Latin America's economic statistics describe a region of dependency; its geology and climate describe a region of
unusual self-sufficiency. A collapse deletes the first description and leaves the second.**

Three things follow, and they are the spine of everything below:

1. **The ECI ranking approximately inverts in importance.** Mexico's +0.914 measures its position in a North American
   production network that a collapse severs. Chile's −0.227 measures an economy organized around copper, and the
   copper is a mountain range. Over 150–250 years, accumulated know-how decays and geology does not.
2. **South America can still make steel.** This is the central finding and it is argued at length in §6.5. No other
   part of the hemisphere in the author's material base can say this.
3. **The continent can feed itself comfortably in the temperate south and the Andes, and much less comfortably
   everywhere the soil had to be chemically manufactured.**

### 6.2 (a) Things that survive regardless — geology, soil, climate, genetics

These cannot be destroyed by war or neglect. They are simply there in 2283.

- **Every ore body in §2.** Escondida, Chuquicamata, Collahuasi, El Teniente, Cerro Verde, Antamina, Cerro Rico,
  Carajás, the Iron Quadrangle, the Brazilian niobium carbonatites, the Bolivian tin belt, Cerrejón's coal, the
  Guiana Shield bauxite. Rock does not care.
- **The same grade caveat as the Sonora file, in sharper form.** What is *workable* is not the 0.4–0.66% porphyry
  copper in the reserve tables. It is (i) near-surface oxide and secondary sulfide ore — chalcocite, and the
  high-grade oxide the Spanish took from Cerro Verde by hand in the 1800s; (ii) the historic high-grade underground
  workings, at grades like Chuquicamata's original 2.58%; and (iii) **a century of tailings, waste dumps and spent
  leach piles** — rock already drilled, blasted, hauled and crushed, which are the energy-intensive steps. Andean
  tailings piles are among the largest artificial deposits of processed rock on Earth and they are the real
  inheritance.
- **Carajás-grade iron ore is the exception to the grade caveat and the most important exception in the file.** At
  **65.4% Fe average**, the ore is usable essentially as it comes out of the ground. There is no beneficiation
  problem to solve. A charcoal bloomery or a small blast furnace can reduce it.
- **The Pampas.** 1,200,000 km² of temperate grassland, deep soil, 600–1,200 mm of evenly distributed rainfall, and
  **no irrigation requirement whatsoever**. The single most valuable agricultural asset in the survey.
- **The Andean crop complex and its genetic diversity.** 3,000 potato varieties in farmers' fields, plus quinoa,
  maize landraces, oca, ulluco, camelids and guinea pigs. **Distinguish sharply between the CIP gene bank in Lima —
  which is a refrigerated institutional collection and is fragile — and the living in-field diversity maintained by
  thousands of highland households, which is not.** The second is the real asset and it survives by being in
  continuous cultivation rather than in storage.
- **Andean terraces, andenes and canal systems.** Masonry and earthworks, built without machines, and in many places
  still functioning or recoverable.
- **Cassava** throughout the lowland tropics: tolerant of poor acid soils, stores in the ground, needs no inputs.
- **The Humboldt Current.** Upwelling is driven by wind and the Earth's rotation. **18–20% of the world marine fish
  catch** will keep arriving off Peru and Chile regardless of who is alive to net it. It fails periodically with
  El Niño, which is a permanent feature, not a new risk.
- **The Atacama's solar resource** and the aridity that produced the nitrate, lithium and potash brines.
- **The rivers.** The Paraná, the Orinoco, the São Francisco, the Amazon, the Magdalena. Both as transport and as
  head.

### 6.3 (b) Things that depend on infrastructure but could plausibly be repaired

The common thread, as in the Sonora file: **gravity, masonry and earthworks** — things whose expensive part is civil
engineering rather than machinery.

- **The hydroelectric dams. This is the most valuable repairable asset on the continent, by a very wide margin.**
  Itaipu, Guri, Tucuruí, Belo Monte, Yacyretá and the twenty-odd other Brazilian plants above 1,000 MW are concrete
  and earth structures creating a head of water. Turbines and generators need bearings, rewinding and machining; the
  dam, the diversion works, the penstocks and the 118-meter head at Itaipu do not go anywhere. **Even at a small
  fraction of nameplate output, a reconstruction society operating two units of a 20-unit plant has 1.4 GW — more
  electricity than any plausible reconstruction economy on this continent knows what to do with.**
  ⚠️ Siltation is the long-term killer, exactly as in the Sonora file: reservoirs fill over generations and a society
  without sluicing capability watches its storage disappear.
- **Small and micro hydro.** Far more important than the giants for most communities. The Andes are the best
  small-hydro terrain in the world — steep, wet, and with head everywhere — and a micro-hydro installation is a
  penstock, a turbine and a generator, all of which are within reach of a competent machine shop.
- **The Panama Canal.** Treated separately in §6.6.
- **Andean terrace and canal systems.** Arguably category (a); listed here because many are currently derelict and
  would need rebuilding.
- **The Paraná–Paraguay waterway and the Amazon.** River navigation needs channel maintenance and not much else.
  Barge and boat traffic on these systems is entirely recoverable, and given §5.5 it is the *only* realistic
  long-distance bulk transport on this continent.
- **Rail alignments.** The Carajás Railway's 892 km of graded, bridged alignment from the world's largest iron mine
  to tidewater represents generations of earthmoving. Relaying track on an existing alignment is vastly easier than
  surveying a new one, and rail is the correct technology for a low-energy society moving ore and grain.
- **Copper and iron smelting sites.** Chile refined 1,900 kt of copper in 2024; the Iron Quadrangle and the Minas
  Gerais charcoal ironmaking district have been making metal continuously for three centuries. The *sites* are right
  for permanent geological reasons, whatever happens to the specific plant.
- **Sugarcane ethanol.** Fermentation and distillation are pre-industrial technologies. Brazil already grows 746.8 Mt
  of cane and already converts much of it to fuel alcohol. **This is the single most recoverable liquid fuel supply
  in the hemisphere**, and it needs no oil industry, no refinery and no drilling. A reconstruction society in
  São Paulo or the Northeast can have a liquid fuel.
- **Charcoal ironmaking.** See §6.5 — the most important item in this list.
- **Lead-acid batteries.** Lead from Peruvian and Bolivian polymetallic ores, sulfuric acid from pyrite, and 1859
  technology. As in the Sonora file, this is the realistic electrical storage of a reconstruction society.

### 6.4 (c) Things that are pure artifacts of global trade and industrial energy — these vanish

- **The Cerrado, as cropland.** This is the largest single loss in the survey. Farming there requires **five tonnes of
  lime per hectare** and applied phosphorus, at a national scale of 25 million tonnes of lime per year. Strip out
  quarries, kilns, crushers, rail and trucks, and the oxisols revert to what they were: acidic, aluminum-affected
  savanna soil "generally suited only for grazing." **Brazil's soybean and much of its maize production is an
  industrial artifact**, and it goes. The Cerrado becomes rangeland — which is not nothing, but it is not 117 million
  tonnes of soybeans.
- **The pre-salt.** Ultra-deepwater, sub-salt, floating production. The most infrastructure-dependent hydrocarbon
  resource in the hemisphere. Gone absolutely.
- **Vaca Muerta.** Fracking. Gone, for exactly the reasons the Sonora file gives for the New Mexico Permian.
- **The Orinoco Belt.** This is the cruelest entry in the list. **Possibly the largest petroleum reserve on Earth, and
  it is unusable.** Extra-heavy crude will not flow, cannot be piped without diluent or heat, and cannot be refined
  without upgraders. Venezuela in 2283 holds 235–513 billion barrels of something it cannot drink, burn or move. The
  author could do a great deal with that image.
- **Open-pit porphyry copper at 0.4–0.66%.** Solvent extraction and electrowinning, imported sulfuric acid at scale,
  flotation reagents, haul fleets. Replaced by the far smaller-scale work described in §6.2.
- **Lithium.** Brine evaporation at Atacama is arguably recoverable — it is solar evaporation in ponds, which is
  ancient technology — but the downstream conversion to battery-grade lithium carbonate or hydroxide is industrial
  chemistry, and the batteries themselves require a supply chain that does not exist. Bolivia's 23 million tonnes of
  *resources* remain what they are today: not reserves.
- **Aluminum.** Bauxite survives; smelting does not, at 13–15 MWh per tonne. ⚠️ Partial exception worth flagging: if
  Guri or Tucuruí can be restarted at even modest capacity, aluminum smelting is the one energy-intensive industry
  that South America could plausibly rebuild, precisely because the smelters were sited next to the dams for that
  reason. This is a genuine possibility, not a certainty, and it sits on the boundary between (b) and (c).
- **Trinidad's ammonia and methanol.** Haber-Bosch ammonia is the synthetic nitrogen leg of the fertilizer tripod and
  it needs natural gas, high pressure and catalysts. Gone. Nitrogen reverts to legumes, manure and guano — and note
  that the **Peruvian and Chilean guano and nitrate deposits** are a real regional asset here, which is why they
  were fought over in the nineteenth century.
- **Costa Rica's medical devices and integrated circuits, and Mexico's automotive assembly.** Definitionally
  externally coupled. Both countries' ECI scores describe capabilities that have no domestic existence to fall back
  on.
- **Embraer.** Aircraft manufacturing is second only to semiconductors in supply-chain dependency. Gone.
- **Export agriculture as a category.** Coffee, cacao, bananas, orange juice, fishmeal, soybean meal. The *plants*
  survive — coffee and cacao are perennial trees and will keep fruiting — but growing 3.5 Mt of coffee makes no sense
  without a world to sell it to. These revert to local and subsistence scale.
- **Chromium-dependent metallurgy.** There is no stainless steel on this continent after the existing stock is used
  up, and no realistic path to it. §2.10.

### 6.5 The steel-and-electricity question — the most important section in this file

**Can South America still make steel? Yes — and it is the only part of the hemisphere in the author's material base
that can.**

The argument has five legs, four of them solidly verified and one flagged.

**1. Iron, at usable grade.** Brazil holds **15 billion tonnes of contained iron in reserves**, roughly 17% of the
world total, in two districts. **Carajás averages 65.4% Fe** — near the theoretical maximum for hematite. This is ore
a bloomery or a small blast furnace can reduce directly, without flotation, pelletizing or magnetic separation. The
Iron Quadrangle's itabirite is somewhat lower grade but has been worked continuously since the 1690s, including
throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries with pre-industrial technology.

**2. The alloying package, complete and in-territory.**

| Element | Source | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Niobium** | Brazil, **91% of world output, 94% of reserves** | HSLA micro-alloying — strength and toughness |
| **Manganese** | Brazil, 270,000 kt reserves (3rd in world), incl. Carajás | **Mandatory** — deoxidation and desulfurization |
| **Molybdenum** | Chile 38,000 t, Peru 41,000 t (byproduct of copper) | Tool steel, high-temperature service |
| **Nickel** | Brazil, **16,000,000 t reserves, 2nd in world** | Toughness; stainless (but see chromium gap) |
| **Silicon** | Ubiquitous | Deoxidation, electrical steel |

**No other territory in the author's material base has this list.** The Sonora file's territory has molybdenum and
would make good steel if it had iron; South America has the iron *and* the alloys.

**3. Electricity, from gravity.** §6.3. Brazil's 109.4 GW of installed hydro, Itaipu's 14 GW at a 118 m head, Guri's
10 GW, Tucuruí's 8 GW powering Carajás today. Even at a small fraction of capacity this is more electricity than a
reconstruction economy can absorb — and **electric arc furnaces melt scrap**, which means the enormous stock of
twentieth-century steel in Brazilian cities becomes feedstock rather than rubble, exactly as in the Sonora file's
treatment of Ternium.

**4. The reduction fuel — and here is the thing that makes South America different from everywhere else.**

South America's metallurgical coal position is weak (§2.10). Brazil's own coal is poor and the steel industry has
historically imported coking coal. In any other analysis that would be the end of the argument: no coke, no blast
furnace, no primary iron.

**Except that Brazil does not entirely use coke.**

What is **verified**:

- **Charcoal-based iron and steel making operations still exist in the Brazilian state of Minas Gerais** — the same
  state as the Iron Quadrangle ([Charcoal iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal_iron)).
- **Brazil is the world's largest charcoal producer**, at **6,374,000 tonnes in 2020 — 11.8% of a world total of
  53,141,602 tonnes.**
- In 2015 Brazil produced **6,187,311 tonnes of charcoal, of which 87.1% came from silviculture** (planted forest)
  rather than from native-forest extraction. **Of the plantation charcoal, 98.8% came from eucalyptus.**
- **Minas Gerais produced 82.8% of Brazil's plantation charcoal**, followed by Maranhão (9.8%) and Bahia (2.1%) —
  **94.7% between them.** Both Minas Gerais and Maranhão are iron districts (the Iron Quadrangle and the Carajás rail
  terminus respectively)
  ([Carvão vegetal, pt.wikipedia, citing IBGE Pesquisa de Extração Vegetal e Silvicultura](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carv%C3%A3o_vegetal)).
- Brazil is the **sixth-largest producer and the world's largest exporter of pig iron**; the principal producing
  centers are **Timóteo, Itaúna, Sete Lagoas, Pitangui, Bom Despacho and Divinópolis** — all in Minas Gerais
  ([Ferro-gusa, pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferro-gusa)).

⚠️ **What I could NOT verify, and the author must know the question exists:** **the precise share of Brazilian pig
iron made with charcoal rather than coke.** The Portuguese-language encyclopedia states that Brazil is the world's
largest producer of charcoal-based pig iron, but **that specific sentence is explicitly tagged on its own page as
lacking a source** (`[carece de fontes]`), and I could not corroborate it from an industry or government source this
session. ResearchGate returned 403; the MDPI paper on charcoal mini blast furnaces returned 403; worldsteel's data
page returned no extractable content; FAOSTAT now requires authentication. **Do not treat any specific percentage as
established.**

⚠️ **My own order-of-magnitude arithmetic, clearly labeled as mine and not as a source:** charcoal-based ironmaking
consumes roughly 0.6–0.8 tonnes of charcoal per tonne of pig iron. If even two-thirds of Brazil's ~6.4 Mt of annual
charcoal goes to metallurgy, that supports something on the order of 5–7 Mt of pig iron per year, against Brazilian
crude steel of 35.4 Mt. **That would put charcoal-based iron somewhere in the range of 15–25% of Brazilian primary
iron** — a minority, but an industrially significant one, operating at a scale of millions of tonnes. The inputs to
that calculation are sourced; the calculation is my inference and the author should verify it before relying on it.

**Why this matters so much even at the low end of the range.** The load-bearing claim is *not* "Brazil makes most of
its iron with charcoal." It is the weaker and much better-supported claim:

> **Charcoal ironmaking from planted eucalyptus is a live, operating, millions-of-tonnes-per-year industry in the same
> Brazilian state that holds the Iron Quadrangle — which means primary ironmaking in Brazil demonstrably does not
> require a coal industry.**

That is a **pre-industrial-compatible reduction technology running at modern scale**, on a renewable feedstock, in
the exact place where the ore is. Eucalyptus coppices — it regrows from the stump — and on a Brazilian rotation it is
harvestable in six or seven years. A reconstruction society in Minas Gerais does not need to rediscover this or
import it. It needs to keep doing, at a smaller scale, something its great-grandparents were doing industrially.

**5. The conclusion.** Put the five legs together:

> **high-grade iron ore + niobium, manganese, molybdenum and nickel + gravity-fed hydroelectricity + charcoal from
> coppiced plantations + a century of scrap — all within one contiguous region, and the ore, the charcoal and the
> alloys in the same country.**

**South America is the part of the Western Hemisphere that can still make steel and electricity, and it is not
close.** The author's North American nations face "can this territory make iron?" as their leading open question,
with Alaska's resource inventory not even listing it. Brazil answers that question comprehensively, and answers it in
a form — high-grade ore plus renewable reduction fuel plus hydro — that is *more* compatible with a low-technology
reconstruction than the coal-and-taconite route the Northern Hemisphere depends on.

**The strategic consequence for the author's world is large and worth stating explicitly: in a post-collapse
hemisphere, finished metal flows north.** Whoever ends up holding Minas Gerais holds something no northern territory
in the author's existing material base can produce for itself. The Sonora territory — copper-rich, iron-poor,
tin-less — is the natural counterparty: its foundry needs Brazilian iron and Bolivian tin, while Brazilian steel
needs nothing from Sonora that Brazil does not already have. **That asymmetry is a trade geography, and it falls out
of the geology rather than from any assumption about who governs what.**

### 6.6 The Panama Canal's fate

**The canal is in better shape than most large infrastructure and worse shape than it looks.**

**In its favor:** the lock system is **gravity-fed** from Gatun Lake at 26 m above sea level. There is no pumping.
The water-reuse basins on the Neopanamax locks are also gravity-fed. The Culebra Cut, the Gatun Dam and the lake are
civil works. The canal was completed in 1914 and operated for a century with technology that a competent
nineteenth-century engineering establishment could maintain.

**Against it, and these are serious:**

1. **⚠️ The gates.** I could not verify this session whether the lock gates and valves are actuated electrically,
   hydraulically, or otherwise. The *water* moves by gravity; the *gates* are machinery, and a 2,000-tonne steel
   miter gate is not something a small society fabricates. **This is the decisive unknown and the author should
   resolve it.** If the gates can be worked manually or with modest power, the canal survives in reduced form. If
   they cannot, it does not.
2. **Water is already the binding constraint, before any collapse.** Transits were cut to 24/day in January 2024 and
   restricted again in 2026. Each transit dumps ~200 million liters of fresh water to the sea. Gatun Lake is
   simultaneously Panama City's drinking water.
3. **Silt.** Gatun Lake and the Culebra Cut require continuous dredging. Without it the channel shoals over decades.
4. **Traffic.** A canal is only worth operating if ships use it. In a world with little long-distance shipping, the
   labor to maintain it may simply not be justified.

**Most likely outcome, offered as a judgment:** the canal survives as a **much smaller waterway** — passable by
shallow-draft vessels and barges, operated intermittently, with the Neopanamax locks abandoned and possibly one of
the original lanes kept working. **Its water problem gets better, not worse, in a depopulated world**, because far
fewer transits means far less water drawn down and Panama City no longer competing for Gatun. The constraint shifts
entirely from water to **gate machinery and dredging**.

**Note the counterfactual the author may find more interesting:** the Panama Railroad, completed in 1855, crossed the
isthmus for 59 years before the canal existed, and a rail portage across 80 km is far easier to maintain than a lock
system. **If the locks fail, the isthmus does not stop being a crossing point** — it reverts to a transshipment
route, which is what it was for four centuries under Spain. That is a much more robust asset than the canal itself.

### 6.7 Who can feed themselves — carrying capacity by region

**Comfortable, rainfed, large surplus:**

- **The Pampas — Argentina, Uruguay, Rio Grande do Sul.** The clear standout. 1,200,000 km², temperate, evenly
  distributed 600–1,200 mm rainfall, deep soil, **no irrigation dependency**, and currently producing grain and beef
  far in excess of the ~50 million people who live there. **This region feeds itself trivially and has a large
  exportable surplus in any scenario.** It is the most food-secure large territory in the survey and one of the most
  food-secure on Earth. Uruguay in particular — small population, superb grassland, a river frontage, a port — is
  about as well-positioned as a territory can be.
- **Southern Brazil — Paraná, Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul.** Semi-temperate, higher rainfall, genuinely fertile
  soil rather than limed oxisol, and the country's rice bowl (Rio Grande do Sul produces 70.5% of Brazilian rice).
  This is the part of Brazil that farms without chemistry.

**Comfortable, pre-industrial, dense:**

- **The Andean highlands — Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, southern Colombia.** This is the best-documented case in the world
  of a large, dense, *state-level* population fed entirely without industrial inputs. The potato and quinoa complex,
  terraces, vertical-archipelago risk management, camelids, and freeze-drying (*chuño*) as a storage technology that
  needs only altitude and frost. **The Inca state fed millions on this basis.** Andean carrying capacity in 2283 is
  limited by terrace maintenance and labor, not by soil or seed.
- **The Pacific coastal valleys of Peru and Chile**, where Andean snowmelt rivers cross the desert — the same
  gravity-irrigation logic as the Sonora file's Yaqui valley, and farmed continuously for three thousand years.

**Adequate:**

- **Mexico's central highlands and the Bajío** — the maize-beans-squash homeland, farmed for millennia, with
  reasonable rainfall.
- **Central America's volcanic uplands** — genuinely fertile volcanic soils, good rainfall, the maize-and-beans
  package.
- **The Caribbean coast and the wet tropics generally** — cassava, plantain, fish. Not a grain surplus, but not
  hunger either.

**Poor, and a large downward revision from present output:**

- **The Cerrado.** §6.4. Reverts to grazing land. The population currently supported by Cerrado grain is not
  supported by Cerrado pasture.
- **Amazonia.** Poor soils, dispersed carrying capacity, riverine fishing and cassava. Supports a modest riverine
  population and nothing resembling a city.
- **The Caribbean islands.** The most exposed places in the survey. Small land areas, dense populations, economies
  built on tourism and imports, and in Jamaica's case an export monoculture in bauxite that becomes worthless. Cuba
  is the partial exception — large enough, with real agricultural land, and with recent historical experience of
  operating an agriculture under severe input scarcity. ⚠️ I did not research Caribbean food self-sufficiency in
  detail; flagged as a gap.
- **The Atacama and the Pacific desert coast.** No water, no soil. The mining towns of northern Chile exist because
  water and food are trucked and piped in. **Antofagasta, Calama and Iquique are as artificial as Phoenix and
  Tucson**, and they depopulate the same way — with the difference that the ore under them is a reason for *somebody*
  to stay, at a fraction of the scale.

**Cities likely to matter most:** Buenos Aires and Montevideo (river, port, Pampas behind them); Belo Horizonte
(iron, charcoal, and a temperate plateau); São Paulo and Santos, much reduced but industrially anchored; Lima (river
valleys, the fishery, and CIP); Cusco and the highland basins; Asunción (river, Itaipu, farmland).

### 6.8 Summary judgment for the author

**Latin America is the mirror image of La República de Sonora: it can feed a great many people and it can build
almost anything, but the two capacities are in different places and the Andes and Amazonia sit between them.**

Where Sonora is metal without water, South America is **metal *and* water *and* soil**, distributed across a
continent whose internal connectivity is the worst of any inhabited landmass of its size. The Pampas grow food in
quantities no one can eat and cannot easily move it to the Andes. Brazil can make steel and cannot easily ship it
over the cordillera. Chile has the copper, the solar and the lithium and almost no fresh water or farmland. **Each
piece of the continent is rich and none of them is complete, and the geography actively resists assembling them.**

The three findings the author should build on:

1. **South America is the hemisphere's foundry.** Brazilian high-grade iron, Brazilian niobium and manganese, Chilean
   and Peruvian copper and molybdenum, Bolivian and Peruvian tin, Brazilian nickel, and hydroelectricity from gravity
   — plus, uniquely, a **charcoal reduction route that needs no coal industry**, operating today in the same state as
   the ore. Nothing north of Panama has this combination. **Finished metal flows north**, and that single sentence
   restructures the trade geography of the author's entire world.
2. **The Pampas are the hemisphere's most secure farmland.** Rainfed, temperate, enormous, and currently feeding
   several times its own population. No dam, no pump, no aquifer, no lime.
3. **The Andes hold the seed stock.** 3,000 potato varieties in continuous cultivation, the crop that feeds marginal
   highlands better than any other, plus quinoa and a fully pre-industrial farming system that has already
   demonstrated it can support a state. This is the CIMMYT-grade fact of the southern hemisphere, and unlike a gene
   bank it survives by being planted every year.

And the three that limit it:

1. **The Cerrado is a chemistry experiment** and Brazil's headline agricultural output largely goes with it.
2. **The oil is a mirage.** The Orinoco Belt may be the largest petroleum reserve on Earth and is unusable without
   upgraders; the pre-salt and Vaca Muerta are gone outright. The continent's real liquid fuel is **sugarcane
   ethanol**, and its real energy is **falling water**.
3. **The continent cannot easily be a continent.** No through rail, an impassable cordillera, an unroadable basin,
   and a canal whose survival turns on whether a set of lock gates can be worked by hand. **The coherent material
   units here are river basins and port hinterlands**, each with better access to the sea than to its overland
   neighbors. That is an observation about terrain and transport cost, not a proposal about borders.
