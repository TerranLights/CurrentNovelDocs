# PART 1 — THE ALASKA HIGHWAY

## What it is

The Alaska Highway runs from **Dawson Creek, British Columbia** to **Delta Junction, Alaska**, by way of
**Whitehorse, Yukon**. It is not one road number but three stitched together: **British Columbia Highway 97**,
**Yukon Highway 1**, and **Alaska Route 2**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Highway

**Length.** About **1,700 mi (2,700 km)** as the wartime pioneer road was completed in 1942; **1,387 mi
(2,232 km)** as of 2012, after seventy years of realignment straightened and shortened it. The 1946 handover
figure was 1,422 mi (2,288 km) — which is why Delta Junction still calls itself "Historic Milepost 1422" even
though the drive there is shorter than that now. The informal milepost system is frozen at the 1947 alignment
and is no longer an accurate odometer reading; BC recalibrated in 1990, Yukon in three stages (2002, 2005,
2008), Alaska never. **Mileposts are place-names in this territory, not distances** — people and businesses
use them as postal addresses.

Delta Junction is the official end. From there the **Richardson Highway** carries on **96 mi (155 km)** to
**Fairbanks**, which is informally Historic Milepost 1520. Mileposts on that final stretch are counted from
the **port of Valdez**, not from Dawson Creek — a small fact that tells you what that road was originally for.

## Built in eight months, in 1942

Approved by the US Army **6 February 1942**, authorized by Congress and Roosevelt five days later,
construction officially begun **9 March 1942**. The US Army Corps of Engineers put **more than 10,000 men** on
it, **about a third of them Black soldiers** of the segregated 93rd, 95th and 97th General Service Regiments.

Four construction thrusts worked at once: southeast from Delta Junction toward Beaver Creek; north and then
west from Dawson Creek (with an advance party reaching Fort Nelson over **winter roads across frozen
marshland** from Northern Alberta Railways railheads); and both east and west from Whitehorse, which was
supplied **by the White Pass and Yukon Route railway** — see Part 3, because that is the dependency that
built the highway.

Crews met at **Mile 588, Contact Creek**, on the BC–Yukon border at the 60th parallel, on **24 September
1942**. The northern link-up came at **Mile 1202, Beaver Creek**, on **28 October 1942**. Dedication at
Soldier's Summit, November 1942 — the outdoor temperature censored from the radio broadcast for wartime
security.

**It was not actually usable by ordinary vehicles until 1943**, and not opened to the public until **1948**.
The 1942 road had steep grades, switchbacks to climb and descend hills, few guardrails, and bridges that
progressed from **pontoon** to **temporary log** before steel arrived.

**This is the single most useful precedent in the file for a reconstruction scenario: a continental-scale road
through subarctic wilderness, built in about eight months, by hand tools and bulldozers, with no precision
surveying and no permanent bridges.** It was bad, but it worked, and it worked immediately.

## Permafrost — the defining maintenance problem

The failure case is documented and specific. In **May and June 1943**, about **100 mi (160 km)** of new road
between **Burwash Landing and Koidern, Yukon** became **nearly impassable** because the construction had
stripped the insulating vegetation mat, the permafrost beneath thawed, and the roadbed dissolved. The fix was
a **corduroy road** — logs laid crosswise — and corduroy still lies under old sections there.

Modern practice is the opposite of 1942 practice: **do not let the permafrost thaw.** Either build a **gravel
berm on top** of the undisturbed ground, or strip the vegetation and soil and replace it with gravel
immediately, before the ground can warm.

**It does not entirely work.** The Wikipedia account is blunt about it: the **Burwash–Koidern section rebuilt
in the late 1990s continues to experience frost heave.** That is the most vulnerable identified stretch of the
highway and it has been the most vulnerable stretch for eighty years.

**⚠️ Not verified in this session:** current Yukon government pavement-condition ratings; the annual
maintenance budget; the status of the **Shakwak Project**, the long-running US-funded reconstruction of the
Yukon portion of the Alaska Highway and the Haines Road, which is widely reported to have lost its US funding
in the late 2010s leaving Yukon to carry the cost alone. **Shakwak is potentially a major finding and it is
unverified — chase it.** The Yukon government's own sites (`yukon.ca`) are Cloudflare-walled from this
environment, as the parent file records.

## Surface and ownership today

Paved end to end, **mostly with bituminous surface treatment** — chip seal, not structural asphalt. That
matters: chip seal is cheap, is resurfaced often, and degrades fast without maintenance. As late as the
**mid-1980s** the highway still had sections of winding dusty gravel between reconstructed paved segments.
The Alaskan portion was fully paved in the **1960s**; the lower 50 miles of the Canadian portion in **1959**,
the rest much later.

**Ownership and maintenance are split four ways:**

| Segment | Authority |
|---|---|
| Mile 0 – Mile 82.6 (km 133) | British Columbia ⚑ **CASCADIAN** |
| Mile 82.6 – Historic Mile 630 | Public Works Canada ⚑ **CASCADIAN territory, federal authority** |
| Historic Mile 630 – Historic Mile 1016 | **Yukon government owns it** |
| Historic Mile 1016 – US border (Historic Mile 1221) | Yukon manages it |
| Mile 1221 – Mile 1422 (Delta Junction) | **State of Alaska** |

Realignment has shortened the road by about **35 mi (56 km)** since 1947.

**Read that table again with the Panhandle exclusion in mind.** Roughly **630 of the highway's ~1,387 miles —
about 45 percent — lie in British Columbia**, which is Cascadian. Whitehorse sits at the far end of a road
whose entire southern half is foreign territory. See Part 5.

# PART 2 — THE ALASKA RAILROAD

## What it is

A **Class II** railroad, **state-owned** (transferred from the federal government to the State of Alaska on
**6 January 1985**, at fair market value), running freight and passenger service.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Railroad

- **Mainline: Seward to Fairbanks, over 470 mi (760 km).**
- **Total track including sidings, yards and branches: 656 mi (1,056 km).**
- Passes through **Anchorage** and **Denali National Park**. **17% of Denali's visitors arrive by train.**
- **Branch to Whittier**, which is the freight interchange point.
- 2025 ridership **227,600**; about **900 per weekday** as of Q2 2026. 2019: **$21.6 M profit on $203.9 M
  revenue**, **$1.1 billion in total assets**.

## Built 1903–1923, and it created Anchorage

The **Alaska Central Railroad** began building north from **Seward** in 1903 and got **51 mi (82 km)** down
before receivership in 1909. The **Alaska Northern Railroad** bought it in 1911 and added **21 mi (34 km)**.
On **12 March 1914** Congress agreed to fund an all-weather railroad from Seward to Fairbanks and bought out
the Alaska Northern. Estimated cost **$35 million**.

**The government opened a construction camp on Ship Creek, and that camp became Anchorage** — now the state's
largest city. In 1917 the government also bought the narrow-gauge **Tanana Valley Railroad**, chiefly for its
Fairbanks railyard. Completed **15 July 1923**, golden spike driven by President Harding at **Nenana**.

**The point worth carrying: Alaska's largest city exists because of the railroad, not the other way around.**

## It connects to no other railroad on the continent

**This is the load-bearing fact, and it is unambiguous.** The Alaska Railroad has no land connection to the
North American rail network. Freight railcars move to and from the contiguous United States **by rail barge**,
sailing between the **Port of Whittier** and **Harbor Island, Seattle**. Railcars are rolled onto a barge in
Puget Sound and rolled off at Whittier.

So the railroad's continental connection is **a marine service**, and the marine service runs from a
**Cascadian-adjacent** (Washington) origin through waters that include the Inside Passage. **The rail barge is
the fragile link, not the rails.** ⚠️ Rail-barge sailing frequency, annual railcar counts, and operator
(historically Alaska Rail Marine / Alaska Marine Lines) were **not verified** in this session.

Whittier is reached by road only through the **Anton Anderson Memorial Tunnel**, a single-lane, one-way-at-a-
time combined road-and-rail tunnel through Maynard Mountain. **A road-and-rail tunnel that alternates
direction is a chokepoint with a schedule.** ⚠️ Tunnel length (commonly cited at about 2.5 mi / 4.0 km) and
current operating schedule **not verified**.

## What it actually carries

⚠️ **This is the weakest part of Part 2 and it should not have been.** The Alaska Railroad's own corporate
pages returned **404** from this environment, so annual freight tonnage by commodity, carload counts, and the
freight/passenger revenue split **could not be obtained from a primary source.** Do not put a tonnage figure
in the fiction without sourcing it. `https://www.alaskarailroad.com/` — try again with a different path.

What **is** verifiable and structurally useful:

- **Coal.** A spur was built to deliver coal to a power station, and the parent file establishes that
  **Usibelli at Healy sits directly on the Alaska Railroad and the Parks Highway.** The railroad is how
  Alaska's only coal mine reaches Fairbanks and the Interior.
- **Petroleum products, gravel and military freight** move on it. The **Port of Alaska has two miles of rail
  spur connected to the Alaska Railroad, and the railroad's main cargo yard is immediately adjacent to the
  port** (Port of Alaska fact sheet, April 2025) — so the railroad is the port's inland distribution arm.
- **Cruise passengers.** A large share of the passenger business is charter service for **Holland America,
  Princess and Norwegian**, running between Whittier/Seward cruise berths and Anchorage, Talkeetna and Denali.
  Those trains use **dome cars owned by Tour Alaska, a Carnival subsidiary**, not by the railroad. The
  passenger business is substantially an appendage of the cruise industry.
- **The Hurricane Turn.** Between Talkeetna and Hurricane the railroad runs **one of the last true flag-stop
  trains in the United States**. Wikipedia's description is exact and worth quoting the substance of: the area
  has **no roads**, and the train is **the lifeline by which residents get food and supplies**. Passengers
  flag it down from the trackside.
- **Port MacKenzie rail extension.** A long-planned branch across Knik Arm from Anchorage, incomplete.
  ⚠️ Current status **not verified**.

**For a collapse scenario the Hurricane Turn is the most instructive thing on the railroad**: it is a
pre-automobile settlement pattern that never ended, sustained by a scheduled train, in a place with no road.

# PART 3 — THE WHITE PASS AND YUKON ROUTE

## What it is

A **3 ft (914 mm) narrow-gauge** railway from **Skagway, Alaska** to **Whitehorse, Yukon** — **107 mi
(172 km)**. Class III. **Reporting mark WPY.** Like the Alaska Railroad, **an isolated system with no direct
connection to any other railroad**; everything arrives or leaves through the port of Skagway or by road at a
few of its stops. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Pass_and_Yukon_Route

## Built for the Klondike, 1898–1900

Born of the **1897 Klondike Gold Rush**. The routes prospectors used — the **Chilkoot Pass** from Dyea and the
**White Pass** from Skagway — required each man to haul roughly **a ton of supplies** over the summit before
Canadian authorities would let him across, usually in many trips. In 1897 the Canadian government received
**32 proposals** for Yukon railroads; almost none were built.

Three companies organized in 1897 to build from Skagway toward **Fort Selkirk, Yukon, 325 mi (523 km)** away,
financed largely by British investors through **Close Brothers**. The builder, **Michael J. Heney**, chose
**3 ft gauge** deliberately: a narrow roadbed means far less rock to blast, and tighter curve radii mean the
line can follow the landscape instead of being cut through it. Even so, **450 tons of explosives** were used
to reach the White Pass summit.

Construction began **May 1898**. The first train in Alaska ran **4 mi (6.4 km)** out of Skagway on
**21 July 1898**. Rails reached the **White Pass summit, 20 mi (32 km) from Skagway, by mid-February 1899**;
Bennett, British Columbia next; and the **last spike was driven 29 July 1900**, with service from 1 August.
**By then the gold rush was largely over.** The line never reached Fort Selkirk; the money went instead into
buying most of the **riverboats**, giving White Pass a combined rail-and-river system from Whitehorse to
Dawson City.

Designated an **international historic civil engineering landmark** by the ASCE and the Canadian Society for
Civil Engineering in **1994**.

## The ore era and the 1982 shutdown

For most of the twentieth century the WP&YR was a **mineral railroad**, not a tourist one. **Ores and
concentrates were the bulk of the traffic.** The company also invented, at Skagway in the 1950s, what became
**intermodal container shipping** on this route — ship, rail and truck moving the same boxes.

**Wartime, 1942:** in the first nine months of 1942 the worn-out railroad moved **more than double its prewar
annual traffic**, and it still was not enough. The US government **leased the entire railroad** for the
duration and the Army's Military Railway Service ran it, scouring the country for usable narrow-gauge
locomotives. **Whitehorse was a principal staging point for building the Alaska Highway precisely because the
WP&YR could supply it.**

**No road existed between Skagway and Whitehorse until 1978.** For seventy-eight years the railway was the
only land route.

The **Faro lead-zinc mine opened in 1969**, and White Pass reinvested heavily — new ALCO locomotives, ore
buckets, a Whitehorse transfer facility, a new Skagway ore dock, a new tunnel and bridge bypassing Dead Horse
Gulch. **That investment made the railway dependent on ore revenue.** In **1982 metal prices collapsed**, Faro
and other mines shut, and **the White Pass and Yukon Route closed on 7 October 1982.**

**This is a clean, real precedent for a one-commodity transport system dying with its commodity** — and Faro
is the same Faro that the parent file lists as one of Canada's most expensive contaminated sites.

## Current status: tourist line, and it does not reach Whitehorse

Revived in **1988** as a **heritage railway**, driven entirely by **cruise-ship traffic at Skagway**. Rails
were relaid along the cruise docks. Reopening proceeded in stages: to **White Pass summit (1988)**, to
**Fraser, BC (1989)**, to **Bennett (1992)**, and a train reached **Carcross in 1997**.

**Track is certified by the Canadian Transportation Agency only as far as Carcross.** Skagway–Carcross is
**67.5 mi (109 km)** of the original **107 mi (172 km)** — **about 63% of the line is in use.** The remaining
~40 miles from Carcross to Whitehorse is **not in service**.

**In Whitehorse the railway is effectively gone from the ground.** Locomotives parked there for six years
were hauled out in 1988–89 and most were eventually sold. **Most of the track in downtown Whitehorse has been
torn up.** The line's nominal terminus is now six blocks south of the old depot at First Avenue and Main
Street. A single new waterfront track carries the **Whitehorse Waterfront Trolley**, a local historical
society's tourist operation.

**Ownership: purchased by Carnival Corporation & plc in July 2018.** Previously a Survey Point Holdings /
Carnival joint venture; before that a subsidiary of Tri White Corporation. **The railway is owned by a cruise
line.**

## ⚑ In this world it is an international line — and a stub

**Skagway is Cascadian. Whitehorse is Alaskan.** Three things follow, and they compound:

1. **The line's port end, its owner, and its entire current customer base are all foreign to the Alaska
   Republic.** Skagway's docks, the cruise ships, and Carnival are Cascadian or beyond.
2. **The line does not currently reach the Alaska Republic at all.** Service stops at **Carcross**, which *is*
   in the Yukon — so about 39 miles of certified track *does* lie inside the Republic — but the last ~40 miles
   to Whitehorse are derelict and the Whitehorse trackage is lifted. **The Republic's capital is not on the
   railway any more.**
3. **Customs and labor jurisdiction were already the hard part of reopening it.** The 1989–92 reopening was
   held up specifically by **customs and Canadian labor-union jurisdictional issues**, in a world where the
   border was a friendly one between allies. Make it a frontier between two post-war states and the friction
   is not hypothetical — there is a documented precedent for it.

**The useful narrative shape:** this is a railway that was built to cross this exact border, has crossed it
for a century, was closed for six years by a commodity price, reopened only for tourists, and is now owned by
a cruise company and stops 40 miles short of the territorial capital. **It is not a transport link. It is the
fossil of one — and the roadbed, grade, tunnels and passes still exist.** See Part 10(a): the *grade* is the
durable asset, not the service.

# PART 4 — PORTS

## Don Young Port of Alaska (Anchorage) — and the "90 percent" correction

Municipality-of-Anchorage-owned and -operated. Opened **1961** as the Port of Anchorage; **renamed Don Young
Port of Alaska by the Anchorage Assembly in January 2024** for the congressman who represented Alaska for 49
years and died in 2022. https://www.portofalaska.com/about-us/

**Throughput — primary source, the port's own Ten Year Dock Tonnage Report 2016–2025:**
https://www.portofalaska.com/wp-content/uploads/2025_Ten_Year_Tonnage_Report.pdf

| Commodity (tons) | 2025 | 2024 | 2020 | 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Petroleum, bulk dockside | 2,543,977 | 2,645,845 | 1,997,845 | 1,419,162 |
| Vans / flats / containers | 1,645,106 | 1,683,498 | 1,642,547 | 1,582,951 |
| Petroleum, shoreside | 1,184,221 | 1,036,306 | 902,439 | 368,708 |
| Dry bulk (cement) | 102,241 | 116,841 | 101,853 | 122,006 |
| **TOTAL TONS** | **5,476,366** | **5,483,779** | **4,704,101** | **3,498,171** |

**Total throughput grew 57% in a decade, and essentially all of the growth was petroleum.** Container tonnage
is close to flat — 1.58 Mt in 2016, 1.65 Mt in 2025, up 4% in nine years. **This is a fuel port that also
handles containers, and its trend line says so.**

**⚠️ The widely repeated "Anchorage handles 90% of goods entering Alaska" claim is wrong.** The port's own
wording, which is the origin of the confusion:

- **"Don Young Port of Alaska handles half of all Alaska inbound fuel and freight, which is distributed
  statewide and consumed by 90 percent of Alaska's population."** (Fact Sheet, April 2025)
- **"Accounts for 75 percent of all non-petroleum marine cargo shipped into Alaska, exclusive of Southeast
  Alaska."**
- **"More than 80 percent of vans and containers shipped into Southcentral Alaska ports."**
- **"Handles five times more inbound marine cargo than all other Southcentral ports combined."**

**Ninety percent is the population share. Half is the cargo share.** Use "half of Alaska's inbound freight,
reaching 90 percent of Alaskans" and you are on solid ground; use "90 percent of goods" and you are repeating
an error. Note also the **"exclusive of Southeast Alaska"** qualifier on the 75% figure — the Panhandle is
already carved out of the port's own statistics because **it is served by barges direct from Puget Sound.**
That is the port telling you, in its own marketing, that the Panhandle was never its market.

**Facilities:** 4,500 ft of dock frontage · three general cargo terminals · two 30-ton and one 40-ton gantry
crane · roll-on/roll-off · three petroleum terminals with fifteen tide-compensating lines · dry and break-bulk
handling · two floating small-vessel docks · dry-barge landing · **all berths dredged to at least 35 ft at
mean lower low water** · 125 acres of cargo yard · **59,200 tons of bulk cement storage** · **3.1 million
barrels of liquid fuel storage** · **two miles of rail spur connected to the Alaska Railroad.**

**Other specifics worth having:** **80 percent of all cement used in Alaska** moves through this port.
**Virtually all avgas used statewide** moves through it. **90 percent of liquid fuel shipped into Southcentral
Alaska**, including most of the jet fuel for JBER and half the fuel for Ted Stevens airport. **Half of all
port freight is delivered to destinations outside Anchorage.** Scheduled service is **twice weekly by Matson
and twice weekly by TOTE Maritime, both from the Port of Tacoma** — so the port's entire scheduled
containerized lifeline is **two carriers on a Puget Sound run.**

**Designated a US Commercial Strategic Seaport** supporting DoD, adjacent to JBER, and it handles most of the
Defense Department's inbound and outbound Alaska freight except munitions and explosives.

**Condition — and this is the part that matters.** The port is in the middle of a **Port of Alaska
Modernization Program** because the existing docks are corroded and seismically inadequate. In 2026: a
**federal lawsuit settlement securing $180 million** (July 2026), **construction beginning on a new cargo
terminal** (June 2026), and **$115.4 million in federal port grants** to Alaska (April 2026).
https://modernization.portofalaska.com/ · **The single port through which half of Alaska's freight and fuel
passes is currently being rebuilt because it was failing.**

**The Cook Inlet problem.** ⚠️ **Not verified in this session and it should be, because it is probably the
most important physical fact about this port:** Cook Inlet has among the largest tidal ranges in North America
(commonly cited around 30 ft at Anchorage), carries heavy glacial silt requiring continual dredging, and
**freezes with drifting ice in winter.** The port's fifteen **tide-compensating** petroleum lines are direct
evidence that the tidal range is extreme. Get NOAA Cook Inlet tide and ice data before writing a scene there.
https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/stationhome.html?id=9455920

## Valdez — the pipeline terminal

**Valdez Marine Terminal** is the southern end of the **Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS)**: an **800-mile
(1,287 km), 48-inch** pipeline from **Prudhoe Bay** to **Valdez**, with **12 pump stations**, built
**1975–1977**. First barrel reached Valdez **28 July 1977**. It is the **northernmost ice-free port in the
United States**, which is the entire reason Valdez was chosen.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Alaska_Pipeline_System

- **Over 19 billion barrels shipped as of 2026.**
- **Demonstrated capacity over 2 million barrels per day. Current throughput about 470,000 bpd** — roughly a
  quarter of what it can carry.
- **"If flow were to stop or throughput were too little, the line could freeze."** That sentence is the whole
  collapse scenario in one clause. A pipeline is not a passive tube; it is a heated, continuously-pumped
  system, and below a minimum flow rate it destroys itself. **TAPS cannot be mothballed and restarted. It
  fails closed.**
- Steel for the original 800 miles of 48-inch pipe was **ordered from Japan in 1969 because US mills did not
  have the capacity.** Hold that next to the parent file's finding that Alaska has no iron ore and no verified
  cement plant.
- The pipeline is **elevated on supports over permafrost** for much of its length — the 1970 warning that hot
  oil in a buried line would melt the permafrost and drop the pipe into mud is why.

**Valdez is also the zero point of the Richardson Highway**, which is why mileposts between Delta Junction and
Fairbanks count from Valdez. And the town's population went from **1,350 in 1974 to 8,253 in 1976** during
construction — a useful shape for a boomtown.

⚠️ **Not verified:** number and size of tanker berths, annual tanker calls, crude storage tank capacity at the
terminal, and the post-*Exxon Valdez* escort-tug and double-hull regime. All are gettable from Alyeska.

## Seward and Whittier — the railroad's two saltwater doors

**Seward** is the **southern terminus of the Alaska Railroad mainline** and the point from which the whole
system was built north beginning in 1903. Ice-free, on Resurrection Bay.

**Whittier** is the **freight interchange**: rail barges run between the **Port of Whittier** and **Harbor
Island, Seattle**, and that barge is the Alaska Railroad's only physical connection to the North American rail
network. Whittier's road access is through the **Anton Anderson Memorial Tunnel**, at nearly **2.5 mi (4 km)**
**the longest combined road-and-rail tunnel in North America** — single bore, one direction at a time, on a
schedule. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Alaska

Both handle cruise ships; both are AMHS ports. ⚠️ Tonnage figures for Seward and Whittier **not obtained.**

## Dutch Harbor / Unalaska — the fisheries port

On **Amaknak Island**, connected to Unalaska by bridge; **59 percent of the city's population lives on
Amaknak, which is under 3 percent of its land area.** Site of the **Dutch Harbor Naval Operating Base and Fort
Mears**, bombed by Japan in June 1942.

**The largest fisheries port in the United States by volume caught, in nearly every year since 1981** — the
parent file records **780.1 million pounds and $224.5 million ex-vessel in 2023, #1 for the 26th consecutive
year.** Since 2000 New Bedford, Massachusetts has beaten it on dollar value.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unalaska,_Alaska

**Its weather is the point.** On **25 October 1977** an extratropical system put **926 millibars** on the
barometer at Dutch Harbor — **the lowest non-tropical pressure ever recorded in the United States**, until
**31 December 2020**, when another Aleutian system read **924.8 mb**. Winds over **100 knots** wrecked
shipping. The **Aleutian Low** is a semi-permanent winter feature, and **North Pacific extratropical cyclones
typically reach maximum intensity there**; the November 2011 and November 2014 Bering Sea cyclones both went
**below 950 mb**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_Low

**Dutch Harbor is also a transshipment point**, and the Port of Alaska notes that **Kodiak and Dutch Harbor
get scheduled container service from Matson vessels running between Anchorage and Tacoma** — they are stops on
the Anchorage run, not independent gateways.

## Nome — the shallow port, and the deep-water project

**Current configuration** (Corps of Engineers Nome Harbor Improvements Project, completed summer **2006**):
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nome,_Alaska

- A **3,025 ft (922 m) breakwater** east of the existing causeway, and a **270 ft (82 m) spur** bringing the
  causeway to **2,982 ft (909 m)**.
- **City Dock: 200 ft long, 22.5 ft depth (MLLW).** Handles the community's bulk cargo and fuel deliveries
  through marine headers.
- **WestGold Dock: 190 ft long, 22.5 ft depth.** Handles nearly all exported rock and gravel for the region
  and is the heavy-equipment berth.
- **Outer Harbor Entrance about 500 ft wide.**
- **Small Boat Harbor: 10 ft depth (MLLW).**
- A **60 ft wide concrete barge ramp** added 2005, with **2 acres** of upland for container and equipment
  storage, so bulk carriers can trans-load freight to **landing craft** for delivery around the region.

**Read the depths again: 22.5 feet.** Compare Anchorage's berths at 35 ft. **Nome is not a deep-water port; it
is a lightering and trans-load point that hands cargo to shallow-draft landing craft.** That is the physical
mechanism by which the whole Bering Sea coast is resupplied.

## 5.2 The Gulf of Alaska is not the Inside Passage, and the ferry system proves it

This is the part that is usually asserted and rarely substantiated. **It substantiates itself out of the
Alaska Marine Highway's own operating history**, which is a sixty-year natural experiment in whether ordinary
vessels can work these waters. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Marine_Highway

**Three documented facts, in order of how damning they are:**

1. **Until 1967, the stretch between Southeast Alaska and Washington was legally classified as "outside
   waters," and *not one* AMHS vessel held the ocean-going certification needed to carry passengers on it.**
   When the governor ordered a ship south to Seattle anyway, the fix was not to build ocean-capable ferries —
   **it was to petition Congress to have the route reclassified as inside waters**, which the federal
   government did. The certification problem was solved by redefining the water.
2. **Cross-gulf sailings did not exist until 1998**, and began only when the **MV *Kennicott*, "a vessel
   certified to operate in open waters,"** joined the fleet. Before that, **Southeast and Southcentral Alaska
   were two disconnected ferry systems**, because nothing in the fleet could cross the Gulf of Alaska.
3. **The Aleutian run is a summer-only service.** In 2008 the MV *Tustumena* went out the chain to Dutch
   Harbor **once a month, April to October**, calling at Chignik, Sand Point, King Cove, False Pass, Akutan
   and Cold Bay. **The trip is not made in winter because of adverse weather conditions.**

**And the weather numbers back it.** The **Aleutian Low** is a semi-permanent winter low-pressure center near
the Aleutians, one of the largest circulation features in the Northern Hemisphere; **North Pacific
extratropical cyclones typically slow down and reach maximum intensity there.** Dutch Harbor holds the record
for the **lowest non-tropical barometric pressure ever recorded in the United States — 926 mb on 25 October
1977**, beaten only by **924.8 mb on 31 December 2020**, also in the Aleutians. Winds over **100 knots**.
The November 2011 and November 2014 Bering Sea cyclones both dropped **below 950 mb**.

**Summary for the fiction: the Alaska Republic's coastline is one where a state ferry system spent thirty
years unable to sail from one half of it to the other.** Not because of politics — because of the sea. Losing
the Inside Passage is not losing a scenic route. It is losing the only water on this coast that an ordinary
hull can work year-round.

## 5.3 The Alaska Marine Highway, counted community by community

**3,500 mi (5,600 km) of routes, 32 terminals across Alaska, British Columbia and Washington.** Part of the
**National Highway System** and federally funded as a highway. Headquartered in **Ketchikan**; main hub
**Juneau**.

**Of the 33 communities AMHS serves year-round, 16 — very nearly half — are outside the Alaska Republic:**

**⚑ CASCADIAN (14 in the Panhandle):** Angoon, Gustavus, Haines, Hoonah, Juneau, Kake, Ketchikan, Metlakatla,
Petersburg, Sitka, Skagway, Tenakee Springs, Wrangell, Yakutat.
**⚑ CASCADIAN (2 southern):** Bellingham, Washington and Prince Rupert, British Columbia.

**Alaska Republic (17):** Akutan, Chenega Bay, Chignik, Cold Bay, Cordova, False Pass, Homer, King Cove,
Kodiak, Ouzinkie, Port Lions, Sand Point, Seldovia, Tatitlek, Unalaska/Dutch Harbor, Valdez, Whittier.

**Now the killer detail.** The Wikipedia account states where the ferry system touches the continent:
**"The Southeast System connects with the continental road system at Bellingham, Washington, Prince Rupert,
British Columbia, and in Alaska at Haines and Skagway."**

**All four of those are Cascadian.** Every single point at which the Alaska Marine Highway hands cars and
freight to a road that leads anywhere is in the neighboring country.

**So the Panhandle exclusion does this to the ferry system:**

- **Its headquarters (Ketchikan) and its principal hub (Juneau) are both foreign.**
- **Fourteen of its year-round ports are foreign.**
- **All four of its road-system connections are foreign.**
- **Its two largest operating subsystems — the Southeast mainline routes and the Southeast day-boat feeder
  routes — are almost entirely foreign.**
- **What remains to the Alaska Republic is the Southwest system**: Prince William Sound (Valdez–Cordova–
  Whittier–Tatitlek–Chenega Bay), the Kodiak–Homer–Seldovia–Port Lions run, and the seasonal Aleutian chain
  run. **All of it in open water. Most of it seasonal.**

**The Alaska Marine Highway does not survive the partition as a system. It survives as two or three regional
boat routes.**

## 5.4 How the Yukon reaches tidewater — and the answer is that it does not

**Whitehorse is landlocked.** Every road from it to salt water crosses into Cascadia. There are exactly three,
and all three are documented below with the border crossing marked.

### Route A — South Klondike Highway to Skagway

**Yukon Highway 2 / Alaska Route 98.** Whitehorse to Skagway, **about 110 mi / 177 km.** ⚠️ The road distance
is commonly cited and was not verified against a primary source here; the **rail** distance on the parallel
WP&YR is firmly **107 mi (172 km)**, so the road figure is at least the right size.

**Where the border is:** the Klondike Highway runs **24 km (15 mi) in Alaska** and **56 km (35 mi) in British
Columbia** before entering the Yukon. **Coming south from Whitehorse you leave the Alaska Republic at the
Yukon–BC boundary, cross 56 km of British Columbia, and then cross into the Alaska Panhandle at White Pass
summit — two frontier crossings, both into Cascadia, in 80 km.** The port is only 15 miles from the second
one. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klondike_Highway

**History note that matters:** **this road did not exist until 1978.** It was begun in the 1950s, abandoned
partway up a mountain out of Skagway, and resumed only in 1976–77 with the **Captain William Moore Bridge**.
Completed August 1978, closed within weeks for the season; first full summer of use 1979. From 1986 it became
a serious commercial route, as the WP&YR railway died. **Seasonal closure is normal on this road.**

### Route B — Alaska Highway plus Haines Highway to Haines

**Whitehorse to Haines Junction** on the Alaska Highway: Haines Junction is **Historic Mile 1016** and
Whitehorse sits around Historic Mile 900–920, giving roughly **100 mi / 160 km.** ⚠️ **Derived from mileposts,

**Haines Junction to Haines** on the **Haines Highway (Haines Road / Haines Cut-Off, Yukon Highway 3 / Alaska

**British Columbia.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haines_Highway

**Total Whitehorse–Haines: roughly 250 mi / 400 km**, with **two border crossings** — Yukon into BC, BC into
the Alaska Panhandle. Both into Cascadia.

The road follows the **Dalton Trail**, itself following a **Chilkat Tlingit trading route** inland from the
coast. **The oldest documented route from the Yukon interior to tidewater is an Indigenous trade road, and it

## 5.5 The deeper cut — the Alaska Highway itself

**The Panhandle exclusion is not the only border problem, and the bigger one is usually missed.**

**The Alaska Highway — the Alaska Republic's only land route to the rest of the continent — begins in Dawson
Creek, British Columbia and runs about 630 of its ~1,387 miles inside British Columbia before it reaches the
Yukon.** British Columbia is Cascadian.

So: **Anchorage and Fairbanks connect to the continent by a road whose first 45 percent is foreign, ending at
a highway system that is entirely foreign.** There is no overland route from the Alaska Republic to any other
country in North America that does not transit Cascadia. **Not one.**

Add the maritime picture and the shape is complete:

- **Land:** the single road out goes through Cascadia.
- **Rail:** the Alaska Railroad connects to nothing; its barge link runs to Seattle through Cascadian-adjacent
  water. The WP&YR is Cascadian-owned and stops short of Whitehorse.
- **Ferry:** the Marine Highway's headquarters, hub, half its ports, and **all four** of its road connections
  are Cascadian.
- **Sea:** what is left is the Gulf of Alaska and the Bering Sea, which the state's own ferries were not

- **Air:** unaffected by borders in peacetime — and the first thing to stop in a collapse (Part 7).

**The Alaska Republic is not merely a country without the Inside Passage. It is a country whose every surface
connection to the rest of the world runs through one neighbor.** If the author wants a single structural fact
to build the nation's politics on, that is it.

# PART 6 — THE DALTON AND DEMPSTER HIGHWAYS

## The Dalton Highway — Alaska Route 11

**414 mi (666 km)**, from the **Elliott Highway north of Fairbanks** (the junction is near **Livengood**) to
**Deadhorse**, an unincorporated community inside the Prudhoe Bay CDP on the Arctic Ocean.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton_Highway

**It is a pipeline service road that the public was later let onto.** Built in **1974** to support
construction of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline System, and it **directly parallels the pipeline** for its whole
length. **390 mi (630 km) of it were built in five months in 1974** — a second, later instance of the same
wartime-tempo road-building the Alaska Highway demonstrates. The pipeline itself was not finished until 1977.

**There was an earlier failure worth keeping.** In 1968–69 a 400-mile **winter road**, the "Walter J. Hickel
Highway," was pushed from Livengood to Prudhoe Bay and completed in March 1969. **Poor engineering exposed the
permafrost, it thawed, and the road was abandoned by April of the same year** — one month. That is the
permafrost lesson in its most compressed form.

Alyeska handed the road to the state in 1979. Public access was restricted to **Mile 211 (Disaster Creek)**
until 1994; the permit system was struck down by the Alaska Supreme Court in 1993 and **the full length opened
to the public on 2 December 1994.**

**Condition and character:**

- **Mostly gravel**, "very primitive in places." As of July 2013 only **129 mi (208 km) were paved**, in seven
  disconnected stretches.
- **Grades up to 12%.**
- Crosses the Brooks Range at **Atigun Pass, 4,739 ft (1,444 m)** — the highest point, and the highest
  year-maintained pass in Alaska.
- **Three settlements on 414 miles:** **Coldfoot (pop. 34)** at Mile 175, **Wiseman (pop. 12)** at Mile 188,
  and **Deadhorse** at Mile 414 — **25 permanent residents, but 3,500–5,000+ seasonal** depending on oil
  activity. Prospect Creek and Galbraith Lake are uninhabited outside camping season.
- **Fuel at exactly three points:** the **E. L. Patton Yukon River Bridge (Mile 56)**, Coldfoot, and Deadhorse.
- **Nearest medical care is Fairbanks or Deadhorse. Nothing in between.**
- **Traffic: about 160 trucks a day in summer, 250 a day in winter.** Note the direction of that — **the
  Dalton is busier in winter**, because frozen ground carries loads that thawed ground will not.

**Two documented failure modes, both climate-driven:**

1. **Sagavanirktok River flooding**, combined with melting ice roads under warmer conditions, has forced
   **weeks-long closures** and repairs costing millions.
2. **"The Blob."** In 2018 a **4,000 ft (1,200 m)** section about **200 mi (320 km) north of Fairbanks** had to
   be physically relocated to escape a **debris flow roughly 1 mi (1.6 km) long** — dirt, ice and trees —
   advancing at **15 ft (4.6 m) per year**. It is expected to threaten the new alignment again **within about
   twenty years.** The road was moved; the mountain was not.

**Deadhorse has no public road onward.** Beyond Mile 414 the roads are private oil-company property, closed to
unauthorized vehicles. **The Dalton reaches the Arctic Ocean and then stops at a gate.**

## The Dempster Highway — Yukon Highway 5 / NWT Highway 8

**736 km (457 mi)**, beginning **40 km (25 mi) east of Dawson City** on the Klondike Highway and running
north-northeast to **Inuvik, Northwest Territories**, on the Mackenzie Delta. It crosses the **Ogilvie** and
**Richardson** mountains and **Tombstone Territorial Park**.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dempster_Highway

**There are no highway or major road intersections along its entire 736 km.** Not one. The **Eagle Plains
Hotel** is the only lodging on the road.

**It follows an old dog-sled route** from Dawson City to Fort McPherson, and is named for the NWMP corporal
who in March 1911 found the bodies of the **Lost Patrol** — four men who died of exposure and starvation after
losing the trail between Fort McPherson and Dawson City. **The road is named after a search for men who froze
to death on the route the road now follows.** That is real, and it is the correct emotional register for this
corridor.

**Construction** began at Dawson City in **January 1959**, driven by an oil discovery at Eagle Plains that
August. Work stalled after **114 km (71 mi)** in 1962 when Eagle Plains proved commercially worthless. It
resumed in **1970**, after the American Prudhoe Bay discovery of 1968, explicitly as a **sovereignty** project
— Canada asserting presence in its own Arctic — and in anticipation of a Mackenzie Valley pipeline that was
never built. **Opened 18 August 1979.**

**Permafrost engineering:** the entire road is built **on top of a gravel berm** so that heat does not reach
the ground. Same principle as the modern Alaska Highway; same principle the Hickel Highway violated.

**River crossings — the seasonality trap.** The Dempster crosses the **Peel River** and the **Mackenzie River**
(at Tsiigehtchic) **with no bridges**. Both are served by **ferries in summer and ice bridges in winter**.
**Between the two seasons — during freeze-up and break-up — there is no crossing at all**, and Inuvik's road
link to the continent simply ceases to exist for some weeks twice a year. See Part 8.

**Onward:** the **Inuvik–Tuktoyaktuk Highway** opened **November 2017**, giving year-round road access to
Tuktoyaktuk and creating **the first all-weather road route connecting the Canadian road network to the Arctic
Ocean**. ⚠️ Its length (commonly given as 138 km / 86 mi) was **not verified here**.

## The two together

The Dalton and the Dempster are the only two all-season public roads in North America that cross the Arctic
Circle. **Both were built for oil. Neither carries a general economy.** One is gravel with three fuel stops in
414 miles and dies at an industrial gate; the other has no intersections at all in 736 km and is severed twice
a year by rivers it never bridged.

## Anchorage as a global air-cargo hub

**Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport (ANC) was the world's third-busiest cargo airport in 2025 at
3,854,614 tonnes**, behind only Hong Kong (5,070,256 t) and Shanghai Pudong (4,096,016 t). It was **fourth in
2024 (3,699,284 t)** and **fourth in 2023 (3,380,374 t)**, and in 2023 it was **the busiest cargo airport in
the United States**. During 2020 it was **briefly the busiest airport in the United States outright**, because
cargo volumes held while passenger travel collapsed.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_airports_by_cargo_traffic

**Why it is there is pure geography.** Anchorage sits on the great-circle route between East Asia and North
America, and **cargo carriers flying Asia–Lower 48 prefer to refuel at Anchorage so they can carry less fuel
and more payload.** The airport advertises itself as **"less than 9.5 hours by air from 90 percent of the
industrial world."** By the mid-1980s its nickname was "Crossroads of the World," back when passengers
stopped there too.

**A regulatory privilege makes it work.** The US Department of Transportation lets Anchorage and other Alaskan
airports be used as a **transfer point for cargo between different aircraft of the same foreign carrier**
without special permission — **a right not available at any airport in the contiguous United States.**

**On the ground:** **FedEx Express** operates the largest cargo facility, handling up to **13,400 packages per
hour** with **1,200+ employees** and full customs clearance; **UPS** handles about **5,000 parcels per hour**.
The **USPS** runs a large sectional center facility for the 995xx ZIP codes, processing mail for every Alaskan
city.

**The crucial distinction, and it is easy to get wrong.** **ANC's 3.85 million tonnes is overwhelmingly
transiting cargo that never enters Alaska.** It is a fuel stop and a transfer point. Compare: the Port of
Alaska moved **5.48 million tons** in 2025 and **that** is the freight Alaskans actually consume. **ANC is a
gas station on a trans-Pacific highway that happens to be sited in Alaska.** For the Alaska Republic this
matters enormously: **the third-busiest cargo airport on Earth sits inside a country of under a million
people, and almost none of that tonnage is for them.**

## Bush aviation and the two subsidy systems

Below the hubs — Anchorage, Fairbanks, Bethel, Nome, Kotzebue, Dillingham — sit regional commuter airlines and
then **bush flying services**, scheduled and chartered, on general-aviation aircraft. **Aircraft are used
extensively for freight, not just passengers.**

**Two federal programs are load-bearing, and both are subsidies:**

1. **Essential Air Service (EAS).** Created after the Airline Deregulation Act let airlines abandon
   unprofitable routes. DOT subsidizes passenger and freight service to communities too small to support it.
   **About 60 Alaskan communities are served by EAS.**
2. **The Bypass Mail System.** Created to relieve congestion at mail processing centers by letting high-volume
   shippers bypass postal facilities entirely and hand freight straight to airlines. **USPS pays the airlines
   to fly it, but charges shippers ordinary USPS ground rates** — the principle being that a customer in rural
   Alaska should pay what a suburban customer pays. Payment is at an "intra-Alaska mainline service rate," an
   average of a pool of carriers' costs. **Some carriers draw as much as 60 percent of their revenue from
   Bypass Mail contracts**, which is what lets them fly passengers as often as they do.

**Read that last sentence carefully, because it is the whole structure of rural Alaskan aviation.** The
passenger service that connects four hundred communities is **cross-subsidized by a federal groceries-by-air
program.** Village air service is not a market outcome. **It is a postal policy.**

# PART 8 — WINTER AND SEASONALITY

## The system is two different systems

**In summer, this territory moves on water.** Barges on the Yukon, the Tanana and the Kuskokwim; landing craft
along the Bering coast; ferries in the Gulf; the sealift to the Arctic coast.

**In winter, it moves on ice.** Ice roads, snow roads, winter trails, ice bridges over the Peel and the
Mackenzie and the Yukon at Dawson City, snow machines, and — historically and still ceremonially — dog teams.

**Between the two it does not move at all.** Freeze-up and break-up are the two annual intervals when water is
neither liquid enough to float a barge nor solid enough to carry a truck, and during them **surface transport
in much of the territory simply stops.**

## Freeze-up and break-up in practice

**The Dempster Highway** crosses the **Peel** and **Mackenzie** rivers with **ferries in summer and ice
bridges in winter, and no bridges at all.** Between those two states, **Inuvik's road connection to the
continent ceases to exist.** The same arrangement governs the **Yukon River at Dawson City**: a car ferry in
summer, an ice bridge in winter. A permanent bridge was announced in March 2004 and **shelved because the bids
came in far over budget** — so the gap is still there, twenty years on.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_River

**Seasonal weight restrictions.** On northern highways, **spring axle-load limits can be cut by as much as 20
percent** because the ground goes soft as it thaws. **The road is legally weaker for part of every year.**

**The Dalton Highway is busier in winter than in summer** — **about 250 trucks a day in winter against 160 in
summer** — because frozen ground carries loads that thawed ground will not. **In the North, winter is the
construction and haulage season, not the dead season.** That inverts the intuition most readers bring.

**Nome's freeze-free period is 78 days.** Average first and last freezing lows: **2 September and 9 June**.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nome,_Alaska

## River barge seasons — the hard numbers

**Yukon and Tanana rivers: barges of about 4.5 feet of draft.** Service is private, and it delivers **most of
the bulk fuel and material shipments for villages in the area.** **The tight ice-free season means some
villages receive barge service only three or four times a year.** **The only permanent port barge facilities
are the rail/barge transfer sites at Nenana** — where the Alaska Railroad hands freight to the river — **and
most bulk fuel is loaded onto barges at North Pole.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Alaska

**That is the whole Interior supply chain in one sentence: Puget Sound → Anchorage or Whittier by ship →
Alaska Railroad to Nenana/North Pole → barge down the Tanana and Yukon → village. Four modes, three transfers,
one season.**

**The Kuskokwim, documented from local reporting (KYUK, Bethel):**

- **First fuel barge of the 2021 season reached Bethel on Monday 7 June**, carrying **300,000 gallons of
  heating fuel and 150,000 gallons of aviation fuel**, with unleaded gasoline following that week.
  https://www.kyuk.org/economy/2021-06-09/first-fuel-barge-of-season-arrives-in-bethel-signaling-price-increases
- **The last barge of the 2023 season was still trying to reach Nunapitchuk on 1 November**, eleven days late,
  after **getting stuck in the ice on the Kuskokwim below Kwethluk** for a couple of days. The operator flew
  **aerial reconnaissance on 31 October** to see whether the Johnson River was still passable.
  https://www.kyuk.org/2023-11-01/nunapitchuk-awaits-the-final-fuel-barge-of-the-season
- **Nunapitchuk's fuel ran out over Halloween weekend** while that barge was en route, and residents got fuel
  from neighboring villages in the meantime. **Its new tank farm holds about one month's supply.**
- **The village was served last because it filed its paperwork last.** Delivery order is administrative.

**So the practical Kuskokwim window is roughly early June to late October, and the last delivery of the year is
a race against ice that is sometimes lost.**

## Ice roads, snow roads and trails — three different things

The Alaska DNR/BLM distinction is worth keeping exact:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Alaska

| Type | Construction | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| **Winter trails** | Unimproved, marked with blazes where there is tall vegetation and **tripods or plastic stakes where there is none** | Dog sleds, snow machines |
| **Snow roads** | Packed snow | Dog sleds, snow machines, **4WD vehicles traveling in convoy** |
| **Ice roads** | Frozen water | **Heavy equipment** |

**"Many interior communities are connected by seasonal ATV trails when the weather is cold enough to freeze
otherwise impassable grounds."** The ground being frozen is what makes it a road.

**Bethel has winter ice roads to several nearby villages, condition varying with temperature and snowfall, and
an extensive snow-machine trail network reaching villages from the Bering Sea to the Yukon.** In areas with no
road or rail, **summer travel is by ATV and winter travel is by snow machine.**

## The Iditarod Trail — the pre-industrial network, still mapped

**The Iditarod National Historic Trail is about 2,400 mi (3,900 km) of trail from Seward to Nome**, declared a
National Historic Trail in 1978. It **began as a composite of trails established by Alaska Native peoples**,
and in the gold-rush era it linked mines, trading posts and settlements.

The **Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race** runs **1,150 mi (1,850 km)** Anchorage to Nome, commemorating the **1925
serum run to Nome**, when dog teams carried diphtheria antitoxin **"when all other means of transportation had
failed."** The **Kuskokwim 300**, run from Bethel every January since 1980, **commemorates an early mail
route.**

**Put those together and you have the answer to a question Part 10 has to ask.** The territory's
pre-industrial transport network was not lost. **It was surveyed, mapped, federally designated, and is
ceremonially traversed every single year.** Alaska maintains, as sport and heritage, a live working knowledge
of how to move people and freight across it without engines.

# PART 9 — FUEL AND RESUPPLY LOGISTICS

## How it actually works, in the state agency's own words

**Primary source: Alaska Energy Authority, Bulk Fuel Upgrade Program.**
https://www.akenergyauthority.org/What-We-Do/Rural-Energy/Bulk-Fuel-Upgrade-Program

The AEA's description is the best short statement of the territory's basic condition that exists anywhere, and
the substance of it is this: **rural Alaska runs on liquid fuel — diesel for power generation and heating,
gasoline for transportation.** Villages sit **either on rivers or on the coast**, so **fuel comes primarily by
barge.** Where barging is unavailable or uneconomic, it comes by **air tanker**, and in a few cases by **tanker
truck**. **Delivery is seasonal, limited by sea or river ice, water levels, or ice road availability.** And
the sentence that should be carved over the whole file:

**"Villages of a few hundred people must store hundreds of thousands of gallons of fuel to meet their annual
energy needs."**

**That is the settlement pattern of this territory in one line.** A village is not a place where people live.
**It is a tank farm with people around it.**

## The tank farms are old and failing

Also from AEA: **many of rural Alaska's bulk fuel facilities were built in the 1950s and 1960s or earlier**,
were **not built to national standards or in compliance with current regulations**, and **some are at the end
of their useful lives** — yet remain in service until replaced, which AEA acknowledges puts both people and
the surrounding country at risk in some communities.

**AEA's program has shifted from new construction to maintenance**, reusing existing tanks where they can be
repainted or repaired, and replacing piping, pumps, valves and tanks at end of life, **using local workers and
equipment.** The stated benefits are revealing: **eliminating spills from leaking tanks lets a community
"use all fuel purchased"** — i.e. some communities are currently losing fuel to leaks they cannot afford to
lose.

## What it costs

**Verified, May 2026, Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta (KYUK):** fuel in **Bethel and nearby villages was running $7–$8
per gallon**, and **in some coastal communities it topped $9 per gallon.**
https://www.kyuk.org/economy/2026-05-20/western-alaska-prepares-to-lock-in-what-may-be-unaffordably-high-fuel-prices-for-the-year

**One other reported figure, flagged:** an Alaska Beacon headline of 3 June 2026 gives a village gasoline
price of **$8.44 per gallon** before that summer's price shock. ⚠️ **Headline only — alaskabeacon.com returned
403 and the article could not be read. Re-source before use.**

**The structural cruelty, which the KYUK reporting makes explicit:** because barge delivery is seasonal,
**a village buys its entire annual fuel supply at whatever the price happens to be on the day the barge
arrives, and then lives with that price for a year.** There is no averaging, no hedging, no buying the dip.
The president of the **Alaska Village Electric Cooperative** framed the consequence precisely: people who
cannot afford heating fuel run electric heat instead and **"get to the springtime and realize, well, now I've
got a $10,000 bill."**

**Named operators:** **Crowley Fuels** and **Vitus Energy** both appear in the Kuskokwim reporting. ⚠️ The
full list of rural fuel distributors, their tonnages, and their fleet composition **was not researched.**

## The subsidy layer

⚠️ **Power Cost Equalization (PCE)** — the Alaska program that subsidizes residential electricity rates in
rural communities to bring them nearer urban rates — **is real and is administered by AEA, but no figures were
obtainable in this session** (the AEA site's document links and the Alaska Fuel Price Report at
commerce.alaska.gov both refused requests). **PCE is a first-order dependency for every off-road community in
the territory and it is a gap in this file.** Get the annual **PCE Statistical Report** from AEA: community
count, average rural cents-per-kWh against Anchorage, and average utility diesel cost per gallon.

**The stack of subsidies keeping rural Alaska supplied is therefore at least four deep:**

1. **Essential Air Service** — subsidizes the passenger and freight flights.
2. **Bypass Mail** — subsidizes the freight, and thereby the flights again.
3. **Power Cost Equalization** — subsidizes the electricity the diesel produces.
4. **Bulk Fuel Upgrade** — pays for the tanks the diesel sits in.

**None of these is a market. All four are transfers from outside the region.** That is the single most
important thing in this file for the post-collapse assessment, and it is why Part 10 is as blunt as it is.

# PART 10 — POST-COLLAPSE ASSESSMENT

⚠️ **Sections (a), (b) and (c) are sourced inference — the facts are cited above, the conclusions are mine and
are labeled as such.** Section (d) is bluntly speculative and says so.

## (a) Survives regardless — geography does not need maintenance

**The routes exist as terrain whether or not anyone maintains them.** This is the most important thing to hold
onto, and it is why the territory is not simply doomed.

- **The mountain passes.** **White Pass (20 mi from Skagway)**, **Chilkoot Pass**, **Atigun Pass (4,739 ft)**,
  the Richardson/Ogilvie crossings on the Dempster, the Coast Mountain notch the Haines Highway uses. **These
  are the only places the ranges can be crossed, they were found by Indigenous traders and gold rushers on
  foot, and they will still be the only places.** Every route in this file follows a line somebody walked
  before there was a road.
- **The river channels.** The **Yukon** (330,000 sq mi of drainage, 125,000 of it in Canada), the **Tanana**,
  the **Kuskokwim**, the **Mackenzie**. These were the highways before 1900 and would be again. **Note the
  4.5-foot barge draft figure**: the modern Interior barge trade already operates in water shallow enough for
  a shallow-draft wooden vessel. **The Yukon carried paddle-wheel riverboats through the Klondike rush, and
  White Pass bought a riverboat fleet rather than build a railway to Dawson.** That is a pre-industrial
  solution that was chosen over an industrial one on economic grounds, and it worked.
- **The harbors.** **Resurrection Bay (Seward)**, **Prince William Sound (Valdez, Whittier, Cordova)**,
  **Kachemak Bay (Homer)**, **Norton Sound (Nome)**, **Dutch Harbor**, **Kodiak**. Anchorage's own harbor is
  the doubtful one — **Cook Inlet's tides, silt and winter ice** make it the least naturally forgiving major
  harbor in the territory. ⚠️ Flagged as inference; verify the Cook Inlet ice and tide data.
- **The Iditarod Trail system — 2,400 mi of mapped trail from Seward to Nome**, federally designated, built
  originally by Alaska Native peoples, and **traversed by dog team every March.**
- **Valdez is the northernmost ice-free port in the United States.** That is a fact about the ocean, not about
  the pipeline, and it does not change.
- **Anchorage's great-circle position** is geometry. It stops mattering the moment there are no aircraft, and
  it starts mattering again the moment there are.

## (b) Needs maintenance — repairable with effort, in rough order of ease

- **Gravel roads.** The **Dalton** was built 390 miles in five months in 1974; the **Alaska Highway** was
  pushed 1,700 miles through in about eight months in 1942 with bulldozers and hand labor. **Gravel road is
  within reach of a low-technology society that has fuel and machines, and the grades and cuts already exist.**
  Rebuilding is far cheaper than building.
- **Corduroy and log bridges.** The 1942 highway ran on **pontoon bridges, then temporary log bridges**, and
  the fix for the Burwash–Koidern permafrost failure in 1943 was **corduroy road — logs laid crosswise.**
  **These are timber-and-labor technologies, and the territory has spruce.** There is a working replica log
  bridge at Canyon Creek, rebuilt in 1987.
- **Ice roads, snow roads, winter trails.** **Essentially free.** They need cold, marking stakes, and
  knowledge. They are already how much of the Interior connects in winter.
- **River barging.** Shallow-draft, seasonal, and the channels do not need dredging in the way a deep-water
  port does. **Nenana already exists as a rail-to-river transfer point.**
- **The Alaska Railroad — repairable, and the highest-value single asset.** 470 miles of mainline over
  relatively benign terrain, connecting **Seward and Whittier (tidewater) → Anchorage (the population and port
  center) → Healy (the only accessible coal in the territory) → Fairbanks (the Interior hub and the head of
  the Tanana barge route).** In 2015 it carried **more than 4.3 million tons of freight.** **And its original
  locomotives were coal-fired** — the preserved ARR 557 was built coal-burning and only converted to oil in
  1955. **A coal mine on the line and a railroad that was designed to burn coal is the single most
  reconstructible industrial system in this file.** [Inference, from sourced facts.]
- **The Alaska Highway and the Cassiar.** Paved with **bituminous surface treatment — chip seal**, which
  degrades quickly without resurfacing but reverts to a usable gravel-and-aggregate road rather than
  disappearing. **Expect them to become gravel roads again, not to vanish.** The **Burwash–Koidern permafrost
  section** is the first to fail and has been failing since 1943.
- **The White Pass and Yukon Route — the surprise candidate.** The **grade, cuts, tunnels and bridges over
  White Pass already exist**, the line is **narrow gauge** (cheaper and lighter to maintain), it **ran on
  steam into the 1950s and still operates a steam locomotive, No. 73**, and it was built with **hand labor and
  black powder in two years, in the 1890s, by a society with no internal combustion.** **Of every rail asset
  in the territory, this is the one a pre-industrial society could plausibly operate.** Its problem is not
  technical — it is that **both its ends are now in different countries.**
- **The Trans-Alaska Pipeline — probably not, and this is the important negative.** It is **800 miles of
  48-inch pipe with 12 pump stations**, and **"if flow were to stop or throughput were too little, the line
  could freeze."** **TAPS fails closed.** It cannot be idled and restarted. The steel had to be bought from
  Japan in 1969 because American mills could not make it. **Treat the pipeline as a one-way asset: it works
  until the day it stops, and then it is 800 miles of the best scrap steel on the continent.**

## (c) Vanishes — the things that are not infrastructure but services

- **Air cargo, immediately and completely.** ANC's **3.85 million tonnes** is a trans-Pacific refueling and
  transfer operation for an intercontinental economy. No intercontinental economy, no tonnage. And **FedEx's
  13,400 packages an hour and UPS's 5,000 an hour are not scalable down** — they are the throughput of
  automated sorting facilities.
- **Bush aviation, on a delay.** Not because the aircraft vanish — **there are 16× as many aircraft per capita
  here as anywhere else in the country** — but because **avgas comes through the Port of Alaska ("virtually
  all avgas used statewide")**, the flights are paid for by **Bypass Mail and Essential Air Service**, and
  **some carriers get 60 percent of their revenue from federal mail contracts.** **Remove the federal
  government and the aircraft are still there and the flying economy is not.** Expect a short, intense period
  of flying on stockpiled fuel and then a hard stop. [Inference.]
- **Containerized resupply.** Two carriers — **Matson and TOTE, twice weekly each, from the Port of Tacoma**.
  That is the entire scheduled container lifeline for a subcontinent. **1.65 million tons a year of vans and
  containers arrive on a service with two operators and one origin port.**
- **The fuel-barge economy.** Crowley and Vitus barges, **$7–$9 a gallon**, **300,000 gallons of heating fuel
  at a time into Bethel**, tank farms holding **a month's supply**, **three or four deliveries a year** on the
  Yukon. This chain begins at a refinery that is not in the village, moves on diesel-powered vessels, and is
  scheduled by paperwork. **All of it stops.**
- **The four-deep subsidy stack** — EAS, Bypass Mail, Power Cost Equalization, Bulk Fuel Upgrade. **These are
  transfers from a federal government. They are the first thing to go and they are what the settlement pattern
  is made of.**
- **The Alaska Marine Highway as a system.** Its headquarters, its hub, half its ports and **all four of its
  road connections** are in the other country (Part 5.3).
- **The Alaska Railroad's continental link.** The rail barge to **Harbor Island, Seattle** is a commercial
  marine service, not a piece of track.
- **Deep-draft port operation.** **Anchorage's 35-foot berths** and **Nome's proposed 40-foot channel** exist
  because of continuous dredging in a silt-laden, high-tide-range inlet and a shallow Arctic roadstead. **Stop
  dredging and the depths go away.** Nome's real, undredged reality is **22.5 feet** and **lightering to
  landing craft** — which, conveniently, is also what a low-technology port looks like.

## (d) The blunt part — what happens to the communities

**The task asks for bluntness, so: most of the off-road communities as presently constituted do not survive,
and the reason is fuel, not food.**

**Put the numbers from this file next to the parent file's numbers and the argument makes itself:**

- **82 percent of Alaska's communities are not on the road system; about 400 depend on aviation for year-round
  access.** (AK DOT&PF)
- **A village of a few hundred people stores hundreds of thousands of gallons of fuel a year.** (AEA)
- **It buys that fuel three or four times a year**, or once, **at whatever price the barge brings**, and its
  tank farm may hold **one month's supply.** (AEA, KYUK)
- **The parent file's subsistence data:** rural Alaskans harvest **276 lb of wild food per person per year**,
  which supplies **176 percent of protein requirement but only 25 percent of calories.** Subsistence harvest
  is **0.9 percent of all fish and game taken in Alaska** — **the resource is not the constraint.**

**So the food is there and the heat is not.** These are settlements at 60–70° north in places where **Nome's
freeze-free period is 78 days**, living in frame houses heated by imported diesel, lit by diesel generators,
supplied by a seasonal barge and a subsidized airplane. **The wild food will keep coming. The diesel will not,
and no amount of salmon heats a house.**

**What the historical record says actually happens, and it is not extinction.** The pre-contact and
early-contact settlement pattern in this territory was **smaller, more mobile, sited for fuel and food rather
than for an airstrip, and built of materials that did not have to arrive by ship.** The communities that exist
now are largely where they are because of **a mission, a trading post, a cannery, a school, a post office or a
gold camp** — Bethel is where it is because Moravian missionaries moved it there in the 1880s; Nome is where
it is because of beach gold; Anchorage is where it is because of a railroad construction camp; Deadhorse is
where it is because of oil; Valdez is where it is because the ocean does not freeze there. **Expect
contraction, consolidation onto the river mouths and the protected bays, and a return to seasonal movement
rather than year-round fixed villages.** [This is reasoning from the sourced settlement histories above, not a
sourced prediction. Flagged.]

**Which routes work with pre-industrial technology, specifically:**

| Route | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Yukon River, Whitehorse → Dawson → Tanana → the Delta** | **Works.** Paddle-wheel riverboats did it through the 1890s rush; White Pass chose riverboats over rails. Seasonal. |
| **Kuskokwim, Bethel upriver and down to the Bay** | **Works.** Shallow draft, already the local highway. Seasonal. |
| **Iditarod Trail, Seward → Nome (2,400 mi)** | **Works.** Dog team and snow machine. Winter. Done annually. |
| **Chilkoot and White Pass, Skagway/Dyea → Bennett → Whitehorse** | **Works, brutally.** Men carried a ton of goods over it in multiple trips in 1898. It is also the frontier now. |
| **Haines Highway corridor, Haines → Haines Junction** | **Works.** It is the **Dalton Trail**, which is a **Chilkat Tlingit trade route.** Oldest verified interior-to-tidewater road in the file. |
| **Alaska Highway, Dawson Creek → Whitehorse → Delta Junction** | **Works as a track**, not as a highway. Reverts to gravel; Burwash–Koidern fails first; bridges are the limiting factor. 1,387 mi is a season's travel, not a day's. |
| **Cassiar 37 / Yellowhead 16, Whitehorse → Prince Rupert (~890 mi)** | **Works as a track.** Long, thin, empty, and two-thirds of it foreign. |
| **Alaska Railroad, Seward/Whittier → Anchorage → Healy → Fairbanks** | **Works if anything does.** Coal on the line; locomotives originally coal-fired. **Best reconstruction asset in the territory.** |
| **WP&YR, Skagway → Whitehorse (107 mi narrow gauge)** | **Works technically; blocked politically.** Grade and tunnels exist; steam locomotive extant; 40 mi of track lifted at the Whitehorse end. |
| **Dalton Highway, Livengood → Deadhorse (414 mi)** | **Fails.** Three fuel stops, three settlements totaling ~70 permanent people, 12% grades, Atigun Pass, and a terminus at an abandoned industrial site. Nothing at the far end worth reaching without oil. |
| **Dempster, Dawson City → Inuvik (736 km)** | **Marginal.** No intersections in 736 km, one hotel, and **two unbridged rivers** that cut it twice a year. It was a dog-sled route first and would be again. |
| **Gulf of Alaska and Bering Sea coastal sailing** | **Hard, and this is the real constraint.** The state's own ferries could not cross the Gulf until 1998 and will not sail the Aleutians in winter. **926 mb and 100+ knots at Dutch Harbor.** Coastal sailing here is a summer trade for skilled crews in small, handy vessels — which is exactly what it historically was. |
| **Inside Passage** | **Works beautifully — and is Cascadian.** |

**The one-sentence version.** *The Alaska Republic keeps every route it ever had as terrain, loses every route
it has as a service, and discovers that the protected water, the ferry headquarters, the only other railway,
and every road to the sea are all in the country next door.*

## Things that conflict or need care

- **Alaska Highway length:** ~1,700 mi (1942 pioneer road) / 1,422 mi (1946 handover) / **1,387 mi (2012,
  current)**. Not contradictions — different dates. **Historic mileposts are not driving distances**, and the
  Alaska Highway article says so explicitly. Alaska never recalibrated; BC and Yukon did.
- **AK DOT&PF aviation page** presents **"82%"** and **"402 communities"** as if they were the same statistic.
  They are not. Both are quoted in Part 7 with the discrepancy marked.
- **Port of Anchorage "90 percent."** Addressed at the top of the file and in Part 4. **Half the cargo, ninety
  percent of the population.**
- **WP&YR length:** 107 mi (172 km) Skagway–Whitehorse; 67.5 mi (109 km) Skagway–Carcross, the certified
  portion; ~63% of the original line in use.
- **Units.** Alaska and Port of Alaska sources use **short tons**; airport cargo rankings use **metric
  tonnes**; Canadian road sources use **kilometers**. Units are preserved as the source gave them and labeled.
