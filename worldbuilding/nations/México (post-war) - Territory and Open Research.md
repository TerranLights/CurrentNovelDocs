## 1. Settled — how Mexico's 32 federal entities divide

Three neighboring nations each take a bite. Everything left over is still México — smaller than the México we
know today, but the same country, not a rename.

| Destination | Mexican estados taken | Non-Mexican territory in the same nation |
|---|---|---|
| **La República de Sonora** | Sonora, Chihuahua | Arizona, New Mexico |
| **Cascadia** | Baja California, Baja California Sur | California (+ its existing Pacific Northwest core) |

## 2. Open research — does Jalisco gain real leverage against CDMX from this?

**Author's question, 2026-09-25: with Sonora, Chihuahua, both Bajas, and the five Yucatán-bound estados gone,
does Jalisco end up with a much stronger bargaining position against Mexico City?** Researched, not settled —
the finding is more interesting and more mixed than a yes/no.

**The straightforward version doesn't hold up.** 2024 INEGI figures: CDMX is 15.0% of national GDP, Jalisco
7.5% — almost exactly 2:1. Removing Sonora (3.3%), Chihuahua (3.8%), Baja California (3.9%), Baja California
Sur (~1%, ⚠️ not directly verified), Chiapas (2.7%), Tabasco (2.2%), Campeche (1.7%), Yucatán (1.6%) and
Quintana Roo (1.5%) strips out roughly **21–22% of national GDP.** But CDMX's and Jalisco's own absolute GDP
figures don't change — only the shared denominator shrinks. Run the numbers and their ratio to each other
barely moves (still close to 2:1 either way). **Removing unrelated third states doesn't change how two
survivors weigh against each other — that's arithmetic, not something specific to this scenario.**

**If this mechanism benefits anyone specifically, it's Nuevo León, not Jalisco.** Monterrey's economy (8.1%
of national GDP) is larger than Jalisco's (7.5%), and Nuevo León is the state that was seriously discussed as
a breakaway candidate (the "Monterrey-anchored norteño polity" option, closed this session — Nuevo León
stays). It sits in second-or-third place among the survivors, ahead of Jalisco either way.

**The stronger, oil-revenue version of the instinct exists — but cuts the other way given current data.**
Historically PEMEX funded up to 40–54% of the federal budget, mostly off Campeche's Cantarell field, which
would have made "México loses its independent oil money and gets more dependent on Jalisco's tax base" a real
argument. But Cantarell collapsed from 2.1 million barrels/day at its 2004 peak to under 100,000 today, and
PEMEX now *requires* federal bailouts — about $22 billion USD in transfers in 2025 alone. Losing Campeche in
the present-day baseline this project uses is closer to losing a liability than a cash cow. This specific
argument doesn't survive contact with current numbers; it would have been much stronger set twenty years
earlier.

**What does hold up, more modestly:** the estados leaving are disproportionately Mexico's export-manufacturing
periphery (the maquiladora border states) and its tourism coast. What's left recenters on the Bajío industrial
corridor — Jalisco, Guanajuato, Querétaro, Estado de México — plus Nuevo León and CDMX. Jalisco's specific
profile (diversified tech/aerospace/electronics — Guadalajara is called "Mexico's Silicon Valley" — a large
enough agricultural base to matter for food security, and tequila as a legally protected, non-substitutable
export good) makes it more load-bearing to whatever's left of the country's *productive* economy, even though
its size relative to CDMX hasn't moved. That's real leverage, just a softer and more diffuse kind than "bigger
GDP share" — and it's shared with Nuevo León and Guanajuato, not unique to Jalisco.

**Net read:** the suspicion is partially right, but for a different reason than raw size, and Nuevo León has
at least as strong a claim to it as Jalisco does — possibly stronger, since it's the larger economy and the
one that was actually on the table as a secession candidate.
