# The Transit Problem — Alaska Republic and Cascadia

2026-09-22. **Three possible resolutions, in full.** None is chosen; this exists so the choice can be made
deliberately. Universe-wide worldbuilding, not scoped to any one book.

---

## The Problem, Stated Precisely

Per `Alaska Republic - Highway Network (read from map reference).md`, of the four overland routes out of the
Alaska Republic's capital at Whitehorse, **only two stay inside the country**:

- **North to Inuvik** — Klondike Highway to Dawson City, then the Dempster. Internal (Yukon → NWT).
- **West to Anchorage** — Alaska Highway to Tok, then the Glenn Highway. Internal (Yukon → Alaska).
- **East to Yellowknife** — **crosses into British Columbia and back out.** From Watson Lake the route drops
  south across the 60th parallel for the Northern Rockies stretch of the Alaska Highway, runs to Fort Nelson,
  then turns north on the Liard Highway to re-enter the NWT at Fort Liard.
- **South to tidewater** — Cascadian almost immediately, whether by the Cassiar Highway or via Skagway.

Two further facts sharpen it:

1. **The Alaska Highway itself is 45% Cascadian.** About 630 of its ~1,387 miles are in British Columbia. So
   Anchorage and Fairbanks also reach the continent only through Cascadia.
2. **There is no road up the Mackenzie Valley between Wrigley and Inuvik.** The northern loop that would close
   an internal circuit does not exist as a highway — only as a summer barge run and a winter ice road.

**The distances, now quantified** (from `material-base/Yellowknife - City Study and Intra-Republic
Connections.md`, derived from published segment lengths, ⚠️ ±40 km):

- **Whitehorse → Yellowknife by road: ≈1,919 km / 1,193 mi, of which ≈650 km / 404 mi is inside Cascadia** —
  roughly a third of the journey, about 22 hours' driving.
- **The internal water route: ≈3,060 km to cover 1,110 km of straight-line distance** — a 2.8:1 detour — and
  open only **mid-June to mid-October, about 120 days a year.**
- **There is no complete internal winter route either.** The Wrigley–Fort Good Hope winter road runs 486.4 km
  and then stops; no maintained route north of Fort Good Hope is listed.

**The consequence:** a sovereign state whose capital cannot reach its own second city, or any deep-water port,
without transiting a neighbor — and whose internal alternative is a seasonal river that nearly triples the
distance and closes for two-thirds of the year. Something has to account for that. Three candidates follow.

---

# Resolution A — A Transit Agreement

**The Republic and Cascadia maintain a treaty granting road, rail and possibly port transit rights.**

## Why this is the strong default

**It is what actually happened in the real world, in this exact corridor.** The Alaska Highway was built by the
United States across Canadian territory in 1942 under a wartime agreement, and handed to Canada in 1946. The
arrangement never ended: under the **Shakwak Project**, the United States has funded reconstruction and
maintenance of the Yukon section of the Alaska Highway and the Haines Road — one country paying to keep up a
road inside another country's borders because it depends on the route. The Alaska Marine Highway ran its
ferries to Prince Rupert, British Columbia, on the same logic.

So this is not an invented arrangement. It is the arrangement that has governed this geography for eighty
years, and the fiction would simply be inheriting it.

## What it looks like in practice

- **Transit rights** for civilian road traffic and freight on the Watson Lake–Fort Nelson–Fort Liard corridor.
- **Maintenance cost-sharing**, most likely the Republic paying for upkeep of roads it does not own — exactly
  the Shakwak pattern.
- **Port access** at Skagway, Haines, Prince Rupert or Vancouver, without which the Republic has no protected
  deep-water outlet at all, since the Inside Passage is entirely Cascadian.
- **Possibly rail**: the White Pass and Yukon Route from Skagway to Whitehorse is now an international line.
- A **customs and inspection regime** at the crossings — and in an era before robot personhood, that regime
  necessarily includes rules about property in transit, which is to say, about dolls.

## What it costs the Republic

**A permanent dependency and a permanent lever.** Cascadia can slow it, price it, restrict categories of
cargo, or close it outright. Every Cascadian political shift becomes an Alaskan domestic crisis.

And the real-world precedent shows the fragility is not hypothetical: **Shakwak funding lapsed around 2019**
and only partly resumed in 2024. A road agreement is not a border — it is a budget line in someone else's
parliament, and budget lines get cut.

## Story affordances

- Negotiators, delegations, and the ordinary machinery of a relationship that has to be renewed.
- Tariffs, quotas, inspection regimes, and the smuggling economy that grows around them.
- A closure — even a threatened one — is an instant national emergency with no military dimension required.
- Border crossings as ordinary, frequent, bureaucratic events rather than dramatic set pieces.

## What it forecloses

A Republic that is genuinely self-contained. Under Resolution A the country is structurally junior in one
specific, humiliating way, and everyone in it knows it.

# Resolution B — The Republic Builds Its Own Road

**After the war and the settlement of borders, the Alaska Republic completes the Mackenzie Valley Highway.**

## What gets built — and a crucial correction: it is under construction right now

**This is not a stalled proposal.** As of **8 September 2026** the Mackenzie Valley Highway has **$405 million
committed**, a national-interest process running, **six simultaneous construction segments**, and — the detail
that matters most — **34 bridges already standing** along the winter-road route. **Completion target: 2032.**

### ⚠️ Critical correction — the funded phase does NOT reach Inuvik

Verified 2026-09-22 against the GNWT's own project page and the Developer's Assessment Report (links in the
Map References section below). In the Government of the Northwest Territories' own words:

> The current phase of the MVH proposes to replace approximately **320 km** of the seasonal Mackenzie Valley
> Winter Road by extending all-season road access from the end of Highway 1 at **Wrigley, to Tulita and
> Norman Wells. Phase 2 would extend the highway from Norman Wells to Inuvik.**

So the real-world project splits:

- **Phase 1 — Wrigley → Tulita → Norman Wells, ~320 km.** Funded, under construction, targeted at 2032. Three
  segments: Wrigley to the Dehcho–Sahtu border (102 km), Tulita south to that border (134 km), and Tulita
  north to the Prohibition Creek Access Road (45 km).
- **Phase 2 — Norman Wells → Inuvik.** *No funding, no timeline, no assessment underway.*

**This matters a great deal, because Phase 2 is the part that closes the internal circuit.** A highway that
stops at Norman Wells does not connect to the Dempster and does not link Yellowknife to Whitehorse. It ends
roughly 800 km short of Inuvik, in the middle of the valley, with the winter road continuing beyond it to
Fort Good Hope and Colville Lake exactly as before.

**Revised framing for the fiction:** the real world is building the *southern half* of the Republic's internal
link and has not committed to the northern half. That gives three postures, all more interesting than a simple
completed road:

- **The road stops at Norman Wells forever**, and the internal link is never closed. Resolution B fails on its
  own terms, and the Republic is left with A or C.
- **Phase 2 gets built later** — by a pre-war Canada, or by the post-war Republic itself as its founding
  national project. The latter is the strongest version: a new country finishing what the old one abandoned.
- **Phase 2 was underway when the war came.** Half-built road, standing bridges, abandoned camps — 800 km of
  unfinished sovereignty lying in the valley.

That changes the shape of this resolution entirely, and in the fiction's favor. The war is in **2083**. If the
highway completes on schedule, it was finished **roughly fifty years before the war** — so the post-war
question is not *would they build it* but **did it survive, and can it be reopened.**

Three framings follow from that, and they are meaningfully different stories:

- **B1 — It was built, and it held.** The Republic inherits a functioning internal road and the transit problem
  largely disappears, leaving only the tidewater dependency. The least dramatic option.
- **B2 — It was built, and it failed.** Fifty years of permafrost, washouts, and no maintenance budget. The
  bridges stand; the roadbed does not. Reopening it is the national project, and the ruins of a completed
  highway are a more evocative object than a road that was never begun.
- **B3 — It was never finished.** The 2032 target slips, as northern megaprojects do, and the war arrives with
  the road still incomplete — perpetually under construction, a sunk cost nobody can abandon.

**B2 is the strongest of the three**, because it gives the Republic both the memory of self-sufficiency and the
present fact of dependence, and because a standing bridge over a road that no longer exists is exactly the kind
of image this setting runs on.

The route the highway closes is the gap between **Wrigley and Inuvik**, running north along the Mackenzie
through Norman Wells and Fort Good Hope. With it, the Republic gains a fully internal circuit:

> Whitehorse → Dawson City → **Dempster Highway** → Inuvik → **Mackenzie Valley Highway** → Norman Wells →
> Wrigley → Fort Simpson → Fort Providence → **Yellowknife**

No foreign transit at any point.

## What it costs

**Enormous, and the research says so plainly.** That is roughly 800 km of highway through continuous
permafrost, muskeg and major river crossings, in a territory that — per
`material-base/Alaska Republic - Northwest Territories and Nunavut (supplement).md` — **could not fund the
Tibbitt to Contwoyto winter road for the 2027 season**, and where the ice-road season is shortening. The
Inuvik–Tuktoyaktuk Highway, a mere 138 km, took three winters of round-the-clock work.

**And maintenance, not construction, is the real problem.** Permafrost highways do not simply persist; they
heave, slump and drain. The real-world corridor already demonstrates the failure mode — **the 2024 Sahtu barge
season failed completely from low water**, fuel had to be flown into Norman Wells, and the town declared a
state of emergency. A post-collapse state inherits the roadbed and the climate, not the maintenance budget.

And the resulting route is **worse in every practical sense**: a vast dogleg north to the Arctic Ocean and back
down again, to reach a city that lies due east. Substantially longer than the Cascadian route, on gravel,
through country with almost no population to service it, and subject to the Dempster's own ferry and ice-bridge
shoulder seasons at both freeze-up and break-up.

## Which is exactly the point

**Nobody builds this road because it is good. They build it because it is theirs.**

A state that spends a generation's construction budget on a longer, harder, emptier route — when a perfectly
serviceable highway already exists through the neighbor's territory — has made a statement about sovereignty
that no treaty could make. The road *is* the foreign policy.

It also changes the character of Resolution A rather than replacing it: with an internal alternative in
existence, the Cascadian corridor stops being compulsory and becomes negotiable. **The redundant road is what
makes the agreement bearable.**

## Story affordances

- A permanent national project: expensive, slow, behind schedule, politically totemic. Generations grow up with
  it unfinished.
- The Mackenzie Valley communities — Norman Wells, Fort Good Hope, Tulita — transformed by being on a road for
  the first time.
- Construction as a setting: camps, seasonal labor, engineering against permafrost.
- A completion date is a national event; a cancellation is a national humiliation.

## What it forecloses

A small, modest Republic. Resolution B requires a state capable of sustained heavy construction in the Arctic,
which implies real industrial and fiscal capacity, and a government able to hold a policy across decades.

# Resolution C — It Is Never Solved

**The east stays loosely held. The Republic accepts that it cannot reach its own eastern territory overland.**

## What this looks like

- Yellowknife functions as a **semi-autonomous eastern seat** rather than a province administered from
  Whitehorse — closer to a dominion or an associated territory than a region.
- The east is supplied as it is in the real world: **summer barge down the Mackenzie** from Hay River, and
  **winter ice roads**. Both seasonal, both weather-contingent, both with shoulder seasons when nothing moves.
- Air links carry people and urgent freight; everything heavy waits for the river or the ice.
- Practical authority drains toward whoever is physically present. Distance becomes governance.

## Why it is the realistic outcome

**The population does not justify anything else.** The Northwest Territories hold roughly 45,000 people, and
Nunavut around 40,000, across a third of a continent. **Yellowknife alone is 45% of the NWT's entire
population.** Real states do not build 800 km of Arctic highway for 85,000 people, and real states are often
content to hold remote territory loosely.

It is also consistent with what the research found about the settlement pattern: these communities already
depend on subsidized resupply — Essential Air Service, Bypass Mail, Power Cost Equalization, the Bulk Fuel
Upgrade program. **None of that is a market; all of it is a transfer.** A post-collapse state with less money
does less of it, and the east becomes more remote rather than less.

## Story affordances

- A country with a frontier *inside itself*, and citizens who have never seen the capital.
- Yellowknife as a rival center of gravity — its own hydro, its own economy, its own reasons to resent or
  ignore Whitehorse.
- Separatism, or quiet practical independence that nobody bothers to formalize.
- The barge season and the ice-road season as the real calendar of eastern life, with everything timed to them.
- Nunavut further out still: **25 communities, no road connection to anywhere, one sealift a year.**

## What it forecloses

A unified national narrative. Under Resolution C the Alaska Republic is less a country than a federation of
regions that happen to share a flag, and its eastern half is somewhere its own government cannot easily go.

# Assessment

**A and B are not exclusive, and the strongest configuration uses both.**

Resolution **A is the working reality** — the corridor through Cascadia is what people actually use, because it
is shorter, paved for much of its length, and already there. Resolution **B is the national project** —
perpetually under construction, perpetually over budget, never quite finished, and existing precisely so that
the agreement in Resolution A is a negotiation rather than a demand.

That pairing generates its own politics. Every Cascadian concession makes the expensive road harder to justify;
every Cascadian slight makes it easier. The construction schedule becomes a barometer of the relationship.

**Resolution C is the honest fallback** if the Republic should read as weaker, poorer, or more provisional —
and it can coexist with A, since a country can maintain a transit agreement and *still* hold its east loosely.
C is incompatible with B only in spirit: a state that finishes the Mackenzie Valley Highway has decided the
east matters, and C is the world where it decided otherwise.

**Recommended if a single answer is wanted:** A as fact, B as aspiration, C as the condition of the far
northeast (Nunavut) regardless of which applies to the Mackenzie corridor.
