## 2. What *Starlit Engines* needs from the Republic

Stated as questions the setting must answer.

1. **What is a Whitehorse railyard in this world, and who owns it?** In the real world there is none to speak of: the White Pass line is certified only to Carcross, the Whitehorse track is lifted, and the owner is a cruise line (note: Transport supplement Part 3). The novel needs an operating yard with engines that need an expert mechanic and a human boss over Lucy.
2. **What do the locomotives burn, and where does it come from?** Lucy fixes "any engine." The fuel decides whether the yard is a symbol of dependence or of improvisation.
3. **How does the Republic treat, register and tax a robot worker?** Lucy works 12 to 16 hour days under a human boss who does not value her (note: Lucy sheet). The law must make that legal and ordinary.
4. **What happens at a crossing when a robot travels with a human who does not own her?** Only Lucy's party actually leaves the Republic. Jennifer and Human #2 travel inside Cascadia (Vancouver, Prince George, Kitwanga), so the Territory file's phrase "both dolls travel this route" is inexact. Jennifer crosses no national border unless the plot later sends her north (note: Central BC Route file).
5. **What is the Republic's government, for a story that mostly happens in the wild?** The state must be present enough to make the rules, and thin enough to be absent on the Cassiar.
6. **What does the Republic want from Cascadia, and what does it fear?** This is the substance behind Blocking Decision 5.
7. **Who owns Lucy, and who owns Jennifer?** The cast file calls Human #1 "Lucy's human" and Human #2 "Jennifer's human" (note: `cast of characters.md`). The brief says the humans do not own them. If Lucy belongs to the yard, leaving with Human #1 is a legal event, not a resignation. PROPOSED handling is in 3(f).

## 3. Proposed extrapolations

### (a) One-line character summary: three candidates

| # | Candidate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A | *A country the size of a continent whose every road to the sea belongs to someone else, and which has built its institutions around keeping a second road in reserve.* | Territory Definitions Finding 6; Transport supplement 5.5; Transit file ("the redundant road is what makes the agreement bearable") |
| B | *Basically a protein-rich frontier with no bread: a nation that can feed itself fish forever and must always ask for flour.* | Profile working sketch; Material Base Part 3(d) |
| C | *A union of peoples whose treaties are older than the republic that now holds them, and whose roads are not.* | Land-claim institutions as the part most likely to survive (note: NWT supplement Part 8); Woodard First Nation |

**Recommendation: A.** It states a structural position, not a personality, which honors the No National Stereotypes law. It carries the novel's geography (the Cassiar is the plot). It leaves room for C's Indigenous layer without committing the map. Candidate B is a good *second sentence*.

### (b) Founding and settlement history

**Layers (the real-world pattern the Profile calls "externally directed"):**

| Layer | What happened | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Indigenous peoples; the Dalton Trail from the coast to the interior follows a Chilkat Tlingit trade road | (note) Transport supplement 5.4 |
| 1 | Klondike rush 1896 to 1900; White Pass line built 1898 to 1900; 450 tons of explosives; the rush "was largely over" by the last spike | (note) Transport supplement Part 3 |
| 2 | Alaska Railroad 1903 to 1923; the construction camp became Anchorage | (note) Transport supplement Part 2 |
| 3 | 1942: Alaska Highway in eight months; Canol pipeline and road 1942 to 1944; Whitehorse was a principal staging point because the White Pass line could supply it | (note) Transport supplement Part 1, Part 3; NWT supplement |
| 4 | Whitehorse becomes Yukon capital April 1, 1953 | (search) |
| 5 | Modern treaties: Umbrella Final Agreement 1993; 11 of 14 Yukon First Nations have Final Agreements; Kwanlin Dün Final Agreement effective April 1, 2005; Nunavut created April 1, 1999 by a land claim | (search); (note) NWT supplement Part 8 |
| 6 | 2083 war, then re-founding | Canon date |

**Who is the "first effective settlement" population?** Woodard's doctrine asks who first re-established stable, self-perpetuating institutions, which is often not the largest group (note: Extraction, Part Two; Cross-Reference).

| Option | Founding population | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Indigenous governments** (Yukon First Nations under modern treaties; Dene, Inuvialuit and Inuit land-claim bodies) | Institutions rest on kinship and land tenure, not capital, so they survive a collapse (note: NWT supplement, "most likely to *survive*"); never fully displaced (Woodard point 8) | Only about 20% of the Republic's people (computed below); does not explain Whitehorse's civil-service culture |
| 2 | **The corridor-state workforce**: Whitehorse territorial machinery, highway and rail crews, placer families | Whitehorse is the hinge; matches Far West "externally directed" colonization | Weak on its own: those institutions depended on outside capital |
| 3 | **Composite**: treaty governments plus the corridor machinery, with the Klondike claim-staking and recorder tradition as the shared civic template | Fits the Far West type (nation defined by environment, not one settler stock); fits Woodard's rule that a founding template can be small | More explanation required |

**Recommendation: Option 3.** Reasoning (PROPOSED): after 2083 the first bodies able to make binding rules along the corridor were those whose authority did not depend on a supply chain: land-claim governments and a small, road-based territorial administration. The placer-claim tradition (stake, record, defend; 90% of Yukon placer operations are family-run, about 2.5 generations per site (note: Material Base, Yukon)) supplies a civic habit that both groups can share.

**The 2083 war: target logic (reasoning, not fact).**

| Place | Real installation | Likely 2083 outcome (PROPOSED) |
|---|---|---|
| Anchorage | Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson, the Alaskan Command headquarters (search) | Counterforce target; heavily destroyed |
| Fairbanks | Eielson, Fort Wainwright (note: Oil/Gas supplement); Clear Space Force Station early-warning radar (search) | Destroyed or contaminated |
| Delta Junction | Fort Greely, base for ground-based missile-defense interceptors (search) | ⚠️ A missile-defense site is a plausible target; **not in any project file** |
| Inuvik, Yellowknife | NORAD forward operating locations, with Iqaluit and Rankin Inlet (search) | ⚠️ Plausible targets; not in any project file. This bears on the eastern half |
| Whitehorse | No NORAD forward operating location (search). Canada has since announced a Northern Operational Support Hub at Whitehorse (search) | Spared or lightly damaged: the settled "capital because it survived" reasoning holds, but see flag below |
| Valdez, North Slope | Economic, not military, targets | Open; affects the oil trade |

**Flag:** the 2025 to 2026 hub announcement means Whitehorse may not be "no military" by 2083. A hub is airstrips, fuel and storage, not a base (search), so "spared" remains defensible. OPEN for the author: how heavy was the damage in the east and at Valdez?

**Population (computed, mixed vintages):**
- Republic today: 668,504 (Alaska minus Panhandle) + 48,493 (Yukon) + 45,808 (NWT) + 36,858 (Nunavut) = **799,663** (note: Oil/Gas supplement Part 4; NWT supplement Part 8).
- Anchorage 289,221 + Fairbanks borough 97,083 = 386,304, which is **48.3%** of that total. If both are lost, the survivors are about 413,000 before famine and fallout.
- The calorie gap (25% of need locally; grain about ten days (note)) means the Railbelt, not the delta villages, is what a collapse starves (note: Oil/Gas supplement, carrying capacity).
- PROPOSED: the post-war Republic is a small nation, counted in the tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands, with its center of gravity on the Yukon corridor. This makes the Republic more dependent on Cascadia, not less, and it supports "the junior partner knows it."

### (c) Government type and system

| # | Option | What it looks like | Enables | Forecloses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Confederal republic with a consensus-style council** | Union of Regions (Alaska, Yukon, the Mackenzie/Dene lands, Nunavut) under a charter; a council seated at Whitehorse; decisions by agreement; treaty governments hold constitutional standing. Precedent: the NWT and Nunavut legislatures run without parties (⚠️M) | Fits a small, spread population; makes Indigenous co-management real; explains why the Canol is never finished (consent takes decades); explains thin enforcement | A strong, decisive center; a dramatic president |
| 2 | **Presidential republic** (Alaska-constitution lineage) | Elected chief executive, strong governor-style powers, a single revenue story | Clear antagonists; clean law-and-order scenes | Contradicts the dispersed reality; makes the Indigenous east into subjects |
| 3 | **Charter-company republic** (the Far West pattern: corridor firms and a thin state) | Rail, fuel and port-access concessions run the real economy; the state licenses them | Fits the railyard and fuel facts; makes "who owns Lucy" a corporate question | Weakens the "republic" label; risks cynicism as the only register |

**Recommendation: Option 1, with Option 3's concession habit inside it** (the state grants operating charters for rail, fuel and freight). Reasoning: the Material Base shows a nation of regions joined by thin lines (Transit file Resolution C) and a Far West-type economy dependent on outside capital (Cross-Reference). The legislature must not be called the "Assembly" (that name belongs to the Assembly of Nations); use a placeholder such as "General Council." Avoid mirroring Cascadia's "People's Democratic" label.

**For the novel:** the state appears as a few concrete offices: a constabulary post, a highway authority camp, a registry clerk, a permit office. All PROPOSED; no named businesses.

### (d) The five cultural axes

All PROPOSED. Each is written as a result of institutions and landscape, with internal variation.

| Axis | Proposal | Connection |
|---|---|---|
| Authority and hierarchy | Distrust of *distant* authority, deference to *local competence*. The center is a service that arrives on a truck; the person who keeps the road open outranks the person who signs for it | Far West "resentment of the distant capital" (note: Extraction); now aimed at Cascadia's tariffs |
| Individual versus communal | Mutual aid by necessity, individual claim by law. The subsistence sharing networks and the claim-staking habit coexist | Subsistence data (note: Material Base); placer family operations (note) |
| Religious or ideological worldview | No state religion; plural by region (Orthodox, Anglican and Catholic mission traditions, Indigenous practice, a secular urban Whitehorse) (⚠️M all). Outsiders are treated as guests with obligations; dissent is tolerated because the population is small and everyone needs the same road | Fits a small-population society |
| Equality versus deference | Egalitarian in manner, stratified in access: whoever controls fuel, freight space and permits holds the power | Fuel and freight logistics (note: Transport supplement Part 9) |
| Economic base | Extraction, harvest and transfer; reinforcing culture: patience with seasons, suspicion of promises, pride in repairs | Profile "Characteristic economic base"; Transit file on subsidized resupply |

**Internal variation to write in:** Whitehorse (administrative, small, cold), the Yukon-Kuskokwim delta (fish and boats), the Railbelt survivors, the Mackenzie communities, Nunavut's 25 coastal communities (note). The "Alaskan" is not one person.

### (e) The Indigenous-majority east

**Numbers (search; computed):** Indigenous share is 22.3% in Yukon (8,810), 49.6% in the NWT (20,035), about 85.8% in Nunavut; Alaska Native and American Indian are 14.8% alone and 21.9% alone or in combination statewide (2020). Taking Alaska at about 100,000, the Republic is roughly 20% Indigenous overall. The east (NWT plus Nunavut) is roughly 63 to 66% Indigenous but holds only about 10% of the Republic's people (82,666 of 799,663, computed) and most of its land (2,964,706 km², computed from note figures).

| Option | Description | Material position | Novel |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | **Fold in** as Regions under a union (METHOD rule 4 already folds the territories in "on an Indigenous-majority reading") | Keeps Mary River iron, the Mackenzie, the Northwest Passage, Yellowknife hydro, tungsten | Almost no effect |
| E2 | **Own nation(s)** (Denendeh and an Inuit nation) per Woodard point 8 | Republic shrinks to Alaska plus Yukon; loses its iron, passage, Yellowknife; Whitehorse to Yellowknife becomes international at both ends; the Canol becomes a foreign road | Changes the Republic's fundamental position. **Contradicts the SETTLED territory definition**, so I do not recommend it |
| E3 | **Fold in, with treaty-partner standing**: land-claim governments hold constitutional status and a consent role over corridors | Same assets as E1, but the Republic *cannot* build the Canol or reopen the Nahanni Range Road without agreement (the Dehcho claim is unsettled; the 2020 bridge agreement with the Liard First Nation is a precedent (note: Interior Corridors, NWT supplement)) | The Yukon where the story starts is largely treaty land; Lucy's yard sits on the traditional territory of the Kwanlin Dün and Ta'an Kwäch'än (search) |

**Recommendation: E3.** It meets Woodard's point 8 (the population is a political actor, not texture) without breaking the settled map. It also provides a *reason* for Resolution B's chronic delay. Park E2 as a later-era possibility (secession or dissolution) the author may want for a Second Interwar plot. Record in the Scope Note that the Republic deliberately does not subdivide the east into separate map nations.

**Caution:** whether to use Indigenous names (Denendeh, Inuit Nunangat) for Regions is for the author, and ideally for consultation with those communities. I flag it rather than decide it.

### (f) Language, religion, money, law, and robots as property

**Language (PROPOSED):** English as language of record. Each Region's own official languages stay official inside it. Real precedents: the NWT recognizes eleven official languages; Nunavut recognizes Inuit language, English and French; Yukon is English and French with services in Indigenous languages (all search). Consequence: the border with Cascadia is a *legal* friction, not a linguistic one.

**Money (PROPOSED):** three options.

| Option | Effect |
|---|---|
| Dollarization (use Cascadia's currency) | Maximum dependence; no monetary policy |
| Own paper unit backed by placer gold and Keno Hill silver (note: Material Base; placer gold as a "currency industry") | Fits the resources; Cascadia's currency still circulates at the line |
| Fuel-credit scrip along the corridor | Local color; informal economy |

**Recommendation:** own unit, assay-backed, with Cascadian money accepted at the crossings. The informal fuel-credit layer is texture, not law.

**Law (PROPOSED):** three layers: Republic statute; Regional law; treaty-government law. Common-law inheritance from both Canadian and U.S. practice. Land-claim co-management boards decide land use (note: NWT supplement).

**Robots as property.** All of this is PROPOSED. The Republic has no manufactures (note), so every robot in it is an import or a Cascadian-built unit under lease or sale.

| Concept | Proposal | Analogy used |
|---|---|---|
| Title | A registry records maker, serial, owner of record | Vehicle title and registration (search: borders ask for registration and ownership proof) |
| Keeper | The *keeper* (custodian) may differ from the *owner*; a signed keeper's letter delegates custody | A permission letter for a borrowed vehicle: "strongly recommended" by both border services, not a legal requirement, checked on secondary inspection (search) |
| Work license | Each robot working for hire needs a regional work license held by the owner or operator | Heavy-equipment or firearm permits (⚠️M) |
| Tax | Owner pays an annual levy; operator pays a machine-hours charge; imports pay duty | A labor-poor state that wants labor |
| Liability | Owner strictly liable for harm; robot never a party | Standard property liability |
| Care standard | Law protects the robot only as *equipment*: service intervals and duty cycles | The irony for Lucy: a boss can overwork her up to the limit of "mechanical wear," because the only protection is for the asset |
| Travel | Robots cross as accompanied goods; the keeper carries title proof and a keeper's letter; temporary admission paperwork (a carnet-type document, ⚠️M) | Lucy leaving without the owner's letter is **removal of property**, not quitting |
| Recovery | Treaty comity: the owner may seek return of stolen property across the line | Creates a pursuit threat (PROPOSED, optional) |

**Ownership proposals for the cast (OPEN):**
- **Lucy:** owned by the yard's operating company (or leased from a Cascadian maker). Human #1 acquires custody by lease, sale or an irregular arrangement that is the quiet "clause" in Decision 5. Her overwork is then lawful and her departure is legally dangerous.
- **Jennifer:** owned by a Vancouver coastal weather-and-radio employer (her built purpose is meteorology and radio engineering, Decision 3). Human #2 holds her as *keeper* by an employer's letter. That letter expires or is exceeded when they go north: a quiet ticking clock.

**Recommendation:** adopt title + keeper + work license. It lets Humans #1 and #2 be *custodians without being owners*, which the brief needs, and it makes every border scene a paperwork scene.

### (g) Street-level texture, in the novel's era

Real geography only; invented elements flagged. All details ⚠️M unless marked.

- **Whitehorse:** on the Yukon River; the capital since 1953 (search); 28,201 people in 2021 (note). The old White Pass depot area is six blocks from the current nominal terminus; the downtown track has been lifted (note). In-world, a rebuilt yard needs land: PROPOSED to place it on the waterfront corridor under lease from a First Nation landlord, since the city sits on Kwanlin Dün and Ta'an Kwäch'än territory (search).
- **Power:** Whitehorse Rapids 41.3 MW, Aishihik 38.2 MW, Mayo 15.7 MW (search; these sum to 95.2 MW, computed, against 96.6 MW reported, so a small discrepancy to note). Yukon Energy had 132.4 MW total, 78% hydro generation in 2024 (search). This closes the open "Yukon electricity" item: the capital plausibly generates its own power. Hydro *sites* survive; dams need maintenance (note: Cascadia profile logic).
- **Fuel:** real Yukon fuel is trucked in from Alberta and BC (search; note). In the Republic, diesel can also reach Whitehorse by an *internal* road from the Valdez plant (Valdez, Glennallen, Tok, Whitehorse), if the oil trade runs (PROPOSED reasoning from the Highway file's internal Tok leg).
- **Winter:** deep cold, short daylight, chip-seal road reverting to gravel; the Burwash to Koidern stretch fails first (note: Transport supplement Part 1).
- **The White Pass line:** 3 ft gauge, 107 miles (172 km), Skagway to Whitehorse; 450 tons of explosives at the summit; closed 1982; revived 1988; owner Carnival since 2018; steam locomotive No. 73 still runs (note; search). In-world, a narrow-gauge fleet means rolling stock is special-built or hand-repaired: exactly where an expert mechanic is irreplaceable.
- **Skagway:** a Cascadian port town with cruise docks (note). The border post at Fraser (opened 1979, daily 8 a.m. to midnight (search)) lies in BC, so after partition it is Cascadia's; the Republic would need its own post near Carcross (PROPOSED).
- **Alaska Highway:** about 630 of 1,387 miles in BC (note); mileposts as addresses (note); Contact Creek, Mile 588, is the BC to Yukon line (note).
- **The Cassiar:** Highway 37 is "a late, thin road through empty country" (note), about 724 km from the junction to Kitwanga, all Cascadian. There is no existing staffed Yukon to BC post on Highway 37 or the Alaska Highway; both are new in this world (⚠️ exact boundary location on Highway 37 not verified). PROPOSED: in S2, a small hut-and-flag post.
- **Texture to invent safely:** a registry counter, a fuel-ration board, a keeper's-letter stamp, a ferry gate. No named businesses.

## 4. Cross-country consistency flags
