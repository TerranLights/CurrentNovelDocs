**✅ FULLY SETTLED, 2026-09-26 — the whole Indonesia partition question is resolved.** The eight-full-nations
premise this doc and the rendered `maritime_sea_map.png` were originally built under is retired. The final
shape, all author-confirmed across several research passes this session:

- **A six-bloc unified core**: **Sumatra, Java, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Nusa Tenggara & Bali, and Maluku** all
  stay one successor state — none has any separatist, ethnic, religious, linguistic, economic, industrial, or

- **The national capital, as of the 2083 war, is Nusantara** — certain given the real-world timeline (even a

  Java's own government apparatus is, by then, physically fused with Kalimantan's, not just administratively

- **West Papua secedes and starts fully independent at 2083** — the strongest popular-support evidence found

- **Aceh becomes an Autonomous Zone, not a fully independent nation**, forming gradually after 2083 as a
  secondary consequence of Indonesia's post-war weakening — grounded in Aceh's own real, already-functioning

faster than the sea is rising (material survey §9) — its north coast subsides up to 1.5m/decade, ~9x faster

**The rendered `maritime_sea_map.png` (still eight separate hard-bordered blocs) is now confirmed out of

**What's still genuinely open for every one of these**: none has an invented name, a government type, or a

## Indonesia's Eight Blocs

### Sumatra — ✅ confirmed part of the unified core
**9 provinces** (every Sumatran province except Aceh): North Sumatra, West Sumatra, Riau, Jambi, South
Sumatra, Bengkulu, Lampung, Bangka Belitung Islands, Riau Islands. Largest of the eight blocs by province
count. Ethnically the most Malay-coded of the bunch (Minangkabau in the west, Malay along the Riau coast,
Batak in the north-central highlands), though — like every bloc except West Papua — broadly Austronesian, not
a tier-1-distinct population the way Papua is. Historically the seat of **Srivijaya**, the 7th–13th century
maritime empire that controlled the Strait of Malacca; that's why Sumatra anchors the Maritime Mandala
sphere's core. Its southern coast holds the Bangka-Belitung tin belt (the Southeast Asian tin belt's
southernmost extension); its northern coast, the Strait itself. **No sectarian or separatist fault line of its
own** — the interesting political material inside old Sumatra is specifically Aceh, which is why Aceh was
carved out rather than folded in. **Added 2026-09-26**: a real, separate coastal-erosion exposure — 24 islands
already lost off the Aceh, North Sumatra and Riau coasts (see the material survey's new §9) — smaller in scale
than Java's subsidence crisis but a genuine, ongoing material fact about this bloc's outer islands.

### Aceh — ✅ SETTLED (2026-09-26): Autonomous Zone, not full independence
**1 province.** The one bloc whose separateness rests on **politics and religion, not ethnicity** — Acehnese
are ~70% of the province's population and are Austronesian like every neighboring group; this is not a
West-Papua-style tier-1 case. What makes it distinct: the precolonial **Aceh Sultanate**, a real independent
state before Dutch conquest; a promise of special status from Sukarno "broken almost immediately"; the **Free
Aceh Movement (GAM)**, which fought for outright independence from 1976 to 2005; and Indonesia's only
region with a **formal state role for Islamic law**, granted first in 1959 and cemented by the 2005 Helsinki
peace accord. Also a real node of the Maritime Mandala (Samudra Pasai, the sultanate's predecessor state, was
a major Strait of Malacca trade hub in its own right).

**✅ SETTLED, 2026-09-26: Aceh becomes an Autonomous Zone, not a fully independent nation, forming gradually
after the 2083 war** as a secondary consequence of Indonesia's post-war weakening (the capital's move to
Nusantara, the loss of West Papua and possibly Maluku) rather than a clean, immediate break — physically still
attached to the Sumatra landmass, unlike West Papua's or Maluku's island geography. Author's own reasoning,
and the real-world evidence backs it as genuinely more durable than the two obvious cautionary analogies:

- **Aceh already has almost exactly this status today, and it's the most stable of the relevant real-world
  cases.** The 2005 Helsinki Agreement gave Aceh real, substantial autonomy — its own regional laws (Qanun,
  based on Islamic principles), control over its own natural resources, and the right to form local political
  parties, genuinely unusual for an Indonesian province. **Twenty years on, it has held.** Honest caveat: "not
  all of [the Agreement's] articles have been implemented" after 14+ years — real gaps, but no collapse or
  rollback.
- **Crimea (Autonomous Republic within Ukraine, 1991–2014) shows one failure mode, and it doesn't apply
  here.** Crimea had real powers (own constitution, legislature, budget) but was never fully stable — Kyiv
  directly intervened and scrapped its constitution in 1995 — and it ultimately ended through **external
  annexation**: Russia exploited a moment of Ukrainian political crisis in 2014, pulled by Crimea's
  ethnic-Russian majority. **Aceh has no equivalent external "Russia"** — no rival foreign power with an
  ethnic-kinship claim on it.
- **Hong Kong ("one country, two systems," 1997–2047 promised) shows the other failure mode, and it also
  doesn't apply here.** Substantially eroded via the 2020 National Security Law, 23 years into a 50-year
  promise — prosecutions still ongoing as of 2026. The failure mode is a **central state growing stronger and
  more confident** over time and choosing to reassert control. **The author's own premise is a weakening
  post-war Indonesia, the opposite condition** — less capacity for a Hong Kong-style reassertion, not more.
- **Structurally distinct from this project's other Autonomous Zone**: the Araucanía–Patagonia Autonomous Zone
  (Latin America) is jointly guaranteed by two neighboring sovereign states (Argentina and Chile) and takes
  1–2 generations to fully form. Aceh's arrangement has no second co-guaranteeing country — it's a single
  central state (the five-bloc Indonesian core) granting autonomy within its own borders, closer to the real
  Crimea/Hong Kong/actual-Aceh model than to the Patagonia Zone's condominium structure. Worth keeping the two
  distinct rather than treating "Autonomous Zone" as one identical designation everywhere it appears.

### Java — ✅ confirmed part of the unified core
**6 provinces**: the Jakarta capital region, West Java, Central Java, Yogyakarta, East Java, Banten. **The
single most important fact about this bloc**: roughly 56% of all of Indonesia's population lives here, on
about 7% of the country's land — an internal population concentration with no real equivalent anywhere else
on either Southeast Asia map. Historically the seat of **Majapahit**, the 13th–15th century empire that is
the other half of the Maritime Mandala's core (alongside Sumatra's Srivijaya) and whose cultural memory Bali
still carries directly. Held the national capital (Jakarta) historically, though **Nusantara (East Kalimantan)
holds that role as of this map's post-2083 setting** — Java keeps the overwhelming majority of Indonesia's
people, industry, and food base regardless. **No separatist history of its
own** — if anything, Java is the *core* every other bloc's separateness is implicitly defined against. **Added
2026-09-26**: that core is physically sinking — Java's north coast subsides up to 1.5m/decade, ~9x faster
than sea-level rise, mostly from groundwater extraction. **See "Java's realistic post-collapse trajectory,"
right after the Kalimantan/Nusantara section below**, for the full projection and material survey §9 for the
sourced research.

### Kalimantan — ✅ confirmed part of the unified core (and holds the capital, Nusantara)
**5 provinces**: West, Central, South, East and North Kalimantan — the Indonesian two-thirds of Borneo
(Malaysia's Sabah/Sarawak and Brunei hold the rest of the island, drawn as their own separate countries on
this map). Site of **Nusantara** (see its own section below). Shares a land border with Malaysia that is
**not fully demarcated in the real world**, a genuine, live boundary ambiguity worth knowing about if this
border ever needs to matter in a story. **No sectarian or separatist history of its own** in the source
material — its interest is more resource/infrastructure (Nusantara) than political fault line.

#### Nusantara, in detail (added 2026-09-26, author-requested)

**The real facts.** Nusantara sits at 0°58′23″S 116°42′31″E, about 30km northwest of Balikpapan, spanning
land Indonesia carved specifically for it (256,142 hectares of land, plus 68,188 hectares of water — 2,562
km² total) across two regencies in East Kalimantan. Construction started in 2022; as of this research, the
core government district is **nearly complete** — the Garuda Palace (the presidential palace) is finished,
legislative buildings are underway, and roughly 4,000 civil servants are scheduled to relocate this year.
Designed capacity is up to **1.5 million residents**; actual 2025 population is only **147,430**. **Funding
has collapsed** — from $2 billion allocated in 2024 to $300 million for 2026, an 85% cut — and the government
has already **downgraded its own ambitions**: Nusantara is now slated to become Indonesia's "political
capital" only, by 2028, with **Jakarta explicitly retaining economic dominance**. That's a real, current,
unresolved split-capital arrangement (the same shape as Naypyidaw/Yangon or Putrajaya/Kuala Lumpur), not a
clean full relocation — worth knowing before assuming Nusantara simply *replaces* Jakarta in any finished
sense. **Kalimantan's own flood/subsidence exposure was not established in this research pass** — IKN sits
inland and on firmer ground than Jakarta's river delta, which is suggestive, but that's an inference, not a
sourced fact; treat it as an open item, not a clean bill of health.

**What this might mean in-world — reasoned speculation, not decided.** Three shapes this could plausibly take
by the time this setting's collapse happens, offered as options rather than a pick:
1. **Nusantara finishes the job the real-world funding crisis is currently failing to finish.** In a setting
   where Jakarta's subsidence crisis gets dramatically worse (see the Java projection below) faster than the
   real world's own timeline, the pressure to actually complete the move — rather than leave it half-funded
   and politically ambiguous, as it is right now — would be much stronger. A government that had already
   started building its institutions somewhere other than a drowning capital has an obvious, urgent reason to
   finish the job for real.
2. **It stays exactly what it currently is: an underfunded, half-built "political capital" that never
   actually absorbs the government's real weight**, with Jakarta's population and economy staying put — right
   up until the collapse makes the whole question moot at once, rather than resolving it first.
3. **Something in between**: a partial, improvised relocation driven by the collapse itself rather than any
   planned schedule — ministries and people fleeing north as North Java becomes uninhabitable, landing in a
   Nusantara that was never actually finished for that scale, straining it in ways the original 1.5M-capacity
   plan never accounted for.

#### Java's realistic post-collapse trajectory (added 2026-09-26, author-requested)

**Grounded in real, current subsidence data (material survey §9), extended with reasoned speculation about
what a societal collapse specifically would do to a crisis that's already unfolding in the real world.
Flagged clearly: everything past the first paragraph below is a projection for the author to react to, not a
researched fact.**

**The real, current baseline**: North Jakarta has already lost up to 4 meters of elevation over four decades
and is roughly 40% below sea level today; Pekalongan is separately flagged as likely to be the first place in
Indonesia to sink rapidly, within the next decade, on the real world's own present timeline. This is already
happening, on its own schedule, independent of anything this project invents.

**Two forces pull in opposite directions once industrial society breaks down, and they don't cancel out
evenly.** (1) Groundwater extraction — the dominant cause of subsidence — plausibly *slows* if the huge
coastal population and industry driving that extraction can no longer sustain it, which would slow the rate
of *new* subsidence in less-developed areas. (2) But the engineered defenses currently holding back the water
in the worst-hit areas — sea walls, pumping stations, the (real, expensive, partly foreign-funded) Jakarta
coastal defense projects — require continuous maintenance and power to function at all. **Those fail fast
without upkeep**, and unlike new subsidence, that failure is not gradual — once a pump station goes dark, the
water it was holding back returns on a timescale of days or weeks, not decades. **Net effect, as a working
hypothesis**: the areas already critically compromised today (North Jakarta, Pekalongan, Semarang, Demak)
become permanently lost fast and early in any collapse scenario, while the *rate* of damage spreading to new
areas might actually be slower than the real world's current trajectory, for lack of the extraction driving
it.

**The population and political consequences this implies, offered as a plausible chain rather than a
decision**: millions of displaced North Java coastal residents need somewhere to go — inland/upland Java
(the Bandung/Yogyakarta highlands) is the geographically obvious absorption zone, but so is emigration off
Java entirely, toward Sumatra or Kalimantan, which is exactly the direction the real Nusantara project was
already trying to pull people before any collapse. **The more interesting implication is institutional, not
demographic**: whichever government apparatus had already begun relocating to Nusantara before the collapse
hit would have a structural survival advantage that a Jakarta-anchored government would not — an inland
capital, on presumably firmer ground, isn't simultaneously losing its ministries, its records, and its civil
service to the same flood that's displacing its people. **That's a genuinely well-grounded, non-arbitrary
reason a unified successor state's center of gravity could end up Kalimantan-anchored rather than
Java-anchored** — independent of, and additive to, the separatism cases already on the table for Aceh, West
Papua and Maluku. Worth treating as a real candidate answer to "why would Java's 56%-of-population dominance
not simply carry over into whatever comes after," not just a hypothetical.

### Sulawesi — ✅ confirmed part of the unified core
**6 provinces**: North, Central, South, Southeast and West Sulawesi, plus Gorontalo. Home of the **Bugis and
Makassarese** peoples — historically among the region's most accomplished maritime traders, and, per the
material survey, not merely a historical fact: the **Bugis pinisi inter-island sailing/freight tradition is a
still-operating commercial fleet today**, not a heritage relic. That's a genuinely useful, concrete asset for
a post-collapse setting — a working pre-industrial cargo-shipping culture already exists here, unlike almost
anywhere else in either Southeast Asia survey. Part of the Maritime Mandala's domain (a continuation of the
same trade-network logic into the early modern period). **No separatist history of its own** in the source
material.

### Maluku — ✅ SETTLED (2026-09-26): confirmed part of the unified core
**2 provinces**: Maluku and North Maluku — the historical **Spice Islands**. Two real fault lines here, both
distinct from anywhere else in the set: (1) a **religious** one — a Christian-Ambonese population descended
from Dutch-era conversion sits alongside the Muslim sultanates' own legacy (Ternate and Tidore, the two
rival clove/nutmeg powers that helped carry Islam eastward in the first place); (2) an actual **failed
secession** — Chris Soumokil declared an independent **Republic of the South Moluccas (RMS)** in April 1950;
the rebellion failed and Soumokil was executed in 1966. A second, bloodier round of sectarian violence hit the
same fault line in 1999–2002 (~5,000 dead). Falls partly within the Linguistic Wallacea sphere.

**✅ SETTLED, 2026-09-26: Maluku stays part of the unified core, joining it as a sixth bloc.** Deeper research
(author-requested) into Maluku's culture, economy and current politics turned up a meaningfully weaker case
for secession than the "real but historically failed" framing above suggested on its own:

- **Maluku's own deepest cultural institution is built around reconciliation, not division.** **Pela gandong**
  — a real, still-living system of alliance oaths binding specific Christian and Muslim villages (sometimes
  whole clans) into mutual-defense/mutual-aid pacts, some grounded in claimed shared ancestry ("gandong"
  means "womb," i.e. villages that are siblings from one origin) — is explicitly credited with helping Maluku
  recover from the 1999–2002 conflict. Scholars attach three core values to it: **social harmony, conflict
  mediation, and unity in diversity.** Maluku's own indigenous social technology is built specifically to
  *hold together* a mixed Christian-Muslim population, unlike anything that preceded Aceh's or West Papua's
  movements.
- **RMS today reads as a diaspora-symbolic movement, not an active insurgency.** Its government-in-exile,
  based in the Netherlands since 1966, is still active (as recently as September 2025, organizing protests
  against Indonesian presidential visits there) — but sustained by descendants of those evacuated after the
  1950 defeat, not an on-the-ground movement inside Maluku itself. No OPM-style armed insurgency, and no
  West-Papua-2017-petition-scale mobilization or polling data was found — the absence itself is informative.
- **Currently the fastest-growing region in Indonesia, not a neglected periphery.** North Maluku posted
  **34.17% economic growth in 2025 — the highest of any Indonesian province** — and 19.64% in Q1 2026, driven
  by a nickel-downstream processing boom, plus gold/copper mining, fisheries (tuna, shellfish, lobster,
  shrimp, squid), and nutmeg (still a premium export commodity). Explicitly positioned as "the main engine of
  economic growth in East Indonesia." Unlike Aceh's grievance case or West Papua's "the core won't let a
  resource-rich periphery go" tension, Maluku currently has a strong, *current*, material reason to want to
  stay integrated, not just a reason for Jakarta to want to keep it.
- **Infrastructure is genuinely peripheral but not isolation-driving**: real connectivity gaps exist (Ambon
  to Ternate by ferry takes 18 hours; 64 ports, under-optimized), but an active government subsidized-shipping
  program (Perintis) is addressing it, and it doesn't rise to the kind of total isolation that would make an
  independent case on its own.

**Net effect**: Maluku joins the unified core as its sixth bloc. Of the eight original blocs, only **West
Papua** (fully independent) and **Aceh** (Autonomous Zone) end up separate from the core successor state.

### Nusa Tenggara & Bali — ✅ confirmed part of the unified core
**3 provinces**: Bali, West Nusa Tenggara, East Nusa Tenggara. The one genuinely mixed bloc, both
religiously and (per the map's sphere layer) linguistically: **Bali is ~86.5% Hindu**, the region's sole
Hindu-majority province and, per the Maritime Mandala sphere, the **living cultural heir of Majapahit** —
Hindu-Javanese elites and their religion fled here as Majapahit fell to Islamic Demak in the 16th century,
which is why Bali's Hinduism survived when Java's own largely didn't. East Nusa Tenggara (Flores, Sumba,
Alor, and Indonesian/West Timor) is the geographic core of the **Linguistic Wallacea** sphere — the real,
peer-reviewed transition zone where Austronesian and Papuan/Melanesian language families sit in long-contact.
Sumbawa, in West Nusa Tenggara, is also inside that same sphere even though its own province-mate Lombok is
explicitly excluded — the two islands share one province, so the map draws a schematic patch to include one
and not the other rather than forcing an all-or-nothing province-level choice. **No separatist history of its
own** beyond that religious/linguistic diversity.

### West Papua — ✅ separation from Indonesia SETTLED (2026-09-26); merger with PNG left open
**6 provinces** (Papua, West Papua/Papua Barat, Central Papua, Highland Papua, South Papua, Southwest Papua)
— the entire Melanesian-populated Papua-island territory, not just the province formally named "Papua Barat."
**The one bloc that is genuinely different in kind from the other seven**: Papuans are **Melanesian, not
Austronesian** — the single tier-1 exception in this whole survey, closer in kind to Tibet's relationship to
the Sinian Federation on the East Asia map than to any of this bloc's seven siblings. Indonesia's hold dates
to a contested 1969 vote; a decades-long transmigration program has moved large numbers of non-Papuan settlers
in, to the point that the territory is now roughly **50/50 indigenous Melanesian vs. settler-descended**
population-wide (highlands remain overwhelmingly Papuan; coastal cities are closer to parity). The **OPM**
insurgency is decades old and ongoing. And the material stake is enormous: **Grasberg**, one of the world's
largest gold/copper mines, sits in Central Papua and was projected to produce **$1.94 billion in revenue in
2022 alone** — the same kind of "the core will not let this go easily" material tension the Guri Dam creates
for Venezuela and Amazonia in the Latin America work. The Bird's Head Peninsula and Cenderawasih Bay, in the
territory's northwest, also fall inside the Linguistic Wallacea sphere — genuinely the honest edge of the
Austronesian/Melanesian contact zone, not a political statement.

**✅ SETTLED, 2026-09-26: West Papua separates from Indonesia after the 2083 war.** Author's own call, backed
by the strongest popular-support evidence found anywhere in this research:

- **The 2017 West Papuan People's Petition** — 1.8 million signatures demanding a UN-supervised independence
  referendum, collected clandestinely after the Indonesian government banned it and threatened signers with
  arrest. 95.77% of signers were indigenous Papuans, and the total represents **over 70% of the entire
  indigenous Papuan population** — a costly, risk-bearing signal, not a cheap one.
- **The founding grievance still drives sentiment today**: the 1969 "Act of Free Choice," the vote that
  formally incorporated the territory into Indonesia, used roughly 1,000 hand-picked representatives under
  military pressure — essentially universally regarded outside Indonesian state framing as illegitimate.
- **Caveat that matters**: this evidence is specifically about *indigenous* Papuans. Roughly half the
  territory's population today is Indonesian-settler-descended (per the 50/50 figure above), a group with no
  evidenced independence sentiment and every structural reason to prefer the status quo. Separatism reads as
  very popular — plausibly majority-to-supermajority — among indigenous Papuans specifically, not as a
  uniform view across the whole territory's population.

**⚠️ OPEN, left for later: does West Papua ever merge with Papua New Guinea?** Real-world evidence doesn't
support the two starting out unified — the actual independence movement (OPM, then the ULMWP since 2014) has
consistently sought a fully independent "Republic of West Papua," not incorporation into PNG, across six
decades of organizing. PNG's own government officially recognizes Indonesian sovereignty over the territory
and maintains a 1986 friendship treaty with Indonesia specifically to avoid this exact scenario — annexing a
neighbor's territory would mean picking a fight with a far larger power, which PNG has consistently avoided.
Indonesia has also worked to blunt West Papuan influence even within the Melanesian Spearhead Group (the real
regional body — Fiji, PNG, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia's Kanaks), securing its own associate
membership there since 2015 specifically to counter the ULMWP's observer-status advocacy. **What is real**:
deep Melanesian ethnic kinship and genuinely strong PNG grassroots sympathy for West Papuan independence
(reportedly stronger than in Vanuatu, the most vocal state-level supporter in the Pacific) — a real basis for
closeness, just not evidence of an existing union or unification plan.

**Author's proposed shape, consistent with an existing pattern in this project's own universe**: West Papua
and Papua New Guinea start as **two separate countries at the 2083 war**, then over the ~480 years to the
Falkland Treaty (June 21, 2564 — already-established canon), deepen economic and trade ties, potentially
culminating in a full merger by treaty time. **This exact structure — start separate, leave a later merger
explicitly open — is already how this project handled Guyana/Suriname/French Guiana in the Latin America
work**, where "a later evolution into a single unified country is left open as a possible development in the
*later* First Interwar Period." Real-world precedent for the mechanism itself (gradual economic integration
preceding political union) exists and is documented — **Yemen (1990)** is the closest real match: North and
South Yemen saw open movement, surging trade, and harmonized institutions *before* their 1990 merger, itself
the product of a unity framework drafted back in 1981. But the honest caveats are real too:
- **Merger failure is arguably the more common historical outcome, not the exception.** The **Mali Federation**
  (Senegal + French Sudan, 1959) collapsed after about two months over leadership rivalry; the **United Arab
  Republic** (Egypt + Syria, 1958–61) dissolved after 3 years when Syria came to resent Egyptian dominance; the
  **Senegambia Confederation** (1982–89) dissolved after 7 years for the same reason — a structurally larger,
  more established partner dominating a smaller one, exactly the dynamic that would be a live risk for
  PNG/West Papua depending on their relative post-collapse size and power.
- **Even Yemen's own "completed" merger wasn't stable** — economic crisis in 1991 and a 1994 civil war
  temporarily dissolved the union before it was reasserted by force.
- **The most durable real-world counter-example is Korea**: shared ethnicity, language, and peninsula, and
  still 75+ years of unbroken separation — partition can become self-reinforcing precisely because each side
  develops its own institutions and vested interests over time, not despite shared kinship.
- **The historically most common realistic outcome for two related, economically-integrating states may be
  neither full merger nor full separation**: deep, lasting economic integration that never quite completes
  into one sovereign state — the EU/Benelux pattern.
- **None of this precedent covers a 500-year timescale.** Every real case above resolved, one way or another,
  within 10–20 years — an order of magnitude faster than the author's proposed arc. Real-world history can
  confirm the *mechanism* is real and can be either outcome; it cannot confirm which outcome a full half-
  millennium produces. Past this point, it's a creative call, not something research settles.

## The Five Sovereign Neighbors

Unlike Indonesia, none of these needed a partition decision — each is kept as its own real, present-day
country, same as every country on the mainland map. Short notes only, since they're already well-understood
real nations rather than invented designations:

- **Philippines** — ✅ **SETTLED (2026-09-26): stays whole, definitely, no further research needed.** 18
  regions drawn (region-level, not the finer ~118-province level). Its one internal fault line worth knowing:
  the **Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM)**, a real, current, first-order-subdivision-
  respecting Muslim-majority autonomous region — drawn as an ordinary Philippines region on this map (not
  promoted to its own block or given a sphere), since the state has already drawn those lines itself.
- **Malaysia** — ✅ **SETTLED (2026-09-26): stays intact as a sovereign country, with Sarawak specifically
  elevated to Autonomous Zone status within it** (the same "Autonomous Zone" designation as Aceh, formed
  gradually rather than a clean break) — **Sabah stays an ordinary state**, unpromoted. Research pass (same
  rigor as Aceh/Maluku): both Sabah and Sarawak have a real, current, quantified grievance (only a 5% oil
  royalty despite holding the bulk of the country's gas reserves — Sarawak's own separate win was a 5% state
  sales tax on petroleum products, not a higher royalty), but **the secession-minded position is an explicit
  minority within the movement, and the federal government has been defusing pressure through concession, not
  losing control** — "anti-federal sentiment in both states is strong but is kept at bay because Putrajaya has
  given in to most of the demands from Sarawak thus far." No party campaigned on secession in the 2025 Sabah
  state election. **Sarawak specifically has the more organized, documented movement** (Parti Bumi Kenyalang
  campaigns explicitly for independence via referendum; the Sarawak For Sarawakians petition collected 270,000
  signatures, over 10% of the state's population, demanding a referendum-mandating law) — the key structural
  difference from Aceh is that **no war forced this issue**; it's playing out through elections and petitions,
  not a 30-year insurgency. That weaker, still-improving trajectory is why Malaysia stays intact rather than
  losing East Malaysia outright, while Sarawak's stronger, more organized case earns it the Autonomous Zone
  tier Sabah doesn't get.
  - **Malaysia's religious composition** (author-requested, since it wasn't previously covered): ~60.4–63.5%
    Muslim nationally, but **Kelantan (95.5%), Terengganu (97.3%), Perlis (87.8%) and Kedah (78.5%)** — the
    four northern states — run dramatically higher, Malaysia's own Malay-Muslim heartland.
  - **The Patani Malay World — a new sphere connecting this map to the Mainland Southeast Asia map for the
    first time** (added 2026-09-26; see `spheres.csv` S4, and the mainland package's own S5): Kelantan and
    Terengganu specifically (not Kedah/Perlis — a separate, different historical connection, see below) share
    a real, named linguistic classification, "Kelantan-Pattani Malay," with Thailand's Deep South
    (Pattani/Yala/Narathiwat) — the same dialect, tracing to the precolonial Sultanate of Patani, split by the
    colonial-era border. **The author's initial proposal — that the Deep South simply joins Malaysia — was
    checked and isn't well-supported**: the actual insurgency (BRN) wants independence, not annexation, and
    Malaysia's government explicitly rules out any territorial claim. Settled the same way as West Papua/PNG:
    the Deep South separates from Thailand as its own **independent nation** at 2083 (✅ settled 2026-09-26 —
    unlike Aceh, Thailand has never granted an ethnic minority region real autonomy anywhere in its modern
    history, and the Deep South's security sits under direct military, not civilian, control — the opposite
    finding from Aceh's case), with a later Malaysia union left open, not asserted. Full detail in the
    mainland package's own README and frameworks survey §3.2 addendum.
  - **Kedah and Perlis are a separate, distinct connection** — also heavily Muslim, also ceded from Siam to
    Britain in the same 1909 treaty as Kelantan/Terengganu, but historically a different lineage (the Kedah
    Sultanate, not Patani's) — not folded into the Patani Malay sphere above.
- **Brunei** — 4 districts. A shrunk survivor of the real precolonial Sultanate of Brunei, the one country in
  this set whose modern border isn't simply an inherited colonial administrative line.
- **Singapore** — no internal subdivision; the whole country is one unit, the only polity on either Southeast
  Asia map with that status.
- **Timor-Leste** — 14 municipalities (corrected from 13; Atauro split from Dili in 2022). Already fully and
  cleanly sovereign since 2002. One geographic curiosity: **Oecusse-Ambeno** is a real exclave, sitting inside
  Indonesian West Timor territory.

## The Four Spheres

These aren't polities at all — they're an overlay layer marking cultural/trade/linguistic reach that cuts
across the hard borders above, the same mandala mechanism the mainland map uses for Isan, Zomia and the Khmer
Krom:

- **The Maritime Mandala (Srivijaya/Majapahit)** — precolonial trade-network reach, not a claim of territory.
  Core: Sumatra and Java (the two empires' actual seats). Domain: Malaysia, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Brunei,
  Aceh and Bali (all real trade-network/cultural-continuity nodes, not political claims).
  **This sphere's exact shape is this project's own reasoned construction** — neither survey proposed specific
  geometry for it, so treat its edges as the softest, least-sourced content on the whole map.
- **The Overseas Chinese Commercial Diaspora** — Singapore (~75.5% Chinese, the region's one Chinese-majority
  polity) as core; Malaysia (~23%) as domain; Indonesia and the Philippines noted as present but too diffuse
  to map precisely (~3% and ~1–2% nationally).
- **Linguistic Wallacea** — the one sphere with real, specific, peer-reviewed geography (Schapper 2015, 2017):
  a genuine transition zone where Austronesian and Papuan language families have long sat in contact.
  Deliberately **not** the same footprint as the 1859 biogeographic Wallace Line — it excludes Lombok and
  Sulawesi, and reaches further into West Papua than the biological line does.
- **S4, The Patani Malay World, added 2026-09-26** — the first sphere in this project split across two
  separate map files rather than contained on one. Core and domain on this map: Malaysia's Kelantan and
  Terengganu (Besut/Setiu districts specifically per the sourcing, though the whole-province rule draws the
  full provinces regardless). This map labels the Mainland side (Thailand's former Deep South — Pattani,
  Yala, Narathiwat, independent as of 2026-09-26) as continuing onto the companion map rather than drawing
  it; the Mainland map does the reverse. Grounded in a real, named linguistic classification (Glottolog's
  "Kelantan-Pattani Malay") spoken continuously across both sides of the border — see `spheres.csv` S4/S5
  on each map for the full sourcing.

## What This Doc Deliberately Doesn't Do

It doesn't invent names, governments, or founding stories for the core, West Papua, Aceh's Autonomous Zone,
Sarawak's Autonomous Zone, or Thailand's Deep South — that's a real, separate creative pass (the same kind of
work that gave North America's nations their capitals one at a time this session), not something to smuggle
in under "explaining the designations." The partition question itself (which pieces separate, in what form)
is now fully settled for this map's own territory — see the top of this document — but naming and internal
governmental character for any of the resulting polities remain open, and the Deep South's
independence-vs-autonomy question is still genuinely open too.
