# The Interior Corridors — Canol and Nahanni Range

2026-09-22. Research into whether the Alaska Republic can link its western and eastern halves **without
entering Cascadia**, by cutting directly east from the Yukon into the Northwest Territories.

**Short answer: yes, and two such corridors already partly exist.** Neither is complete, but both are real
roads with real history, and one of them lines up almost exactly with the Mackenzie Valley Highway.

---

## Why This Matters

Per `Alaska Republic - Highway Network (read from map reference).md`, the only all-season road from Whitehorse
to Yellowknife dips south into **British Columbia** — Cascadian territory — for roughly 650 km of a 1,919 km
journey. The Republic's two halves are joined by a neighbor's highway.

The question was whether a direct east–west crossing exists in the middle, bypassing BC entirely. It does, in
two places.

---

# Corridor 1 — The Nahanni Range Road

**This corridor already crosses the border. It is the finding that matters most.**

## What exists

| Segment | Road | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Watson Lake → **Tuchitua** → Miner Junction → Ross River → Faro → Carmacks | **Robert Campbell Highway (Yukon Hwy 4)**, 583 km | Mostly gravel; year-round to Ross River; sections "narrow, and sometimes hazardous" |
| **Miner Junction → Tungsten, NWT** | **Nahanni Range Road (Yukon Hwy 10)** | All gravel. **Crosses into the Northwest Territories.** |

**The Nahanni Range Road physically enters the NWT.** It runs from the Robert Campbell Highway east across the
territorial boundary to **Tungsten** and the **Cantung Mine**, in the Flat River Valley of the Selwyn Range.

**This is a genuine Yukon → NWT road link that does not touch British Columbia, and it is there today.**

## Its condition, which is the problem

- **"Not recommended for general public use."** Maintenance has been sporadic.
- **Abandoned after mining ceased in 1986**; partial restoration after the mine's intermittent return in 2003.
- The **Cantung Mine closed in October 2015** when its owner went bankrupt; the federal and NWT governments
  were still trying to sell it as of 2019. So the road's only reason to exist has been gone for a decade.
- A **$17 million bridge-replacement agreement** was announced in 2020 between the Yukon Government and the
  Liard First Nation — so the route is not entirely abandoned politically.

## Where it stops

**Tungsten is a dead end.** The road reaches the Cantung mine site and goes no further. Between there and
Nahanni Butte lies **Nahanni National Park Reserve** — 30,050 km², **no roads anywhere within it**, UNESCO
World Heritage Site since 1978, and some of the most severe terrain on the continent: limestone canyon walls
up to **1,000 m**, and **Mount Nirvana at 2,773 m**, the highest point in the Northwest Territories.

# Corridor 2 — The Canol Road, and why it is the better answer

**This is the one that lines up with everything else.**

## The history

The **Canol Road** was built **1942–44** as part of the wartime Canol Project, to service a pipeline carrying
oil from **Norman Wells, NWT to Whitehorse, Yukon.** The pipeline is long gone. **The road survives.**

That history matters enormously for the fiction: **this exact crossing has already been built once, by a
determined state, in about two years, under wartime urgency.** It is not a theoretical route.

## What exists

| Segment | Road | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Johnson's Crossing** (Alaska Hwy, ~78 mi east of Whitehorse) → NWT border | **South Canol Road (Yukon Hwy 6)**, 449 km | **"Little changed from 1945"** — narrow bridges, winding, unimproved, unsuitable for RVs. **Maintained seasonally, summer only.** Designated Hwy 6 in 1978. |
| Camp Canol (NWT) → Yukon border | **North Canol**, 380 km / 236 mi | **Unmaintained and deteriorating. All bridges are gone.** Vehicles can reach an abandoned airstrip around Milepost 222 from the Yukon side, terrain permitting. |
| Yukon border → Norman Wells | **Canol Heritage Trail**, ~355 km / 220 mi | Converted to a hiking and mountain-biking trail. Remediation of hazardous materials ongoing since 2007. |

The Canol Road meets the **Robert Campbell Highway near Ross River**, so it connects into the same interior
network as Corridor 1.

## **The alignment nobody planned**

The Canol corridor runs from the Yukon to **Norman Wells** — which is **exactly where the Mackenzie Valley
Highway's funded Phase 1 terminates.**

Put them together and the Alaska Republic gets a fully internal route:

> **Whitehorse → Johnson's Crossing → South Canol (Hwy 6) → Ross River → North Canol → MacMillan Pass →
> [Canol Heritage Trail corridor] → Norman Wells → Mackenzie Valley Highway → Wrigley → Fort Simpson →
> Fort Providence → Yellowknife**

**No British Columbia. No Cascadia. Entirely national territory.**

And both halves already partly exist — one as an abandoned WWII roadbed with its bridges removed, the other as
a funded highway under construction. **The Republic would not be building a road across unknown country. It
would be reopening one and meeting a new one halfway.**

This is, by a wide margin, the strongest available answer to the transit problem, and it is far more
interesting than the Mackenzie Valley Highway's unfunded Phase 2 to Inuvik — because the Canol route is
*shorter*, *historically proven*, and runs through the middle of the country rather than looping to the Arctic
Ocean and back.

## The route, leg by leg

| Leg | Distance | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Whitehorse → **Johnsons Crossing** (Alaska Hwy) | ~125 km | Existing, paved |
| Johnsons Crossing → Ross River → **Macmillan Pass** (Canol Road, Yukon Hwy 6) | 449 km | **Existing** — gravel, "little changed from 1945," **summer-maintained only** |
| Macmillan Pass → **Norman Wells** — ***the Canol Trail Highway*** | ~355 km | **GAP.** Heritage trail only; **all bridges gone** |
| Norman Wells → Tulita → **Wrigley** — ***the Mackenzie River Highway*** | ~320 km | **GAP — but under construction.** MVH Phase 1, $405M committed, target 2032 |
| Wrigley → Fort Simpson → **Fort Providence** (NWT Hwy 1) | ~226 km | Existing |
| Fort Providence → Behchokǫ̀ → **Yellowknife** (NWT Hwy 3) | ~339 km | Existing |
| **TOTAL** | **≈1,814 km** | **675 km unbuilt (37%)** |

⚠️ Derived from published segment lengths, not measured end to end. Treat as ±5%.

## Three conclusions, and they are strong

### 1. The internal route is SHORTER than the Cascadian one

**≈1,814 km entirely inside the Republic, against ≈1,919 km through British Columbia.** The sovereign route is
**roughly 105 km shorter** than the foreign one.

That inverts the usual logic of such projects. This is not a patriotic detour built at a penalty — it is the
*better line*, and the only reason it is not the main road is that 37% of it has never been surfaced. The
Cascadian route wins on pavement, not on geography.

### 2. Only 355 km is genuinely missing

Of the 675 km gap, **320 km is the Mackenzie Valley Highway's Phase 1 — already funded, already under
construction, targeted at 2032.** That leaves **355 km** as the only piece nobody is building: the Canol
Heritage Trail segment from Macmillan Pass to Norman Wells.

**355 km is not a visionary megaproject. It is a road that already existed in 1944** and whose bridges have
since been removed. The graded corridor is still there; people hike and cycle it today.

### 3. This resolves the capital argument

Whitehorse was settled as capital partly because it is the hinge between the Republic's halves — and the
concern raised then was that the hinge is broken on the eastern side. **It is not broken. It is unsurfaced.**

And the corridor starts at **Johnsons Crossing, 125 km from the capital, on the Alaska Highway.** A Republic
seated at Whitehorse is sitting at the head of its own internal road east. That is a far better justification
for the capital than "the other two cities were bombed."

## What this does to the three resolutions

This substantially strengthens **Resolution B** and reshapes it. From
`Alaska Republic and Cascadia - The Transit Problem (three resolutions).md`:

- The Mackenzie Valley Highway's unfunded **Phase 2 to Inuvik is no longer the pivot.** That was always the
  weaker option — a dogleg to the Arctic Ocean and back.
- **The Canol is the pivot.** 355 km, historically proven, on a corridor that still exists.
- A post-war Republic rebuilding the Canol is doing something a wartime state already did **in about two
  years, in the 1940s, with 1940s equipment.**

**Strongest single framing:** the Mackenzie Valley Highway was completed before the war, as scheduled. The
Canol was not. So the Republic inherits a road that runs from Yellowknife to Norman Wells and simply stops —
pointing west at 355 km of mountain trail with the rotted stubs of wartime bridges in the creek beds. The
whole national question, visible in one place.

# If There Is No Road — Traversing the Land Anyway

The user asked whether the country can be crossed regardless. It can, three ways.

## 1. On foot

**Confirmed: "Some visitors hike from the Nahanni Range Road at Tungsten"** into Nahanni National Park
Reserve. So a walking connection from the road end into the river system is established practice, not
speculation. The **Canol Heritage Trail** is the same principle at scale — 355 km of abandoned roadbed that
people traverse on foot and by bicycle today, with every bridge gone.

For a low-technology society, a former road with no bridges is still a **cleared, graded corridor through
mountains** — which is most of the work. Fords and ferries replace bridges.

## 2. By water — downstream only

There is a continuous watercourse from the Tungsten area to Nahanni Butte:

> **Flat River → South Nahanni River → Liard River at Nahanni Butte**

Cantung sits in the **Flat River Valley**; the Flat joins the South Nahanni inside the park; the South Nahanni
runs ~540 km and **empties into the Liard at Nahanni Butte, 90 km north of Fort Liard.**

**The obstacles are severe and specific:**
- **Virginia Falls (Nailicho) — 90 m / 295 ft**, substantially higher than Niagara. **A mandatory portage.**
- **Four canyons** (First through Fourth) with walls hundreds of meters high.
- Serious whitewater throughout — this is a world-class rafting river, run by expedition parties.

**And it only works one way.** Downstream is a boat trip; upstream is not practical. A river corridor into the
east is not a river corridor out of it.

## 3. By air

Floatplane and helicopter, usually from Fort Simpson, with landing sites at Virginia Falls, Rabbitkettle Lake,
Glacier Lake and others. **Not available after a collapse** except to whoever still has aircraft and fuel.

# One More Gap, at the Destination

**Nahanni Butte itself is not road-connected.** An all-season road was completed in **October 2010 only as far
as the Liard River.** From there: **river taxi in summer, ice road in winter, and explicitly no plans for a
vehicle ferry.** The community also has an airport.

So even arriving from the NWT side, there is an unbridged river between the road and the town.

---

# Assessment

**For the fiction, in order of usefulness:**

1. **Reopen the Canol.** Historically proven, aligns with the Mackenzie Valley Highway's actual terminus at
   Norman Wells, runs through the center of the country, and leaves visible ruins to write about. The bridges
   are gone, which is a solvable problem and a good one — a nation's reunification measured in river
   crossings.
2. **Rebuild the Nahanni Range Road** as far as Tungsten, and stop there. Cheap, already crossing the border,
   but a dead end into a mountain park. Useful for reaching the tungsten, not for reaching Yellowknife.
3. **Push a road through Nahanni** from Tungsten to Nahanni Butte. Shortest on a map, worst on the ground:
   ~150–200 km through 1,000 m canyon walls and the highest mountains in the NWT, and — if the designation
   survives — through a World Heritage Site.

**The park designation is itself a worldbuilding decision.** Whether a 1978 UNESCO listing means anything in
2250 is entirely the author's call, and it is a good question: a republic that refuses to road the Nahanni on
principle is characterized by that refusal.
